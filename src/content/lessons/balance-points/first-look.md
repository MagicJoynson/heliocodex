Things in space do not usually stay put. But there are a few special places where they nearly can.

Think of the Sun and the Earth. In five spots near them, the pull of the Sun and the pull of the Earth work together in just the right way. A spacecraft put in one of those spots goes round the Sun in exactly one year, in step with the Earth, and so keeps its place beside us.

These spots are called Lagrange points, after the mathematician who worked them out.

They are very useful. The James Webb Space Telescope sits at one of them, 1.5 million km from the Earth, about four times as far as the Moon.

Nature uses them too. Thousands of asteroids are gathered at two of Jupiter's balance points, travelling round the Sun ahead of the planet and behind it.

**Why "nearly"?** There are two kinds of balance. A marble in the bottom of a bowl is balanced, and if you nudge it, it rolls back. A pencil stood on its point is balanced too, but the slightest nudge and it falls. Two of the five points are like the bowl. The other three, including the two that spacecraft use most, are like the pencil. A spacecraft there has to keep correcting itself with small puffs from its thrusters.

**Still moving.** A spacecraft at a balance point is not standing still. It is going round the Sun at 30 km a second, just as the Earth is. It only seems to stay put because it keeps pace with us exactly, like two runners side by side on a track.

**The view from the sunward point.** A spacecraft at the point between the Earth and the Sun never has its view of the Sun blocked, day or night. One of them, called SOHO, has watched the Sun without a break since 1995. Along the way it has found more than 5,000 comets, most of them on their way to fall into the Sun.

**The view from the far point.** The point on the other side of the Earth, away from the Sun, is where Webb lives. The Sun, the Earth and the Moon are all behind it in the same direction, so one sunshade keeps the heat and glare of all three off its mirror.

**Found on paper first.** Joseph-Louis Lagrange worked the points out in 1772 with nothing but mathematics. Nobody knew whether nature made use of them. Then in 1906 a German astronomer, Max Wolf, found an asteroid travelling round the Sun on Jupiter's own orbit, a sixth of a lap ahead of the planet, exactly where Lagrange had said something could stay. Thousands are now known at Jupiter's two steady points.

**The Moon has them too.** Every pair of bodies has its own set of five. In 2018 China placed a relay satellite near the balance point beyond the Moon. From there it could see both the Earth and the far side of the Moon, and it passed radio messages to and from the first lander on the far side, which could never have been reached directly.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Gravity cancels out at a balance point.* It does not. The pulls of the Sun and the Earth combine to give exactly the pull needed for a one-year orbit at that distance.
- *A spacecraft parks on the point.* It loops slowly round it, in a wide circuit that takes months.
- *There could be a hidden planet at the point behind the Sun.* That point is the pencil kind, so a planet could not stay there. And its pull on the other planets would have given it away long ago.

Things in space do not usually stay put. But there are a few special places where they nearly can.

Think of the Sun and the Earth. In five spots near them, the pull of the Sun and the pull of the Earth work together in just the right way. A spacecraft put in one of those spots goes round the Sun in exactly one year, in step with the Earth, and so keeps its place beside us.

  <figcaption><b>A diagram, not to scale.</b> The five balance points of the Sun and the Earth, seen from above. L1 and L2 are really a hundred times closer to the Earth than to the Sun. The Webb telescope is at L2.</figcaption>
</figure>

These spots are called Lagrange points, after the mathematician who worked them out.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/earth-moon-dscovr-2015.jpg" alt="The whole sunlit Earth, blue with white cloud, with the grey Moon passing in front of it." width="900" height="900" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> A view only a balance point can give. The DSCOVR spacecraft sits at L1, between the Earth and the Sun, 1.5 million km away, and so always sees the Earth fully lit. In July 2015 it caught the Moon crossing in front. The side of the Moon in view is the far side, which never faces the Earth. Credit: NASA/NOAA.</figcaption>
</figure>

They are very useful. The James Webb Space Telescope sits at one of them, 1.5 million km from the Earth, about four times as far as the Moon.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/webb-mirror-goddard-2017.jpg" alt="A huge honeycomb mirror of gold hexagons standing upright in a clean room, with two people in white suits on lifts in front of it." width="1100" height="1100" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The mirror of the James Webb Space Telescope before launch, 6.5 metres across. It now sits at L2, on the side of the Earth away from the Sun, where the Sun, Earth and Moon are all in the same part of its sky and one shield can shade it from all three. Credit: NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center.</figcaption>
</figure>

Nature uses them too. Thousands of asteroids are gathered at two of Jupiter's balance points, travelling round the Sun ahead of the planet and behind it.

**The puzzle they solve.** Kepler's third law says that the closer something is to the Sun, the faster it goes round. A spacecraft a little nearer the Sun than the Earth should pull ahead of us, and one a little further out should fall behind. So how can anything keep station?

The answer is that the Earth's own pull joins in. At the right spot, it adds to or takes away from the Sun's pull by just enough to make the spacecraft's year the same length as ours. Joseph-Louis Lagrange found the complete set of five such points in 1772. Every pair of orbiting bodies has them: Sun and Earth, Earth and Moon, Sun and Jupiter.

**The five points, for the Sun and Earth.**

| Point | Where | Steady? | What is there |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | 1.5 million km from the Earth, towards the Sun | No | Sun-watching spacecraft such as SOHO, with an unbroken view |
| L2 | 1.5 million km from the Earth, away from the Sun | No | Telescopes: Webb, and Euclid |
| L3 | On the far side of the Sun, opposite the Earth | No | Nothing |
| L4 | On the Earth's orbit, a sixth of a lap ahead | Yes | Two small asteroids found so far |
| L5 | On the Earth's orbit, a sixth of a lap behind | Yes | None found yet |

**Why L2 suits telescopes.** From L2 the Sun, Earth and Moon are all in the same part of the sky. A telescope there can hide from the heat and glare of all three behind one sunshade and look out at the rest of the universe in the cold and dark. Webb's mirror has to stay below about −220 °C to see faint infrared light, and that would be impossible near the Earth.

**Not steady, but cheap.** The first three points are like the top of a hill: a ball placed exactly on top stays, but the slightest nudge sends it rolling away. Spacecraft do not sit on them. They trace wide, slow loops around them, and fire small thrusters every few weeks to stay close. Webb uses only a little fuel a year this way and should last well over twenty years.

**Steady: the Trojans.** L4 and L5 are different. An object nudged away from either of them is steered back, and ends up circling the point slowly. Things that wander in can stay for billions of years. Jupiter has gathered many thousands of asteroids at its two, called Trojans. NASA's Lucy spacecraft is on its way to see them at close range and reaches the first group in 2027. Mars and Neptune have Trojans of their own, and so does the Earth, though only two small ones are known.

**A worked example: where is L1?** For a small body going round a much larger one, L1 and L2 lie at a distance from the small body equal to the size of its orbit times the cube root of its mass divided by three times the mass of the large one.

| Pair | Mass ratio | Distance of L1 and L2 from the smaller body (sums) |
|---|---|---|
| Sun and Earth | 1 to 333,000 | 1.5 million km |
| Sun and Jupiter | 1 to 1,047 | 53 million km |
| Earth and Moon | 1 to 81 | About 61,000 km from the Moon |

The first line is where the 1.5 million km in the table above comes from. The second shows why Jupiter can hold moons so far out: its most distant known moons are more than 20 million km from it.

**Why a sixth of a lap?** L4 and L5 each make a triangle with the Sun and the planet in which all three sides are the same length. That is true whatever the masses are. Whether the points are steady does depend on the masses: the larger body has to be at least 25 times heavier than the smaller. The Sun and any planet pass easily. The Earth and Moon pass, at 81 to 1. Pluto and its moon Charon, at about 8 to 1, do not.

**What an orbit round L4 looks like.** An asteroid at one of the steady points does not sit there. Seen from the planet, it drifts slowly along a stretched loop shaped like a tadpole, towards the planet and away again, taking many of the planet's years to go round once. Jupiter's Trojans are spread over a wide arc of its orbit for this reason. Some bodies go further and swing all the way round past L3 from one steady point to the other, in a horseshoe.

The Earth's two known Trojans are both at L4. The first, called 2010 TK7 and about 300 m across, was found in 2010 by a space telescope. The second, 2020 XL5, was found in 2020. They are very hard to spot from the ground because they are always in the twilight sky, close to the Sun.

**Where the Moon's maker may have come from.** The body that struck the young Earth and made the Moon had to arrive slowly, or the collision would have been too violent. One suggestion is that it grew at the Earth's own L4 or L5, sharing our orbit for millions of years until it was nudged out and drifted into us. It is an idea, not an established fact, but it would explain why the Moon's rock is so like the Earth's.

**Getting there is cheap as well.** The balance points are linked by paths along which a spacecraft can drift using almost no fuel, carried by the shifting pulls of the Sun, Earth and Moon. The journeys are slow, months where a direct flight would take days, but several spacecraft have used them to reach the Moon or to move from one balance point to another on a few kilograms of fuel.

**Lucy's route.** Lucy will make its first pass of a Trojan asteroid in August 2027, in the swarm ahead of Jupiter. It then swings back past the Earth and out again to the swarm behind, which it reaches in 2033. It is the first spacecraft to visit either.

**Common mistakes.**

- *There are only five such points in the Solar System.* There are five for every pair of bodies: five for the Sun and each planet, and five for each planet and each of its moons.
- *L2 is in the Earth's shadow.* Webb loops round L2 in a circuit hundreds of thousands of kilometres wide and stays in sunlight, which it needs for its solar panels.
- *Trojans follow Jupiter round like a tail.* They are 60 degrees ahead and behind, as far from Jupiter as Jupiter is from the Sun.

Some moons share an orbit in the same way. Saturn's moon Tethys has two tiny companions, Telesto and Calypso, one a sixth of a lap ahead and one behind.

**The limit of a planet's hold.** L1 and L2 also mark roughly how far a planet's gravity wins over the Sun's. Inside that distance something can stay in orbit round the planet. Outside, the Sun takes it away. For the Earth this reach is about 1.5 million km, and the Moon, at 384,400 km, is safely inside. Every planet's moons lie within its own such region, which is called its Hill sphere.

A black hole is not a thing made of some special material. It is a region of space where gravity has become so strong that every path leads inwards. All it takes is enough mass in a small enough space.

**How small is small enough.** Every mass has a size it would have to be crushed inside to become a black hole. The size grows in step with the mass: about 3 km of radius for each mass of the Sun.

| If you crushed | It would become a black hole at a width of |
|---|---|
| The Earth | About 18 mm, the size of a small coin |
| The Sun | About 6 km |
| A star of 10 Suns | About 60 km |
| The black hole at the centre of our galaxy (4.3 million Suns) | About 25 million km, 18 times the width of the Sun |

Nothing is going to crush the Earth or the Sun like this. Nature has only a few ways of doing it.

**From dying stars.** When a heavy star runs out of fuel, its core collapses. If what is left of the core has less than about 2 to 3 times the Sun's mass, it stops as a neutron star: a ball about 20 km across, held up by the pressure of particles packed as tightly as they can go. A teaspoonful would weigh more than a billion tonnes. If the core is heavier than that, no known force can hold it up, and it goes on falling in. The result is a black hole of a few Suns to a few tens of Suns in mass. Our galaxy probably holds tens of millions of them, perhaps many more. The nearest one known, found in 2022, is about 1,560 light years away.

**At the centres of galaxies.** Almost every large galaxy has a far heavier black hole at its centre, from hundreds of thousands to billions of times the mass of the Sun. The largest known reach tens of billions. How they grew so large, so early in the universe's history, is not yet understood.

**How to find something invisible.** Three ways, all of which have worked.

*By its pull on a neighbour.* Stars near the centre of our galaxy have been tracked for almost thirty years. One, called S2, goes round an empty-looking point every 16 years, reaching 7,650 km/s at its closest. Kepler's laws give the mass of what it orbits: 4.3 million Suns, in a space smaller than our Solar System. Only a black hole fits.

*By the glow of gas falling in.* Gas pulled from a neighbouring star cannot fall straight in. It spirals inwards in a flat accretion disc, and friction heats it to millions of °C, hot enough to shine in X-rays. The first black hole found this way, in the 1960s and 70s, was Cygnus X-1: an unseen object of about 21 Suns circling a blue giant star every 5.6 days.

*By the ripples when two collide.* On 14 September 2015 two detectors in the United States, 3,000 km apart, each felt their 4 km arms change length by far less than the width of a proton. The cause was a gravitational wave from two black holes, of about 36 and 29 Suns, that had merged 1.3 billion light years away. The single hole they made had about 62 Suns of mass. The missing 3 Suns' worth had been turned into energy and carried off as ripples in space, in a fifth of a second. Hundreds of mergers have been recorded since.

**What they are not.** A black hole's gravity far away is no stronger than that of any other object with the same mass. Planets can orbit one safely. Only close in, within a few times its own width, does anything unusual happen. That is the subject of the next lesson.

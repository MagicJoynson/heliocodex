Saturn's rings look solid, like a hat brim. They are not.

They are made of billions of separate pieces of ice. Some are as small as grains of dust. Some are as big as a house. Every piece goes round Saturn on its own, like a tiny moon.

The rings are enormously wide. If you laid them between the Earth and the Moon they would fill most of the gap.

But they are also amazingly thin: in most places only about ten metres from top to bottom. For their width, they are as thin as a sheet of paper three kilometres across.

Saturn is not the only planet with rings. Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune have them too. Theirs are dark and faint, and were only found in the last fifty years.

**Galileo's puzzle.** Galileo was the first to see the rings, in 1610, but his telescope was too weak to show what they were. He saw a blob on each side of Saturn and wrote that the planet had "ears". Two years later the ears vanished, and he did not know what to say. The rings had turned edge-on to the Earth, and they are far too thin to see that way. It was 1655 before a Dutch astronomer, Christiaan Huygens, with a better telescope, worked out that Saturn is surrounded by a thin, flat ring that touches it nowhere.

**See them yourself.** Saturn's rings are one of the few wonders of the sky that a small telescope really shows. Magnifying 30 times is enough to see that the planet has a ring round it. Binoculars are not quite enough: through them Saturn looks slightly oval.

**How much is there?** Much less than it seems. Sweep up every piece of the rings and press them into one ball of ice, and the ball would be about 320 km across (a sum from the mass that the Cassini spacecraft measured). That is smaller than Saturn's little moon Mimas. The rings look grand because the ice is spread so thinly over so wide an area.

**Round and round.** The pieces near the inner edge of the bright rings go round Saturn in about 8 hours. Those at the outer edge take about 14 (sums). So the inner pieces are always overtaking the outer ones. Neighbouring pieces are travelling at almost the same speed, though, so when they touch it is a gentle nudge, at about the pace of a snail.

**Why they are so bright.** The pieces are almost pure water ice, and they shine in sunlight like fresh snow. The rings of the other three giant planets are made of dark, dusty stuff, more like soot, which is why nobody saw them for so long.

**Rings in unexpected places.** In 2013 astronomers found two thin rings round Chariklo, a lump of rock and ice only about 250 km across that orbits between Saturn and Uranus. Rings have since been found round the distant dwarf planets Haumea and Quaoar. A ring does not need a giant planet.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The rings are solid, like a disc.* They are countless separate pieces, with gaps between them. Stars can be seen shining through.
- *The rings are as thick as they are wide.* They are thinner, for their width, than anything you have ever handled.
- *A spacecraft could not get through.* The Cassini spacecraft dived through the gap between the rings and the planet 22 times in 2017, and came to no harm.

Saturn's rings look solid, like a hat brim. They are not.

</figure>

They are made of billions of separate pieces of ice. Some are as small as grains of dust. Some are as big as a house. Every piece goes round Saturn on its own, like a tiny moon.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/saturn-ring-wave-cassini.jpg" alt="Dozens of fine parallel bright and dark stripes running diagonally, close together at one side and wider apart at the other." width="900" height="900" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> A small part of the rings from close up, by Cassini on 4 June 2017. The picture is about 500 km across. The stripes are a ripple set going by the tug of a moon called Janus. Each dot of the picture is half a kilometre wide, so even here not one piece of ice shows on its own. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/Space Science Institute.</figcaption>
</figure>

The rings are enormously wide. If you laid them between the Earth and the Moon they would fill most of the gap.

But they are also amazingly thin: in most places only about ten metres from top to bottom. For their width, they are as thin as a sheet of paper three kilometres across.

Saturn is not the only planet with rings. Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune have them too. Theirs are dark and faint, and were only found in the last fifty years.

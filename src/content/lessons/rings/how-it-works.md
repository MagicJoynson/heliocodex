**Why they are not solid.** In 1859 James Clerk Maxwell proved on paper that a solid ring round a planet would be torn apart: its inner edge would need to go round faster than its outer edge. A ring has to be a crowd of separate bodies, each on its own orbit, the inner ones lapping the outer ones. Spacecraft later showed he was right.

**Why they are flat.** A piece on a tilted orbit crosses the ring twice each lap and collides with others as it does. Every collision takes away some of the up and down motion. After enough of them everything has settled into one thin sheet over the planet's equator. Saturn's main rings are about 280,000 km across and mostly about 10 m thick.

**Why they do not gather into a moon.** Close to a planet, tides are strong: the planet pulls harder on the near side of anything than on its far side. Inside a certain distance, called the Roche limit, that stretching beats the gravity that would hold a loose pile of rubble together. For Saturn the limit lies between two and two and a half times the planet's radius from its centre, depending on what the rubble is made of. Rings live inside it, and large moons outside.

**Gaps and edges.** The rings are full of structure, and moons cause most of it.

- A moon outside the rings can clear a gap where ring pieces would orbit in step with it. The Cassini Division, the wide dark band you can see in a small telescope, is where a piece would go round exactly twice for each orbit of the moon Mimas.
- Small moons orbiting beside a narrow ring keep its edges sharp. They are called shepherd moons. Saturn's thin, twisted F ring runs between two of them, Prometheus and Pandora.
- A few tiny moons orbit inside gaps in the rings and sweep them clear.

**The four ring systems.**

| Planet | Found | What they are like |
|---|---|---|
| Saturn | 1610 by Galileo, understood as a ring by Huygens in 1655 | Broad and bright, almost pure water ice |
| Uranus | 1977, when a star flickered before and after passing behind the planet | Thirteen narrow, dark rings |
| Jupiter | 1979, by Voyager 1 | Faint dust knocked off small inner moons |
| Neptune | Confirmed in 1989 by Voyager 2 | Dark and dusty, with the outer ring gathered into clumps |

**How old are Saturn's rings?** This is still argued over. In 2017, at the end of its mission, the Cassini spacecraft flew between the rings and the planet and weighed them. They came out light, with less than half the mass of the small moon Mimas, and remarkably clean. Dust from space should have darkened them if they were old, so many researchers concluded they formed no more than a few hundred million years ago, perhaps from a moon or comet that strayed too close. Others reply that the rings may have ways of staying clean and could be as old as Saturn. Cassini also found ring material steadily falling into the planet, so the rings may not be permanent.

**Maxwell's proof, tested.** Maxwell's argument was mathematics. The test came in 1895, when James Keeler in the United States spread the light of the rings into a spectrum. The lines were tilted. The inner edge of the rings was moving faster than the outer edge, by just the amount that separate bodies in orbit would show. A solid disc would have done the opposite, with its outer edge moving fastest, like the rim of a wheel.

**A worked example: the Roche limit.** For a loose pile held together only by its own gravity, the limit lies at about 2.44 planet radii, multiplied by the cube root of the planet's density divided by the pile's density. Saturn's average density is 0.69 of water's and ice is 0.92. The cube root of 0.69 ÷ 0.92 is 0.91, so the limit is 2.2 times Saturn's radius, or about 133,000 km from its centre (a sum). The outer edge of the bright rings is at 136,800 km. The rings end almost exactly where a loose pile of ice could first hold together as a moon.

The same sum for the Earth and a body like the Moon gives about 18,000 km from the Earth's centre. The Moon is at 384,400 km and in no danger. Mars's inner moon Phobos is slowly spiralling inwards, and in a few tens of millions of years it will reach the limit for Mars and may be pulled apart into a ring.

**How the rings of Uranus were found.** On 10 March 1977 astronomers flying in an aircraft fitted with a telescope watched Uranus pass in front of a star, hoping to measure the planet's air. Before the planet reached the star, the star winked out briefly, five times. After the planet had passed, it winked five times more, in the reverse order. Something thin and dark lay on both sides of Uranus at matching distances. Nobody had seen the rings themselves. The same method found the rings of Chariklo, Haumea and Quaoar.

**The rings as an instrument.** Saturn itself is not perfectly still inside. The planet quivers, and its changing pull raises faint spiral ripples in the rings, in the way that a small earthquake shakes a seismograph. By reading the ripples in Cassini's pictures, researchers have worked out how fast the deep interior of Saturn turns and that its core is large and blurred, without sharp edges. The clouds, which move at different speeds at different latitudes, could never show this.

**Rain from the rings.** Tiny charged grains of ice are pulled out of the rings along Saturn's magnetic field and fall into the planet. From the measured rate, a NASA study in 2018 estimated that the rings could be gone in about 300 million years. Taken with their low mass and clean ice, that suggests we are living at a lucky time to see them, in the middle of a show that has not always been on and will not last.

**A ring that should not exist.** The ring of Quaoar, announced in 2023, lies at more than seven times that body's radius, far outside its Roche limit. By the reasoning above it ought to have gathered into a small moon within a few decades. It has not. It may be that the icy pieces bounce off each other too briskly to stick. The puzzle is not settled, and it shows that the Roche limit is a guide and not a law.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The gaps in the rings are empty.* Most have some material in them. The Cassini Division only looks dark next to the bright rings on either side.
- *Rings are made of bits that never managed to become a moon.* For Saturn that may be backwards. The rings may be what is left of a moon or comet that was broken up.
- *All the giant planets' rings are alike.* Saturn's are bright ice, Jupiter's are thin dust, and those of Uranus and Neptune are narrow and dark.

**Seen edge on.** Saturn is tilted, so we see the rings from different angles as it goes round the Sun. About every 14 years the Earth passes through their plane and they all but vanish, because there is nothing to see of a sheet ten metres thick from more than a billion kilometres away. It last happened in March 2025.

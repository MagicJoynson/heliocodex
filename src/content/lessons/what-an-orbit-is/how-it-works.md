The speed for Newton's cannon can be worked out from two facts.

Anything dropped near the ground falls about 5 metres in its first second. And the Earth's surface curves away from a level line by about 5 metres for every 8 km you travel. Put them together: if the ball covers 8 km in the second it takes to fall 5 metres, the ground has dropped away by the same 5 metres, and the ball is no nearer to it than before. So the speed needed is about 8 km every second. The exact figure at the surface is 7.9 km/s.

**Real orbits.** The International Space Station flies about 400 km up, at 7.7 km/s, and goes round the Earth in 93 minutes. Higher orbits are slower, because gravity is weaker further out and less sideways speed is needed to keep missing. A satellite 35,786 km up moves at 3.1 km/s and takes one whole turn of the Earth to go round, so it hangs over one spot on the equator. That is where television and weather satellites sit. The Moon, 384,400 km away, moves at just 1 km/s and takes 27.3 days.

**Floating is falling.** People on the Space Station are not beyond gravity. At that height it is still nearly 90% as strong as on the ground. They float because they, the station and everything in it are all falling together, so nothing presses on anything else.

**Too slow, too fast.** Below the circular speed, the path dips towards the ground on the far side and, close to the Earth, runs into the air. A little above it, the orbit stretches into an oval that swings out further on the far side. At 1.41 times the circular speed the oval never closes, and the ball leaves for good. From the Earth's surface that escape speed is 11.2 km/s.

**How Newton checked it with the Moon.** Newton tested the idea on the only thing he could: the Moon. The Moon is 384,400 km away, about 60 times as far from the centre of the Earth as the ground is. If gravity weakens with the square of the distance, it should be 60 × 60, or about 3,600 times, weaker out there. On the ground a dropped stone falls about 4.9 metres in its first second. Divide by 3,600 and the Moon should fall about 1.35 millimetres in a second.

It does. The Moon moves at 1.022 km/s, and for a path that fast to bend into a circle that size it has to curve inwards by 1.35 millimetres every second. The pull that brings down an apple, thinned out by distance, is exactly what is needed to keep the Moon going round. Both sums are made from the figures in NASA's fact sheets.

**A table of orbits.** The same sum works at any height. These speeds and times are worked out from the measured strength of the Earth's gravity, for circular orbits.

| Height above the ground | Speed | Time for one lap |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 km | 7.4 km/s | 1 hour 45 minutes |
| 10,000 km | 4.9 km/s | 5 hours 47 minutes |
| 35,786 km | 3.1 km/s | 23 hours 56 minutes |
| 384,400 km (the Moon) | 1.0 km/s | 27.3 days |

The third line is 23 hours 56 minutes and not 24 hours because that is how long the Earth takes to turn once against the stars. The extra four minutes of an ordinary day come from the Earth's own movement round the Sun.

**The rule behind the table.** Johannes Kepler found the pattern in 1619, long before anyone could explain it: the square of the time for a lap goes up in step with the cube of the size of the orbit. Make an orbit four times as wide and each lap takes eight times as long. Newton later showed that this is what gravity weakening with the square of the distance must produce.

**Three common mistakes.**

- *A satellite needs its engine running to stay up.* It does not. Above the air there is nothing to slow it, and a thing that is not slowed keeps going. The Moon has circled for about 4.5 billion years with no engine at all. Engines are for getting into orbit and for changing it. The lowest satellites are a small exception, because a trace of air reaches even 400 km. The International Space Station sinks by about 2 km a month and is pushed back up from time to time.
- *Getting to space is about going up.* Height is the easy part. Lifting a kilogram 400 km takes about an eighth of the energy needed to get it moving sideways at 7.7 km/s (a sum from the figures above). That is why a rocket starts to lean over soon after it leaves the pad. Nearly all of its fuel is spent on going sideways.
- *There is no gravity in orbit.* If that were true, a satellite would fly off in a straight line. Gravity is the only thing bending its path.

**Slow down to catch up.** In orbit, steering by common sense fails. In June 1965 the astronaut James McDivitt, flying Gemini 4, tried to fly up to the used rocket stage that had put him in orbit. He pointed at it and fired his thrusters, and the stage drew further away. Thrusting forwards raised his orbit, and a higher orbit is a slower one, so he fell behind. To catch something ahead of you in orbit you have to brake. Braking drops you into a lower, quicker orbit that gains on the target from below. Engineers worked out the method later that year, and every docking since has used it.

**The Earth is in orbit too.** The Earth goes round the Sun at an average of 29.78 km/s, which is about 2.6 million km a day and 940 million km a year. Its orbit is a slightly stretched circle, so the speed changes: 30.29 km/s in early January, when the Earth is closest to the Sun, and 29.29 km/s in early July, when it is furthest. The Sun is in orbit as well. It carries the whole Solar System round the centre of the galaxy at about 230 km/s, and one lap takes about 225 million years. Since the Sun formed it has been round about 20 times.

Not one of these numbers depends on what is being thrown. A grain of dust and a space station at the same height keep the same orbit at the same speed.

On a dark night, look towards the constellation Sagittarius and you are looking at the middle of our galaxy. This is a real photograph of that part of the sky. The glow is the light of millions of far-off stars. The black lanes across it are clouds of dust lying in the way, and they hide the centre completely.

Dust blocks ordinary light, but it lets a good deal of infrared through. This is the same direction photographed in infrared from a mountain in Chile. The dark lanes have mostly gone and the crowded heart of the galaxy shows through, about 27,000 light years away.

Go right in to the very middle and the stars are packed together as nowhere near us. This picture is only about three light years from side to side. Round the Sun, a space that size holds no other star at all. Here there is a whole crowd of them.

Astronomers watched these stars year after year and found that they move. One of them, called S2, goes round an empty-looking point once every 16 years. At its closest, in May 2018, it was moving at more than 25 million kilometres an hour, which is nearly three hundredths of the speed of light.

Only something very heavy could swing a star round that fast. From S2's orbit, the thing at the centre weighs about four million times as much as the Sun, and yet it gives off almost no light. It is a black hole. In 2022, eight radio observatories working together as one telescope the size of the Earth showed it: a ring of glowing gas round a dark middle.

Everything on this site began with someone going outside and looking up. You can do the same tonight, and you need nothing but your eyes. The first thing to give them is time. Step out of a lit room and you see a handful of stars. Wait a quarter of an hour and there are many more. After about thirty minutes your eyes are as sharp as they will get. One glance at a phone screen undoes it. If you need a light, use a red one, which does far less harm.

The second thing is darkness. Street lamps and buildings throw light up into the air, and the glow drowns the fainter stars. This picture from space shows how much of it there is. From the middle of a big city only the Moon, the planets and the brightest stars get through. Even a short trip out of town helps, and so does a night with no Moon.

Under a properly dark sky you will see something most people in towns never do: a pale, ragged band of light from one horizon to the other. That is the Milky Way, our own galaxy seen from the inside. The photograph here shows the whole of it. By eye it has no colour, only a soft grey glow.

Start with the easy things. The Moon is best when it is not full, because the shadows along its edge of night pick out the craters. A bright light that does not twinkle is a planet: Venus, Jupiter, Mars and Saturn are all easy. From Britain the Plough is up on every clear night. The Pleiades are a tight little cluster in which most people can count six or seven stars. On a dark autumn night a faint smudge in Andromeda is another galaxy, two and a half million light years away.

When you want more, try binoculars before a telescope. They are easy to point, and they turn the Milky Way into clouds of separate stars. And before you go out, look at the sky map on this site. It shows what is over your own roof tonight.

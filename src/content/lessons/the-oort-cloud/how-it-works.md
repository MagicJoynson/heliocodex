The Oort cloud is an idea that fits the evidence, not a place anyone has photographed. Everything below is worked out from the comets that arrive from it and from calculations of how the young Solar System behaved. The figures are wide ranges because that is honestly how well they are known.

**How far.** Distances this large are measured in astronomical units (au). One au is the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 150 million km.

| Place | Distance from the Sun | Time for sunlight to get there |
|---|---|---|
| Earth | 1 au | A little over 8 minutes |
| Neptune | About 30 au | About 4.5 hours |
| Heliopause, where the Sun's wind ends | Beyond the Kuiper belt | About 17 hours |
| Inner edge of the Oort cloud | 2,000 to 5,000 au | 10 to 28 days |
| Outer edge of the Oort cloud | 10,000 to 100,000 au | Perhaps a year and a half |

If the outer edge is at 100,000 au, that is about 1.6 light years, more than a third of the way to the nearest star. Voyager 1, the most distant spacecraft, covers about a million miles (1.6 million km) a day. At that speed it will not reach the inner edge for about 300 years, and may need 30,000 years to come out of the far side.

**Why a cloud and not a belt.** The planets, the asteroid belt and most of the Kuiper belt go round the Sun the same way in roughly the same flat plane. Oort cloud objects are not thought to. Their orbits can be tilted at any angle, passing over, under and around the Sun, so together they fill a thick round shell.

**How it got there.** The leading idea is that these bodies did not form where they are now. When the planets formed 4.6 billion years ago, a great many leftover lumps called planetesimals remained among them. The gravity of the planets, Jupiter most of all, flung these lumps outwards. Some were thrown out of the Solar System for good. Others ended up on long, stretched orbits, still held by the Sun but so far out that the gravity of the rest of the galaxy tugged on them too. That steady pull from the galaxy is thought to have lifted their orbits clear of the planets, where they have stayed ever since. The cloud may also have picked up some bodies that formed round other stars.

**How many.** There may be hundreds of billions of icy bodies in the cloud, perhaps trillions. Spread through so much space, they are still very far apart.

**The comets it sends us.** Now and then something disturbs one of these bodies and it starts a long fall towards the Sun. It arrives as a long-period comet. Two recent visitors:

| Comet | What happened |
|---|---|
| C/2012 S1 (ISON) | Passed too close to the Sun and broke apart |
| C/2013 A1 (Siding Spring) | Passed very close to Mars in 2014 and survived. It will not be back for about 740,000 years |

Most long-period comets have been seen only once in recorded history. Some have orbits so long that our species did not exist the last time they came by.

**What would settle it.** No spacecraft will reach the cloud in any of our lifetimes, and its members are too faint to see where they are. For now the evidence stays indirect: the paths of the comets, traced backwards, and the calculations that explain how they got out there.

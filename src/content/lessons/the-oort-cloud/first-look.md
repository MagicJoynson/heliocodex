Every so often a comet turns up that nobody was expecting. It falls in from the dark, swings once round the Sun and heads back out, not to return for hundreds of thousands of years.

These comets come from every direction: from above the planets, from below them and from the sides. So wherever they start, it cannot be a flat ring like the planets' orbits. It has to be all around us.

Astronomers think the Sun is wrapped in a huge, round shell of icy lumps, like the skin of a ball with the planets as a speck in the middle. It is called the Oort cloud, after the Dutch astronomer Jan Oort, who worked this out.

It is a very long way off. Sunlight reaches the Earth in about eight minutes. It takes between 10 and 28 days to reach the inside of the Oort cloud, and perhaps a year and a half to come out of the far side.

Nobody has ever seen the cloud itself. Each lump is small, dark and much too far away for any telescope. We know it only from the comets that fall out of it, which is why the shell you see here is marked as a model.

**Why water.** Water dissolves the things a cell feeds on and lets the chemistry inside a cell go on. NASA's list of what to look for on another world starts with it for that reason.

**Why those six elements.** Carbon atoms join to each other in chains and rings, and to the other five, in an endless variety of shapes. All six are made in stars and scattered through space when stars die. The lesson on [where the elements come from](/learn/where-the-elements-come-from) tells that story.

**Energy without the Sun.** Plants take their energy from light. The microbes at a sea-floor vent take theirs from chemicals in the hot water instead, and the larger animals there live on the microbes. This matters a great deal for the search, because the oceans of moons such as Europa and Enceladus lie under kilometres of ice, where no sunlight reaches.

**Lovers of extremes.** Living things that need conditions which would kill most others are called extremophiles.

| Where | What lives there |
|---|---|
| Hot springs such as Yellowstone's | Microbes that thrive above 80 °C, and others in strong acid |
| Sea-floor vents | Whole communities with no sunlight; one microbe grows at 122 °C |
| The dry valleys of Antarctica | Microbes, in a frozen desert |
| Open space, for 10 days | Water bears, which came home alive |

**How hot is the vent water?** NOAA gives "over 700 degrees Fahrenheit", which is about 370 °C (a sum). It does not boil because of the pressure of the deep sea.

**A worked example: the pressure at a vent.** Each 10 metres of sea water adds about as much pressure as the whole of the Earth's air. A vent 2,500 metres down is therefore under about 250 times the pressure at the surface (a sum). Under that weight water stays liquid at temperatures far above 100 °C, and the microbes round the vent live in water that would be steam at the surface.

**What is special about water.** Other liquids exist in the Solar System. Titan has lakes of liquid methane at about −180 °C. Water has three advantages over them. It stays liquid over a wide range of temperatures. It dissolves more kinds of substance than any other common liquid. And its ice floats, which is unusual: most solids sink in their own liquid. Because ice floats, a sea freezes from the top down and the lid of ice keeps the water below from freezing. That is exactly the arrangement on Europa and Enceladus.

**Why carbon and not silicon.** Silicon is often suggested as a stand-in for carbon, because it can also make four bonds. But long chains of silicon fall apart in water, and when silicon joins oxygen it makes solid rock. Carbon joined to oxygen makes a gas that dissolves and moves about. The Earth has far more silicon than carbon, and life here still chose carbon.

**The ingredients are common.** In 1952 Stanley Miller, a student in Chicago, passed electric sparks through a flask of simple gases and water for a week. The water turned brown, and in it were amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. Nobody had put them there. Since then the same substances have been found ready-made in space. A meteorite that fell at Murchison in Australia on 28 September 1969 contained dozens of kinds of amino acid. The pieces of the asteroid Bennu brought back by NASA in 2023 held 14 of the 20 amino acids that life on the Earth uses, and all five of the bases that carry the code in DNA and RNA.

None of that is life. It shows that the parts are made easily and are probably lying about on every young planet. The step from the parts to a living cell is the one that nobody has yet explained or repeated.

**Life began early here.** The Earth is 4.54 billion years old. The oldest undisputed traces of life are layered mounds built by microbes at least 3.5 billion years ago, and there are disputed chemical signs from earlier still. For most of the time since, all life was single cells. Animals large enough to see came in only the last 600 million years or so.

**More limits.**

| Condition | The record |
|---|---|
| Radiation | A microbe called Deinococcus radiodurans shrugs off a dose a thousand times greater than would kill a person |
| Acid | Microbes that grow in liquid as strong as battery acid |
| Cold | Microbes still active in salty water at about −20 °C |
| Depth | Microbes in rock several kilometres below the ground |

Every one of these still needs liquid water. Nothing has been found that lives without it. Some things, like the water bear, survive drying out by shutting down, but they do nothing until water returns.

**How life might be spotted from far away.** Nobody can visit a planet round another star, but its air can be read from its light. A living planet changes its air. The Earth's air is one fifth oxygen only because plants and microbes keep making it. Without them it would be gone within a few million years. Oxygen and methane found together would be a strong hint, since the two destroy each other and something must be making both.

In 2025 a team reported possible signs of a gas called dimethyl sulphide in the air of a planet named K2-18b. On the Earth that gas is made only by living things. Other astronomers looked at the same data and were not persuaded that the gas is there at all. A claim like this needs several independent checks before it means anything.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Life needs sunlight.* Life needs energy. Sunlight is one source and chemical energy is another.
- *Life needs oxygen.* The Earth had almost none in its air for about the first two billion years, and life did well without it. Oxygen in the air is something life made.
- *Where there is water there is life.* Water is necessary as far as anyone knows. Nobody knows whether it is enough.

**A caution.** All of this is life as we know it, drawn from one example. Life elsewhere might use something else. But water, those elements and energy are things that can be searched for now, so that is where the search begins.

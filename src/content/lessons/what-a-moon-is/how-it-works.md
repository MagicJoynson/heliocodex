**Two families.** Look at where a giant planet's moons are and they fall into two groups that could hardly be more different.

**Regular moons** sit close to the planet, on nearly circular orbits, level with its equator, all going round the same way the planet spins. They formed where they are, from a disc of gas and dust around the young planet, much as the planets formed around the Sun. Jupiter's four big moons and Saturn's Titan are regular moons.

**Irregular moons** are far out, on stretched and steeply tilted orbits. Many go round backwards. They are small, and there are a great many of them: they make up more than four in every five of the moons known. They are thought to be passing bodies that the planet caught early on.

| Planet | Moons known | Of which far-out and irregular |
|---|---|---|
| Earth | 1 | 0 |
| Mars | 2 | 0 |
| Jupiter | 115 | 107 |
| Saturn | 292 | 267 |
| Uranus | 29 | 10 |
| Neptune | 16 | 8 |
| Pluto | 5 | 0 |

The counts are from NASA/JPL's list in November 2026. Triton is counted with the regular moons here because it is close in, but see below.

**The odd ones out.** A few moons fit neither pattern, and each one records an accident.

Our own Moon is far too big for the Earth to have grown it from a disc. The best explanation is that a body about the size of Mars struck the young Earth some 4.5 billion years ago, and the Moon gathered from the wreckage.

Triton, Neptune's one large moon, goes round backwards. No moon can form that way, so it must have been caught. It looks very like Pluto and probably came from the same region.

Mars's two moons are tiny. Phobos is so close in that it creeps 1.8 metres nearer to Mars every century. In about 50 million years it will either hit the planet or be pulled apart into a ring.

**How far a planet can reach.** A planet can only hold a moon inside the region where its own pull beats the Sun's. Astronomers call it the Hill sphere. Saturn's reaches about 65 million km, and its outermost moons range as far as 40 million km from it. Mercury and Venus are so close to the Sun that their regions are small, and nothing has stayed.

**Why the giants have so many.** The size of that region depends on the planet's mass and its distance from the Sun. Worked out from NASA's masses and distances, the Earth's reaches about 1.5 million km, four times as far as the Moon. Jupiter's reaches about 53 million km and Saturn's about 65 million. The giants swept a far larger volume of space, at a time when there was far more loose material about, and kept some of what came by.

**A moon compared with its planet.** What makes a moon matter to its planet is not its size but its size compared with the planet.

| Moon | Planet | Moon's width as a share of the planet's |
|---|---|---|
| Ganymede | Jupiter | 3.8 per cent |
| Titan | Saturn | 4.4 per cent |
| The Moon | The Earth | 27 per cent |
| Charon | Pluto | 51 per cent |

The shares are sums from NASA's widths. A moon and its planet really circle a balance point between them. For the Earth and Moon that point is 4,670 km from the Earth's centre, which is still inside the Earth, so it is fair to say the Moon goes round us. For Pluto and Charon the balance point lies in open space between them. Each circles it, and some astronomers call the pair a double world.

**Bigger than a planet, but lighter.** Ganymede is 5,268 km across and Titan 5,150 km. Mercury is only 4,879 km. Yet Mercury has more than twice the mass of Ganymede. Mercury is rock and iron, and the two big moons are about half ice.

**How new moons are found.** The moons still being found are a few kilometres across and hundreds of millions of kilometres away, far too faint to show in one picture. Astronomers take dozens of long exposures of the sky round a planet over several hours. A computer then slides the pictures over one another at every speed and direction a moon might be moving, and adds them up. If a moon is there, its light piles up into a dot. The candidate has to be followed for a year or more before its orbit is certain and it is added to the list. In March 2025 a single announcement added 128 moons to Saturn's count.

**Too close to be a moon.** There is a distance inside which a planet's tides are stronger than the gravity holding a loose moon together. Anything of that kind straying inside is pulled apart. Saturn's bright rings lie inside that distance, which is why the ice there has never gathered into a moon. Phobos is heading the same way at Mars.

**Moons that keep rings tidy.** Small moons orbiting beside a ring herd its particles with their gravity and keep its edge sharp. Saturn's narrow outer ring is held between two of these, Prometheus and Pandora. Astronomers call them shepherd moons.

**Catching a moon is hard.** A passing body arrives too fast to stay. A planet's gravity speeds it up as it comes in and it leaves with all the speed it came with. To be caught it has to lose some: by ploughing through gas that surrounded the young planet, by striking something, or by arriving as a pair and leaving its partner behind. The last is the favoured story for Triton.

**What a big moon does.** The Moon raises the Earth's tides, and it is slowly lengthening the day. Computer models suggest it also steadies the Earth's tilt. Without it the lean of the axis could wander widely over millions of years, and the seasons with it. Mars, with two tiny moons, has had no such help.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A moon is always smaller than a planet.* Two moons are wider than Mercury, as the figures above show.
- *Moons are round.* Only about twenty are. The rest are too small for their own gravity to pull them into a ball, and look like potatoes.
- *A moon could have a moon.* None is known. Close to a moon, the planet's pull would in time drag a small companion away. But spacecraft have been put in orbit round our Moon and stayed there for years.

**One face inward.** Nearly every regular moon keeps the same face towards its planet, as ours does. The lesson on tides explains why.

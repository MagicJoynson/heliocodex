A moon is a natural object that orbits a planet. The planet orbits the Sun, and the moon goes along with it, circling as they travel.

Earth has one, and we call it the Moon. Mars has two small ones. Mercury and Venus have none at all.

The giant planets have most of them. In November 2026 the count stood at 115 for Jupiter, 292 for Saturn, 29 for Uranus and 16 for Neptune. Even little Pluto has five. The numbers keep going up, because the ones still being found are small, dark and very far away.

Moons are not all alike. Ganymede and Titan are bigger than the planet Mercury. Most of the others are lumps of rock and ice only a few kilometres across, too small for their own gravity to pull them round.

**How big is ours?** The Moon is 3,475 km across, a little over a quarter of the width of the Earth. Four moons in the Solar System are bigger, but all of them belong to giant planets. For the size of its planet, ours is unusually large.

**The first moons anyone found.** For most of history there was one moon, ours. In January 1610 Galileo turned a small telescope on Jupiter and saw four points of light beside it. Night by night they changed places, always staying close to the planet. They were going round it. This mattered far beyond Jupiter. Nearly everyone then believed that everything in the sky went round the Earth, and here were four things that plainly did not.

**Worlds of their own.** The big moons are as varied as the planets. Io is covered in volcanoes. Europa has an ocean under its ice. Titan has thick air, clouds and rain, though the rain is liquid methane. Enceladus sprays water into space. Until spacecraft went to look, nobody guessed that moons could be like this.

**Not only planets have them.** Pluto is not a planet and has five moons. Even an asteroid can have one. In 1993 the Galileo spacecraft flew past an asteroid called Ida, and in its pictures, found a few months later, was a moon just over a kilometre across. It was named Dactyl.

**How small can a moon be?** There is no rule. Nobody has agreed a smallest size. Saturn's rings are made of countless bits of ice, each going round the planet on its own path, and nobody counts those. The moons on the lists are simply the ones big enough to have been spotted and followed.

**Where the names come from.** Moons are named by agreement among astronomers, and each planet has a theme. Jupiter's are named after people in the stories of the Greek god Zeus. Most of the moons of Uranus are named after characters in the plays of Shakespeare.

**A common mistake.** People often say the Moon does not spin, because we always see the same face. It does spin: exactly once for each trip round the Earth. Try walking round a chair while always facing it. By the time you are back where you began, you have turned to face every wall of the room.

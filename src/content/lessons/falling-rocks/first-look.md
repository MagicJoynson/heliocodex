A shooting star is not a star. It is a tiny piece of rock or dust, often no bigger than a grain of sand, hitting the Earth's air at enormous speed.

It is moving so fast that the air in front of it glows white hot. The grain burns away in a second or two, high above the ground. All you see is the streak.

Three words are easy to mix up. Out in space, the piece is a meteoroid. The streak of light is a meteor. If any of it lands, that piece is a meteorite.

On certain nights of the year there are many more meteors than usual. Those are meteor showers. They happen when the Earth passes through the dust left behind by a comet.

**How to watch a shower.** No telescope is needed, and one would get in the way, because the streaks can appear anywhere in the sky. Go somewhere dark, away from street lamps. Lie back, look up and give your eyes twenty minutes to get used to the dark. On a good night of the Perseids in August, a patient watcher under a dark sky can count 50 to 100 an hour.

**Why after midnight is best.** The Earth ploughs through the dust as it goes round the Sun. After midnight your side of the Earth is facing forwards, into the dust, like the front windscreen of a car in the rain. Before midnight you are looking out of the back window, and far fewer grains catch up.

**How high are they?** A meteor that seems to come down just behind the next hill is really burning up 80 to 100 km above the ground. It only looks close because it is so bright.

**How fast?** The grains of the Perseid shower arrive at 59 km a second. That is London to Edinburgh in about nine seconds. At that speed a grain carries about 400 times as much energy as the same weight of high explosive (a sum), which is why something so small can be seen from so far away.

**Fireballs.** Now and then a piece the size of a pebble or a fist arrives. It makes a meteor brighter than any star or planet, sometimes bright enough to cast shadows, and it may break up with a rumble heard minutes later. That is a fireball. If any of it reaches the ground, people go out looking.

**Where meteorites are found.** They fall everywhere, but they are easiest to find where a dark stone stands out: on the white ice of Antarctica and on the pale floors of deserts. Most of the meteorites in museums came from those two kinds of place.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A shooting star is a star falling.* The nearest star beyond the Sun is millions of millions of kilometres away. A meteor is a grain of dust about 100 km up.
- *Meteorites land red hot.* Only the outside melts, and only for a few seconds. A stone that reaches the ground has spent minutes falling through cold air and is usually no more than warm.
- *Meteors and comets are the same thing.* A comet is far away and hangs in the sky for weeks, hardly seeming to move. A meteor is over in a second.

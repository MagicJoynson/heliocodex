**Speed is the reason.** Anything arriving from space meets the Earth at between 11 and 72 km a second, tens of times faster than a rifle bullet. At that speed the air cannot get out of the way. It is squashed in front of the object and heated to thousands of degrees, and the object's surface boils off. Most meteors shine between about 100 and 70 km up and never get lower.

About 44 tonnes of this material is estimated to fall on the Earth every day, nearly all of it as dust.

**Showers.** A comet sheds dust all along its orbit. If the Earth's orbit crosses that trail, we run through it on the same date every year. The grains are travelling side by side, so their streaks all seem to spread out from one point in the sky, the way straight railway lines seem to meet in the distance. A shower is named after the constellation that point lies in.

| Shower | Best around | Where the dust comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Perseids | 12 August | Comet Swift-Tuttle |
| Leonids | 17 November | Comet Tempel-Tuttle |
| Geminids | 14 December | The asteroid Phaethon, unusually |

**Meteorites.** A piece that starts out bigger than a fist or so may reach the ground. Most meteorites are stony, some are nearly pure iron and nickel, and almost all are chips off asteroids. A few have been knocked off the Moon and Mars by impacts there. They are the oldest things anyone can hold: the oldest grains inside them formed 4.567 billion years ago, and that is how the age of the Solar System is known. Two spacecraft have also gone and fetched samples that never had to survive the fall: Japan's Hayabusa2 from the asteroid Ryugu in 2020, and NASA's OSIRIS-REx from Bennu in 2023.

**Bigger things.** The larger the object, the rarer it is.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| 2013, Chelyabinsk, Russia | A rock about 20 m across burst roughly 30 km up. The blast broke windows across the city and about 1,500 people were hurt, mostly by flying glass. |
| 1908, Tunguska, Siberia | An object perhaps 50 m across burst in the air and flattened about 2,000 square kilometres of forest. |
| About 50,000 years ago, Arizona | An iron meteorite about 50 m across dug Meteor Crater, 1.2 km wide. |
| 66 million years ago, Mexico | An asteroid about 10 km across made the Chicxulub crater, some 180 km wide. It is the main reason the dinosaurs died out. |

The Earth has been hit at least as often as the Moon, for its size. It shows fewer scars because rain, ice and moving continents wear craters away, while the Moon keeps every one.

**Watching for the next one.** Surveys have now found nearly all of the near-Earth asteroids more than a kilometre across, and none of them is on course to hit us in the next century or more. The search goes on for the smaller ones. In September 2022 NASA's DART spacecraft was deliberately flown into Dimorphos, a small moon of an asteroid, and shortened its orbit by about half an hour. It was the first time people had changed the path of a body in space, and a first test of how an impact might one day be turned aside.

**Where the two speeds come from.** The slowest arrivals come in at about 11 km a second because that is the speed anything picks up in falling to the Earth from far away, the same figure as the Earth's escape speed. The fastest is set by the Sun. The Earth goes round the Sun at about 30 km a second, and nothing that belongs to the Solar System can be moving faster than about 42 km a second at our distance. Meet such a thing head-on and the two speeds add to about 72 (a sum). The Leonids come in almost head-on, at about 71 km a second, and are among the fastest of all.

**A worked example.** The energy of a moving thing depends on its mass and on the square of its speed. A Perseid grain weighing one gram, at 59 km a second, carries about 1.7 million joules. That is the energy of a one-tonne car at just over 210 km an hour (a sum). All of it is dumped into the air in about a second, and that is why a speck can be seen from 100 km away.

The same rule explains Chelyabinsk. That rock is estimated to have released as much energy as about 440,000 tonnes of high explosive, roughly thirty times the bomb dropped on Hiroshima, and nearly all of it went into the air high above the city.

**How often things arrive.**

| Size | About how often |
|---|---|
| Dust and sand | All the time: more than 100 tonnes a day |
| A car | About once a year. It burns up as a fireball |
| A football pitch | About every 2,000 years. It does serious damage to the region where it falls |
| Big enough to threaten civilisation | Once every few million years |

The figures are NASA's, and they are averages. Nothing in them says when the next one is due.

**What meteorites are made of.** About 19 in every 20 that are seen to fall are stony. Most of those are full of tiny round beads of once-molten rock that formed in the cloud round the young Sun, before there were planets, and have not been melted since. The iron ones are pieces of the cores of small worlds that melted, sorted themselves into metal and rock, and were later smashed apart. A slice of an iron meteorite, polished and etched with acid, shows a criss-cross pattern of crystals that takes millions of years of slow cooling to grow. It cannot be faked, and it is one way of telling a real one.

**A close pass to watch.** On 13 April 2029 the asteroid Apophis, about 340 m across, will pass about 32,000 km above the Earth's surface. That is closer than the ring of television satellites, which sit at about 36,000 km. It will be visible to the eye from Europe and Africa as a moving point of light. Its path has been measured well enough to rule out any risk of an impact for at least a century.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Meteors glow because of friction.* Rubbing plays a small part. Most of the heat comes from the air in front being squashed, in the way that a bicycle pump warms up.
- *A crater is the size of the thing that made it.* It is far wider, often by twenty times or so. Meteor Crater is 1.2 km across and was dug by an object of about 50 m.
- *A meteor shower is dangerous.* Shower grains are specks from comets and none has ever been known to reach the ground. The rocks that land are pieces of asteroids, and they do not arrive in showers.

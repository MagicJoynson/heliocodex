Nobody has ever been to a star. Nobody has even sent a machine to one, apart from our own Sun. Yet we know what stars are made of.

The secret is in their light. If you pass sunlight through a prism it spreads into a band of colours. Look very closely at that band and you find it is crossed by hundreds of thin dark lines, like a barcode.

Each kind of atom makes its own set of lines. Hydrogen has one pattern. Iron has another. So the lines are like fingerprints. Find the pattern, and you know that atom is there.

In this way people found a new element in the Sun before anyone had found it on Earth. They named it helium, after the Greek word for the Sun.

**See it for yourself.** Tilt the shiny side of an old CD or DVD under a lamp and it throws the light back as a band of colours. The fine grooves on the disc do the job of a prism. An ordinary light bulb gives a smooth band. An orange street lamp, or a neon sign, gives something different: a few separate stripes of colour with dark gaps between them. Those stripes are the fingerprint of the gas inside the lamp.

**Bright lines and dark lines.** A thin, glowing gas gives out its own colours as bright lines. The same gas, sitting in front of a brighter light, takes those colours away and leaves dark lines in exactly the same places. A star is a bright, hot ball with thinner gas round the outside. So its light has dark lines in it, made by that outer gas.

**The same stuff everywhere.** The lines in the light of the furthest galaxies are the same patterns that hydrogen, calcium and iron make in a laboratory on the Earth. Atoms are the same everywhere that anyone has looked. The iron in your blood and the calcium in your bones have the same fingerprints as the iron and calcium in the stars, because that is where they were made.

**What else the lines tell.** They are more than a list of ingredients. If a star is coming towards us, the whole pattern of lines slides a little towards the blue end. If it is going away, the pattern slides towards the red. How strong each line is tells how hot the star is. From one streak of light, astronomers can read what a star is made of, how hot it is, how fast it is moving and how fast it spins.

**The story of helium.** On 18 August 1868 there was a total eclipse of the Sun over India. A French astronomer, Jules Janssen, looked at the light from the Sun's edge and saw a bright yellow line that nobody could place. Two months later an English astronomer, Norman Lockyer, saw the same line from Britain and decided it must belong to an unknown element. Helium was found on the Earth 27 years later, in 1895.

**Common mistakes.**

- *We know what stars are made of because spacecraft have been there.* Nothing has ever been sent to another star. It is all read from light.
- *Stars are made of strange stuff that the Earth does not have.* They are made of the same elements. The mixture is different: a star is nearly all hydrogen and helium.
- *The dark lines are colours that the star does not make.* The hot body of the star makes every colour. The lines are colours that were taken out again on the way through its outer gas.

Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun, just as half of the Earth is always in daylight. What changes is how much of that lit half is turned towards us.

The Moon goes round the Earth about once a month. When it is on the Sun's side of us, the lit half faces away and we see nothing: new Moon. A week later we see it side-on, half lit and half dark. A week after that it is on the far side of us from the Sun, with its whole lit face towards us: full Moon. Then the same in reverse.

The dark part is not the Earth's shadow. It is simply the Moon's own night side.

The Earth's shadow does reach the Moon now and then, and that is an eclipse of the Moon. It is rare because the Moon's path is slightly tilted, so most months the Moon passes just above or below the shadow.

Now and then the Moon passes exactly between us and the Sun, and hides it. That is an eclipse of the Sun. For a few minutes the day goes dark, and the Sun's thin outer glow, which is always there but drowned by daylight, stands out round the black Moon.

**The ghost of the full Moon.** Look at a thin crescent Moon just after sunset and you can often see the rest of the disc glowing faintly. That is earthshine. Sunlight bounces off the Earth, lights the Moon's night side, and a little of it bounces back to your eye. The Earth is a far brighter lamp in the Moon's sky than the Moon is in ours.

**The Moon by day.** The Moon is in the daytime sky as often as it is in the night sky. Around half Moon it is easy to find in a blue afternoon. It only seems to belong to the night because that is when it stands out.

**Seen from the other side.** An astronaut on the Moon would see the Earth go through phases too, and always the opposite of ours. When we have a new Moon, the Moon has a full Earth, nearly four times as wide as the Moon looks to us.

**Why an eclipsed Moon turns red.** During an eclipse of the Moon the Earth blocks the Sun's direct light, but the Earth's air bends a little sunlight round into the shadow. Air scatters blue light away and lets red through, which is why sunsets are red. The light that reaches the eclipsed Moon has skimmed through the air all round the edge of the Earth. It is the glow of every sunrise and sunset in the world at once.

**How often?** There is an eclipse of the Sun somewhere on Earth about every 18 months, but each one can be seen as total only from a narrow strip. Any one town waits, on average, close to 400 years between them. An eclipse of the Moon is the other way round: everyone on the night side of the world sees it at once.

**Two mistakes, and a warning.** The far side of the Moon is often called the dark side. It is not dark. It gets exactly as much sunshine as the near side, and at new Moon it is in full daylight. And the Moon does not make its own light at all: moonlight is sunlight, reflected. The warning is about the Sun. Never look at it directly, even when most of it is covered. Only during the few minutes when it is completely hidden is it safe, and an eclipse of the Moon is always safe to watch.

**Two kinds of month.** Measured against the stars, the Moon goes round the Earth in 27.32 days. The phases take longer to repeat, 29.53 days, because in that time the Earth has moved about a twelfth of the way round the Sun and the Moon has to travel a bit further to get back in line.

**Reading the phase.** The lit edge of the Moon always faces the Sun. A thin crescent in the evening sky is a young Moon following the Sun down. A half Moon high at sunset is a week old. A full Moon rises as the Sun sets and stays up all night. After that it rises later each night, by about 50 minutes, and the lit part shrinks from the other side.

**Why there is not an eclipse every month.** The Moon's orbit is tilted by 5.1° to the Earth's. That sounds small, but the Earth's shadow at the Moon's distance is only about 1.4° wide, so the Moon usually misses it. An eclipse needs the full or new Moon to fall near one of the two points where the tilted orbits cross. That line-up comes round about every six months.

**An eclipse of the Moon** happens when the full Moon passes through the Earth's shadow. Everyone on the night side of the Earth can watch, and the whole thing can last hours. In the deepest part of the shadow the Moon does not vanish. It turns a dull red, lit by sunlight that has been bent through the Earth's air.

**An eclipse of the Sun** happens when the new Moon passes in front of the Sun. The dark core of the Moon's shadow is narrow by the time it reaches us, never more than about 270 km across, and it sweeps over the ground at more than 1,700 km/h. Only people on that narrow track see the Sun fully covered, and never for longer than about seven and a half minutes.

A total eclipse works because of a coincidence. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and, at present, about 400 times further away, so the two look the same size. When the Moon is at the far end of its orbit it looks slightly too small, and a ring of Sun is left showing.

**The coincidence, in numbers.** Sizes in the sky are measured as angles. These are sums from NASA's fact sheets.

| | Smallest it looks | Largest it looks |
|---|---|---|
| The Sun | 0.525° (early July) | 0.542° (early January) |
| The Moon | 0.491° (furthest) | 0.548° (nearest) |

The two ranges overlap. When the Moon looks bigger than the Sun the eclipse is total. When it looks smaller, a ring of Sun is left, and the eclipse is called annular, from the Latin for a ring. The Moon is only just big enough even at its best, which is why the longest total eclipse lasts a few minutes and not hours.

**It will not last.** The Moon is moving away from the Earth by 3.8 cm a year. As it goes it looks smaller. Several hundred million years from now it will be too small to cover the Sun even at its nearest, and there will be no more total eclipses. In the distant past it was closer and covered the Sun with room to spare. We live in the stretch of time when the fit is nearly exact.

**Eclipses repeat.** Three rhythms of the Moon's orbit come back into step every 18 years, 11 days and 8 hours. After that time the Sun, Earth and Moon return to almost the same line-up and an eclipse repeats. Babylonian astronomers knew of this cycle more than two thousand years ago, and it is called the saros. The odd 8 hours matter: the Earth has made an extra third of a turn, so each repeat falls about a third of the way round the world. The total eclipse seen from Cornwall on 11 August 1999 came back over the United States on 21 August 2017, and returns over China and Japan on 2 September 2035.

**How rare for one place.** The last total eclipse of the Sun visible from the mainland of Britain was that one in 1999. The next is on 23 September 2090.

**How fast it all moves.** Against the stars the Moon moves its own width about every hour (a sum: 360° in 27.32 days is 0.55° an hour). The Earth's shadow at the Moon's distance is about 2.7 Moons wide, so the Moon can stay wholly inside it for more than an hour.

**Eclipses elsewhere.** The close fit is ours alone. On Mars the moon Phobos crosses the Sun several times a year, but it is too small to cover it and passes as a lumpy dark spot. Rovers have filmed it. At Jupiter the big moons throw round black shadows on the cloud tops, which can be seen through a small telescope from the Earth. No other planet has a moon that so nearly matches the Sun in its sky.

**What eclipses have taught.**

- *The Earth is a ball.* The edge of the Earth's shadow on the Moon is always part of a circle, whatever the time of night. Only a ball casts a round shadow from every side. Aristotle used this argument more than 2,300 years ago.
- *The Moon's size and distance.* Aristarchus, a century later, timed the Moon crossing the shadow and worked out how the Moon's width compares with the Earth's.
- *A new element.* In 1868 an unknown line was seen in the light from the Sun's edge during an eclipse. It was helium, found on the Sun before it was found on the Earth.
- *Gravity bends light.* In 1919 stars near the eclipsed Sun were photographed slightly out of place, as Einstein had said they would be.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A supermoon is huge.* A full Moon at the near end of the orbit is about 12 per cent wider than one at the far end, and about a quarter brighter (sums from the fact sheet). Side by side the difference would be plain. On separate nights hardly anyone can tell.
- *The Moon is bigger when it is low.* It looks enormous near the horizon, but it is the same size as when it is overhead. Hold a small coin at arm's length against it both times. Nobody is quite sure why the eye is fooled.
- *A blue Moon is blue.* The name is given to an extra full Moon in a season or a month. The colour is the same as ever.

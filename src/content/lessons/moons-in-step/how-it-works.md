**Whole numbers.** Two moons are in resonance when their periods are in a simple ratio, such as 2 to 1. Then they meet at the same point of their orbits every time, and each meeting pulls the same way as the last. Small pulls that would otherwise cancel out add up instead.

**Jupiter's three.**

| Moon | Time for one orbit | Orbits per orbit of Ganymede |
|---|---|---|
| Io | 1.769 days | 4.04 |
| Europa | 3.551 days | 2.01 |
| Ganymede | 7.155 days | 1 |

The ratios are a shade off 4, 2 and 1, and that small difference is part of the pattern, not a flaw in it: the meeting points drift slowly round Jupiter, and they drift together. One result is that the three moons can never all line up on the same side of Jupiter at once. When Europa and Ganymede pass each other, Io is always on the far side.

**What it does.** The repeated tugs stop the orbits from settling into circles. An oval orbit means a tide that rises and falls on every lap, and that means heat. This one rhythm keeps Io's volcanoes going and helps keep Europa's ocean liquid.

Saturn has the same arrangement on a smaller scale. Enceladus goes round twice for each orbit of the larger moon Dione, and that helps power its jets: the pairing keeps its orbit slightly oval, and the tides that follow do the heating.

**A moon that runs fast and slow.** Mimas goes round Saturn twice for each orbit of Tethys. The pull of Tethys does not hold Mimas at a steady pace. It makes Mimas run ahead of a steady clock for decades, then fall behind, swinging by tens of degrees either way over a cycle of about 70 years. Anyone drawing Saturn's moons has to allow for it, and this site does.

**Two moons, one orbit.** Janus and Epimetheus share almost the same path round Saturn, 50 km apart, and each takes about 16 hours 40 minutes. The inner one is a touch faster, so about every four years it catches the other up. They never collide and never pass. As they close to about 15,000 km, each one's gravity lifts the other into the opposite lane: the inner moon moves out and slows, the outer drops in and speeds up, and they draw apart again. Nothing else in the Solar System is known to do this.

**Gaps and edges.** Resonances shape rings too. A ring particle that goes round twice for every orbit of Mimas gets tugged at the same spot each time and is cleared away. The result is the Cassini Division, the wide dark gap in Saturn's rings that can be seen through a small telescope.

**Beyond moons.** The same thing happens between planets. Pluto goes round the Sun twice for every three orbits of Neptune, which is why the two never meet even though their paths cross.

**The rule the three obey.** The French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace noticed in the 1780s that the three inner moons keep to a rule that is far more exact than "about 4, 2 and 1". Work out how many degrees each moon covers in a day. Then take Io's figure, subtract three times Europa's, and add twice Ganymede's.

| Moon | Degrees round Jupiter each day |
|---|---|
| Io | 203.489 |
| Europa | 101.375 |
| Ganymede | 50.318 |

203.489 − 3 × 101.375 + 2 × 50.318 comes to zero, to three decimal places (a sum made from NASA's more exact periods of 1.769138, 3.551181 and 7.154553 days). The moons are not near a pattern. They are locked into one.

**How moons get into step.** They are not born that way. Tides raised in Jupiter by its moons slowly push the moons outward, and the inner ones move faster than the outer ones. As Io's orbit widened it caught up with the 2 to 1 rhythm with Europa and was trapped in it. The pair then moved out together until they caught Ganymede in the same way. Callisto, the fourth big moon, is not part of the pattern. It takes 16.69 days, which is 2.33 times Ganymede's lap and matches nothing.

**The price Io pays.** Io's tides lift its solid surface by as much as 100 metres. More than twenty times as much heat leaks out through each square metre of Io as through a square metre of the Earth. Io has turned itself inside out many times over.

**The same thing makes gaps.** Being in step can hold bodies in place or throw them out, and which one happens depends on the details. In the asteroid belt, an asteroid that would go round the Sun three times for each lap of Jupiter gets the same tug again and again and is pulled onto a stretched orbit that takes it away. The belt has clear gaps at those distances. It is the same clearing that Mimas does in Saturn's rings. So the rhythm that keeps Io where it is has emptied parts of the asteroid belt.

**A planet in step with itself.** Mercury turns on its axis exactly three times for every two trips round the Sun: 58.65 days for a turn and 87.97 days for a year. The Sun's tides trapped it there. One result is very strange. A day on Mercury, from one sunrise to the next, lasts two of its years.

**Why Neptune and Pluto never meet.** Pluto's stretched orbit brings it closer to the Sun than Neptune for 20 years in every 248. That looks like a collision waiting to happen. But three laps of Neptune take 494 years and two laps of Pluto take 496, near enough the same (sums from NASA's figures). Whenever Pluto is crossing Neptune's path, Neptune is far away round the other side of its orbit. The two never come closer than about 17 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

**Chains round other stars.** The seven planets of TRAPPIST-1 are in step all the way along: in the time the outermost goes round twice, the others go round 3, 4, 6, 9, 15 and 24 times. A star called HD 110067 has six planets in a chain of the same kind. Such chains are thought to be left over from the way planets drift inward through the disc they form in. They are fragile, and a system that still has one has probably been undisturbed since it formed.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The numbers have to be exact.* The laps are close to 4, 2 and 1 but not exactly. What is exact is the rule in the table above.
- *Being in step always keeps things steady.* It can do the opposite, as the gaps in the asteroid belt show.
- *This is astrology.* It is plain gravity, acting at the same place again and again. Nothing here depends on how things look from the Earth.

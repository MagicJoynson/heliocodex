A black hole is the one thing nothing escapes from. Whatever crosses its edge is gone for good, light included. So a black hole can only ever grow, and should last for ever. That was the settled view until 1974. It is probably wrong.

In that year Stephen Hawking, a 32-year-old physicist at Cambridge, did a sum that joined two parts of physics which do not usually meet: gravity, and the rules that govern atoms and smaller things. To his own surprise, the answer said that a black hole is not perfectly black. It should glow. The glow is very faint, and it comes from just outside the edge, so nothing has to break out from inside. But the energy for it has to come from somewhere, and it comes out of the black hole's mass.

How faint? A black hole with the mass of the Sun would glow like something at 60 billionths of a degree above the coldest possible temperature. Space itself is warmer than that: the faded light of the Big Bang fills it at 2.7 degrees. So every black hole known today takes in more than it gives out. They are still growing. And the rule runs backwards from what you would expect: the bigger the black hole, the colder it is.

But the universe is cooling. One day, a very long time from now, space will be colder than the black holes, and they will begin to shrink. Then the backwards rule turns into a runaway. As a black hole loses mass it gets hotter. Hotter, it glows more brightly and loses mass faster. It ends in a flash. In its last second it gives out the energy of about five million of the biggest hydrogen bombs.

The times are beyond imagining. A black hole with the mass of the Sun would take about 2 followed by 67 noughts of years to disappear. The universe is 14 billion years old, which is 1 followed by only 10. The giant black holes in the middles of galaxies would take far longer still. If the theory is right, they will be the last things left, and then they will go too.

Is it right? Nobody has ever seen this glow. From any real black hole it is far too faint to measure. There is one hope. If very small black holes were made in the first moments of the universe, some would be reaching their final flash about now, and telescopes in space are watching for flashes of that kind. None has been found. Almost every physicist believes Hawking's result all the same, because it fits so well with everything else.

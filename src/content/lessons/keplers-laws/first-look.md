Four hundred years ago Johannes Kepler spent years working through careful measurements of where Mars had been seen in the sky. He found three rules that every planet obeys.

  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Mars, the planet whose odd path gave the game away. The Hubble Space Telescope took this in 2018, when a dust storm had spread over the whole planet. Credit: NASA, ESA and STScI, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

**One.** A planet's path is not a circle. It is a slightly squashed circle called an ellipse, and the Sun is not in the middle but a little to one side.

**Two.** A planet speeds up as it comes nearer the Sun and slows down as it moves away.

  <img src="/img/learn/mercury-transit-2016-sdo.jpg" alt="The orange disc of the Sun with a line of small black dots running straight across it." width="1100" height="619" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>Many photographs, added together.</b> Mercury, the nearest planet to the Sun and the quickest, crossing in front of it on 9 May 2016. Each dot is Mercury a few minutes later. The crossing took seven and a half hours. Credit: NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center/SDO/Genna Duberstein.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Three.** The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year, and not only because it has further to go. It also moves more slowly. Mercury goes round in 88 days. Neptune takes 165 years.

<figure class="photo">

  <figcaption><b>A chart.</b> The third rule as a picture. Each step on either scale is ten times the last. Drawn like this, all eight planets fall on one straight line, which is what a rule looks like. Distances and years from NASA's planetary fact sheets.</figcaption>
</figure>

Kepler did not know why these rules worked. That took Isaac Newton, about seventy years later, who showed that all three follow from gravity.

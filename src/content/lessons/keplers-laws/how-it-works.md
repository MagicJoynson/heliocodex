**First law: orbits are ellipses, with the Sun at one focus.** How stretched an ellipse is gets measured by its eccentricity, from 0 for a circle up towards 1 for a long thin loop. The planets' orbits are close to circles. The Earth's eccentricity is 0.017 and you could not tell its orbit from a circle by eye. Mars is more lopsided at 0.094: it swings between 206.7 and 249.3 million km from the Sun. That difference was just big enough for Kepler to catch, which is why Mars gave the game away.

**Second law: a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.** Near the Sun the line is short, so the planet has to cover more of its path to sweep the same area. In other words it goes faster. The Earth moves at 30.3 km/s when nearest the Sun in January and 29.3 km/s when furthest in July. For a stretched orbit the change is huge. Halley's Comet passes the Sun at about 54 km/s and creeps round the far end of its orbit, out beyond Neptune, at less than 1 km/s.

**Third law: the square of the period goes as the cube of the orbit's size.** With the period P in years and the average distance a in au, it could not be simpler:

P² = a³

| Planet | a (au) | a³ | P (years) | P² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury | 0.387 | 0.058 | 0.241 | 0.058 |
| Earth | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| Mars | 1.524 | 3.54 | 1.881 | 3.54 |
| Jupiter | 5.203 | 140.9 | 11.86 | 140.7 |
| Neptune | 30.07 | 27,190 | 164.8 | 27,160 |

The two columns agree to about one part in a thousand. The small differences are real: the planets pull on one another, and their own mass counts for a little.

The same law holds for moons round a planet, but with a different constant, and the constant depends only on the mass of the thing in the middle. So timing a moon weighs its planet. That is how the masses of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune were first measured, and it is still how the mass of almost everything in astronomy is found.

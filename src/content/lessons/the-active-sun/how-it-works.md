**It is all magnetism.** The Sun is made of plasma, gas so hot that it carries electric currents, and moving currents make magnetic fields. The Sun does not turn as a solid ball. Its equator goes round in about 25 days and the regions near its poles take about 36. That difference drags the magnetic field round and round, winding it up like elastic until it is tangled and strained.

**Sunspots** are where bundles of that field break through the surface. The strong field holds back the hot gas rising from below, so the middle of the patch cools to about 3,900 °C while the surface around it is at about 5,500 °C. It is still very bright. It only looks dark beside its surroundings.

**The cycle.** Over about eleven years the tangle builds up, the spots multiply, and then the whole field collapses and re-forms the other way up, with north and south swapped. Then it starts again. People have counted sunspots since the early 1600s, and the cycles are numbered. The current one, the 25th, began in December 2019 and reached its busiest stretch around 2024.

**Flares and eruptions.** When strained magnetic field above a group of spots suddenly snaps into a simpler shape, the energy has to go somewhere.

| | What it is | Reaches the Earth in | What it does here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flare | A burst of light, including X-rays | 8 minutes | Can black out radio on the daylit side |
| Coronal mass ejection | A cloud of plasma, often a billion tonnes or more, thrown clear of the Sun | One to three days | Shakes the Earth's magnetic field: auroras, and trouble for power grids and satellites |

**Auroras.** The Earth's magnetic field turns most of the Sun's outflow aside. During a storm, particles are driven down along the field towards the two magnetic poles, and strike the thin air 100 km and more above the ground. Oxygen glows green, and red higher up. Nitrogen adds blue and purple.

**When it goes badly.** In September 1859 a great storm, now named after the astronomer Richard Carrington who saw the flare, made telegraph wires spark and put auroras in the sky over the tropics. In March 1989 a storm shut down the power grid of Quebec for nine hours. In May 2024 the strongest storm for twenty years brought auroras far further south than usual and did little damage, partly because grid and satellite operators now get warnings and prepare.

**How the cycle was found.** Nobody set out to look for it. In 1826 a German chemist and amateur astronomer, Heinrich Schwabe, began checking the Sun on every clear day, hoping to catch an unknown planet crossing its face. He never found one. But he recorded the spots, and after 17 years of notes he saw that their number rose and fell on a regular beat. He published it in 1843, guessing at ten years. Later counts, pushed back through old records to the 1600s, settled on an average of eleven, though single cycles have run from about nine to fourteen.

**How anyone knows it is magnetism.** Light from a strongly magnetised gas shows a tell-tale mark: each line in its spectrum is split into two or three. In 1908 the American astronomer George Ellery Hale found that split in the light from sunspots. The fields there are thousands of times stronger than the Earth's. It was the first time magnetism had been found anywhere beyond the Earth.

**When the cycle stopped.** Between about 1645 and 1715 sunspots almost vanished. Astronomers were looking, with good telescopes, and in some decades saw fewer spots than now appear in a single busy week. The gap is called the Maunder Minimum. It shows that the eleven-year beat is not guaranteed, and nobody can yet say when it might falter again.

**How fast a storm travels.** Light from a flare takes 8 minutes to reach the Earth, so by the time a flare is seen, its X-rays have already arrived. The cloud of gas is slower. A typical one takes about two days to cover the 150 million km, which works out at about 870 km a second. The cloud of 1859 took only 17.6 hours, which is about 2,400 km a second (both are sums from the distance and the time).

**How warnings are given.** Spacecraft watch the Sun from a point 1.5 million km from the Earth on the sunward side. They see flares at once and can watch a cloud set off. As the cloud sweeps over them they measure its speed and, most important, which way its magnetic field points, because a cloud does most damage when its field is opposite to the Earth's. At 500 km a second the cloud then needs about 50 minutes to cover the last 1.5 million km, and a very fast one needs less than a quarter of an hour (sums). That short gap is the firm warning that grid operators get. Forecasts made days ahead are rougher.

**What is at risk.** A storm makes the Earth's magnetic field shake, and a changing magnetic field drives electric currents in anything long and conducting: power lines, pipelines, undersea cables. Those currents can overheat the large transformers that grids depend on. Above the air, satellites are struck by fast particles, and the upper air swells and drags low satellites down. Astronauts outside the Earth's magnetic shelter, on the way to the Moon, would need a shielded place to wait out a bad storm.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Sunspots are holes in the Sun.* They are shallow dips in the bright surface, a few hundred kilometres deep on a ball 1.4 million km across.
- *A flare and an eruption are the same thing.* They often happen together, but a flare is a flash of light and an eruption is a cloud of matter. The table above shows how differently they behave.
- *Solar storms are getting worse.* They follow the cycle. What has changed is how much we rely on satellites and long power lines.
- *The corona is hot, so it would feel hot.* It is far too thin. A spacecraft inside it is heated mainly by sunlight, not by the gas, which is how the Parker Solar Probe survives.

**The mystery at the top.** The Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, is at more than a million degrees, hundreds of times hotter than the surface beneath it. That is like the air getting hotter as you walk away from a fire. Magnetic energy must be doing it, but exactly how is still argued over. NASA's Parker Solar Probe was sent to find out. In December 2024 it passed 6.1 million km above the surface, inside the corona itself, at about 690,000 km an hour: closer and faster than anything people have ever built.

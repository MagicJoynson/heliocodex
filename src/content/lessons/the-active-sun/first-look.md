Never look straight at the Sun. It will damage your eyes. But with a safe filter, astronomers can see that its face is not blank.

There are dark spots on it. Each one is a patch that is a little cooler than the rest, and many are bigger than the whole Earth. They come and go over days or weeks.

The number of spots rises and falls. About every eleven years there are many, and in between there are almost none.

When there are many spots the Sun is stormy. Sometimes it hurls a great cloud of gas out into space. If the cloud comes our way, it lights up the sky near the Earth's poles with moving curtains of green and red. These are the northern and southern lights.

**How anyone found out.** In 1610, soon after the telescope was invented, Galileo and others turned it on the Sun (by throwing its image onto paper, never by looking through it) and saw dark marks. Day by day the marks moved across the face of the Sun and went out of sight round the edge. Some came back two weeks later on the other side. That showed two things at once: the Sun is not a perfect, unchanging light, and it turns.

**How big is a spot?** A middling sunspot is about as wide as the Earth. A big group can be ten times that, and can be seen without a telescope through eclipse glasses. They look small only because the Sun is so large: 109 Earths would fit across it.

**Why are they dark?** A sunspot is a place where strong magnetism has broken through the surface and is holding back the heat coming up from below. The spot is about 1,500 degrees cooler than the gas round it. It is still hotter than molten iron, and on its own it would shine brilliantly. It looks black only because everything round it is brighter still.

**The Sun has weather.** Apart from the spots there are sudden flashes called flares, and vast loops and clouds of glowing gas that rise off the surface and sometimes break free. A thin stream of particles, the solar wind, blows out from the Sun all the time and fills the space between the planets. When the Sun is stormy the wind is gusty.

**What a storm does here.** The Earth is well protected. Its magnetism turns most of the Sun's particles aside, and its air stops the rest, so a solar storm cannot hurt anyone on the ground. What it can upset is our machinery: satellites, radio, and the long wires of power grids. In March 1989 a storm cut the electricity to the Canadian province of Quebec for nine hours.

**Where and when to see the lights.** The northern lights are seen most often from places near the Arctic Circle, such as northern Norway, Iceland and Alaska. In a big storm they spread towards the equator. In May 2024, during the strongest storm for twenty years, they were seen from all over Britain. The southern lights are the same thing seen from the far south, over Antarctica and at times from Tasmania and New Zealand. The years round the peak of the eleven-year cycle give the best chance.

**Two common mistakes.** The northern lights are not sunlight bouncing off ice or cloud. They are made by the air itself, glowing where particles strike it about 100 km up, in the same way that the gas in a neon sign glows. And an eleven-year cycle does not mean eleven-year weather on the Earth. Over a cycle the Sun's total output changes by only about a tenth of one per cent.

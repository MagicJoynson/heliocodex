**What comes first.** Most of the matter in the universe is dark matter, which pulls with gravity but gives off no light. It gathered into clumps first, and ordinary gas fell into the clumps and cooled there until it could make stars. That is why galaxies sit where the cosmic web is thickest: both were shaped by the same pull.

**The rise and fall of star making.** These figures are from Piero Madau and Mark Dickinson's review of 2014, which gathers the measurements of many surveys.

| | |
|---|---|
| Peak of star making | About 3.5 billion years after the beginning |
| How it has fallen since | It drops to about a third in every 3.9 billion years |
| Stars formed before the peak | About a quarter of all stars today |
| Half of all stars formed by | About 5 billion years after the beginning (the review gives redshift 1.3, turned into years by a sum) |
| Stars formed in the first billion years | Fewer than 1 in 100 |

Carried forward to today, that fall leaves the rate at about one fourteenth of its peak (a sum from the two figures above).

**Why some galaxies stop.** A galaxy makes stars only while it has cold gas. The big red galaxies have used theirs up, lost it, or had it heated until it cannot gather into stars. The same review found that the growth of the giant black holes in the middles of galaxies rose and fell in step with the making of stars, which is a sign that the two are linked. How is still being worked out.

**The galaxy the Milky Way ate.** The team that found it, led by Amina Helmi, named it Gaia-Enceladus. It was about the size of one of the Magellanic Clouds, the two small galaxies that can be seen from the southern half of the Earth. The Milky Way was much smaller then, so the two were matched about four to one. The collision is thought to have puffed up the Milky Way's early disc of stars.

**How anyone can know the history.** Nobody can watch one galaxy for billions of years. But looking far away is looking back in time, so a deep photograph holds galaxies at every age. The method is the one a visitor could use to work out how people grow up after a single day in a town: look at babies, children and adults and put them in order. The nearby galaxies are the adults. The faint red smudges at the limit of the Webb telescope are the infants.

**Small first, big later.** Galaxies were built from the bottom up. The first ones were small, lumpy and blue with new stars, and there were far more of them than there are galaxies today. Over time they ran into each other and merged, and the survivors grew. A galaxy as big as the Milky Way has probably swallowed dozens of smaller ones. It is still doing so: streams of stars torn from small galaxies can be traced right round our sky.

**A worked example: what happens when galaxies collide.** The stars themselves almost never touch. The Sun is 1.4 million km across, and the nearest star is 40 million million km away, about 29 million times further than the Sun is wide (a sum). Scale the Sun down to a grain of sand a millimetre across and the next grain is 29 km away. Two swarms of sand grains that thin pass straight through each other.

The gas is another matter. Clouds of gas are huge and they do collide. They are squeezed, and they make stars at a furious rate for a few hundred million years. Meanwhile gravity scrambles the orderly orbits of the stars. When two large spirals merge, what is left is usually a round, featureless galaxy whose gas has been used up: an elliptical.

**The Milky Way's future, revised.** The Andromeda galaxy, 2.5 million light years away, is coming towards us at about 110 km a second. If it kept to that speed and came straight on, it would arrive in about 7 billion years (a sum). Gravity will speed it up, and for years the standard forecast was a collision in about 4.5 billion years. In 2025 a new study allowed for the pulls of two smaller neighbours and for the uncertainty in Andromeda's sideways motion, which is very hard to measure. It found only about an even chance that the two will merge within the next 10 billion years. It is a good example of a confident figure that newer measurements have loosened.

**Black holes and their galaxies.** Nearly every large galaxy has a giant black hole in the middle, and the mass of the black hole is tied to the mass of the ball of stars round it: about a thousandth, give or take. That is strange, because the black hole's gravity matters only in the very centre. The likely link runs the other way. When gas pours into the black hole, the region round it blazes and drives fierce winds through the galaxy, which heat the gas or blow it out and so shut down the making of stars. The black hole and the galaxy seem to limit each other's growth.

**Where a galaxy lives matters.** A spiral that falls into a cluster of galaxies ploughs through the hot gas there at a thousand kilometres a second or more. The headwind strips its own gas out behind it in long streamers. With no gas it can make no more stars, and it fades to red. That is one reason the middles of clusters are full of red galaxies.

**Webb's surprise.** When the Webb telescope began work in 2022 it found more bright galaxies in the first few hundred million years than the models had predicted. Either the first galaxies made stars more efficiently than anyone expected, or their light is being misread. This is being argued over now, and the account in this lesson may need revising when it is settled.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Galaxies are fixed and unchanging.* They change all the time, but over hundreds of millions of years.
- *When galaxies collide, stars smash into each other.* Almost none do. The gaps are too wide.
- *A spiral turns into an elliptical as it gets old.* Age alone does not do it. It takes a merger, or the loss of its gas.
- *The biggest galaxies formed first.* The small ones did. The giants were assembled from them.

**The record will not stand.** JADES-GS-z14-0 was the most distant galaxy with a measured distance when its picture was published on 30 May 2024. Webb is still looking, and records of this kind are made to be broken.

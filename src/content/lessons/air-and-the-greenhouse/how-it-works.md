**How the blanket works.** Sunlight arrives mostly as visible light, and air is transparent to that. The ground soaks it up, warms, and gives the energy back out as infrared, which is heat you cannot see. Some gases, carbon dioxide and water vapour above all, absorb infrared. They catch the heat on its way out and send part of it back down. The ground ends up warmer than it would be with no air.

**Three planets compared.**

| | Venus | Earth | Mars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distance from the Sun (Earth = 1) | 0.72 | 1 | 1.52 |
| Air pressure at the ground (Earth = 1) | 92 | 1 | 0.006 |
| The air is mostly | Carbon dioxide, 96% | Nitrogen 78%, oxygen 21% | Carbon dioxide, 95% |
| Average temperature at the ground | About 464 °C | About 15 °C | About −65 °C |
| Warming from the greenhouse effect | About 500 degrees | About 33 degrees | About 5 degrees |

**Venus** is the extreme. Its clouds are so bright that it soaks up less sunlight than the Earth does, even though it is closer to the Sun. With no greenhouse effect it would be colder than we are. Instead its crushing carbon dioxide air holds the surface at about 464 °C, day and night, pole to equator. That is hotter than Mercury, which is much nearer the Sun.

**The Earth** without its greenhouse gases would average about −18 °C, and the oceans would freeze over. The natural effect is what makes the planet liveable. The concern today is about adding to it: burning coal, oil and gas has raised the carbon dioxide in the air by about half since the 1700s.

**Mars** has air that is mostly carbon dioxide too, but so little of it that it barely helps. Dry riverbeds and lake deposits show that Mars once had thicker air and running water.

**Working out a planet's temperature without its air.** A planet settles at the temperature where it gives out as much energy as it takes in. Three numbers are enough: how strong the sunlight is, how much of it the planet reflects straight back, and the rule for how fast a warm body gives out heat. The sums below use NASA's figures.

| | Venus | Earth | Mars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunlight arriving, watts on each square metre | 2,601 | 1,361 | 586 |
| Share reflected back to space | 77% | 29% | 25% |
| Sunlight kept, averaged over the whole globe | 150 | 240 | 110 |
| Temperature that balances it | −47 °C | −18 °C | −63 °C |
| Real temperature at the ground | 464 °C | 15 °C | About −65 °C |

Two things stand out. Venus keeps less sunlight than the Earth does, because its bright clouds throw three quarters of it away. By this sum Venus ought to be colder than the Earth. And the gap between the last two rows is the greenhouse effect: about 510 degrees for Venus, 33 for the Earth, and too small to be sure of for Mars.

**How it was found out.** In the 1820s the French mathematician Joseph Fourier did a sum like the one above and saw that the Earth was warmer than sunlight alone could make it. Something had to be holding heat in. In 1859, at the Royal Institution in London, John Tyndall measured which gases do it. He shone heat through tubes of different gases and found that oxygen and nitrogen let it straight through, while water vapour and carbon dioxide soaked it up. In 1896 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius worked out, by hand, how much the Earth would warm if the carbon dioxide in the air were doubled.

**How anyone knows what Venus is like.** Venus is covered in cloud, and until the space age people imagined swamps and jungles beneath it. Radio measurements in the late 1950s gave the first hint that it was very hot. The Soviet Venera landers settled it. Venera 7, in 1970, was the first craft to send signals from the surface of another planet. It lasted about twenty minutes before the heat and pressure finished it. The pressure there is the same as nearly a kilometre down in the sea.

**"Greenhouse" is a poor name.** A glass greenhouse stays warm mainly because the glass stops the warm air inside from blowing away. The air round a planet works differently. Nothing is shut in. The gases catch heat radiation on its way out and send some of it back. The name has stuck, but the two are different things.

**A balance that corrects itself, slowly.** The Earth has a thermostat. When the planet warms, more rain falls and washes carbon dioxide out of the air and into rock a little faster, and the blanket thins. When it cools, the washing slows, volcanoes go on adding carbon dioxide, and the blanket thickens. This has kept the Earth's seas liquid for nearly 4 billion years, even though the Sun was fainter at the start. But it works over hundreds of thousands of years. It is far too slow to deal with changes made in a century or two.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Venus is hot because it is close to the Sun.* Mercury is closer and its night side is far below freezing. Venus is hot because of its air.
- *Carbon dioxide is most of our air.* It is about 0.04 per cent. That such a small share matters so much is the whole point.
- *Mars is cold only because it is far away.* Distance is part of it. But with air as thick as the Earth's, of the same gases as it has now, Mars would be a good deal warmer than it is.

**Why they ended up so different.** All three probably began with similar ingredients.

- **Venus** was a little too close to the Sun. Any early oceans evaporated, and water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas, so warming fed more warming. With no water, carbon dioxide from volcanoes had nowhere to go, and it stayed in the air.
- **Earth** kept its oceans. Rain washes carbon dioxide out of the air, and it ends up locked in limestone and other rock. There is about as much carbon in the Earth's rocks as in the whole atmosphere of Venus.
- **Mars** is small, with a tenth of the Earth's mass. Its weak gravity holds air poorly, its interior cooled, and it lost the magnetic field that had sheltered its air from the solar wind. Most of its atmosphere leaked away to space.

How much water Venus ever had, and for how long, is still an open question. No spacecraft has yet settled it.

**A wider lesson.** Astronomers talk about a habitable zone round a star, where a planet could have liquid water. Venus and Mars both sit near the edges of the Sun's. They show that being in the zone is only the start: what a planet's air does matters as much as where the planet is.

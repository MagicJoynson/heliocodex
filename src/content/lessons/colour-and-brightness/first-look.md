Look closely on a clear night and you will see that the stars are not all white. Betelgeuse, in the shoulder of Orion, is orange. Rigel, at Orion's foot, is blue-white.

The colour is a thermometer. Think of a piece of metal in a fire. First it glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white. If you could make it hotter still it would turn blue.

Stars work the same way. Red stars are the coolest. Blue stars are the hottest. The Sun is in between.

Brightness is harder. A star can look bright because it really is powerful, or just because it is near. To know which, you have to find out how far away it is.

**Try it tonight.** Orion is in the evening sky from late autumn to early spring. Find the three stars in a row that make its belt. Betelgeuse is the bright star on one side of the belt and Rigel is the bright star on the other. Look from one to the other and the difference in colour is plain. Binoculars make it stronger.

**Why most stars look white.** The part of the eye that sees colour needs plenty of light. Most stars are too faint to set it off, so they look white or grey even when they are not. Only the brightest few dozen show their colours to the eye alone. A camera gathers light for longer, which is why photographs of the sky are so much more colourful than the sky looks.

**Near or powerful? Two examples.** Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky. It is also one of the nearest, 8.6 light-years away, and it gives out about 23 times as much visible light as the Sun. Rigel looks a little fainter than Sirius, but it is about a hundred times further off. To look that bright from there, it has to give out about 52,000 times as much visible light as the Sun. If Rigel were moved to where Sirius is, it would be a single point nearly as bright as a half Moon, and it would cast shadows (a sum from the two distances).

**What colour is the Sun?** White. It looks yellow from the ground because the air scatters some of its blue light away across the sky, and it looks orange or red at sunset for the same reason. Among the stars, the Sun is in the middle for temperature.

**The stars nobody can see.** Most of the stars in our part of the Galaxy are small, cool and red, and they are so dim that not one of them can be seen without a telescope. That includes the nearest star of all, Proxima Centauri. The bright stars in the sky are not typical. They are the rare powerful ones, seen from a long way off.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Red means hot and blue means cold.* That is the code on taps, and stars are the other way round.
- *The North Star is the brightest star.* It is not in the top forty. It is famous because it hardly moves as the sky turns.
- *A star flashing red, green and blue is changing colour.* Sirius often does this when it is low in the sky. The star is steady. The flashing is made by the Earth's unsteady air, which bends each colour a little differently.

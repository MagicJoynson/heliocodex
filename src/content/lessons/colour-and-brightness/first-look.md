Look closely on a clear night and you will see that the stars are not all white. Betelgeuse, in the shoulder of Orion, is orange. Rigel, at Orion's foot, is blue-white.

  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The colours are real. This is Orion above the ALMA telescopes in Chile, seen upside down from the south. Betelgeuse is the orange star in the middle of the sky, to the left of the largest dish. Rigel is the bright blue-white star above that dish. Credit: ESO/B. Tafreshi (twanight.org), CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

The colour is a thermometer. Think of a piece of metal in a fire. First it glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white. If you could make it hotter still it would turn blue.

Stars work the same way. Red stars are the coolest. Blue stars are the hottest. The Sun is in between.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/omega-centauri-hubble.jpg" alt="A field packed with thousands of stars: many yellow-white, some bright blue, some orange and red." width="1100" height="562" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> About 100,000 stars in the cluster Omega Centauri, by the Hubble Space Telescope, in their real colours. The red ones are cool giants, the blue ones are the hottest, and the yellow-white ones are like the Sun. Credit: NASA, ESA and the Hubble SM4 ERO Team, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

Brightness is harder. A star can look bright because it really is powerful, or just because it is near. To know which, you have to find out how far away it is.

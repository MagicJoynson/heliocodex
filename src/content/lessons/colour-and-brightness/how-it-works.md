**Colour is temperature.** Anything hot and dense glows, and the hotter it is the bluer its light. So the colour of a star gives the temperature of its surface, with no need to go there. Temperatures here are in kelvin: the same size of degree as Celsius, counted from absolute zero.

Astronomers sort stars into seven main classes by the pattern of dark lines in their light, which turns out to be an order of temperature.

| Class | Colour | Surface temperature | An example |
|---|---|---|---|
| O | Blue | Above 30,000 K | Alnitak, in Orion's Belt |
| B | Blue-white | 10,000 to 30,000 K | Rigel |
| A | White | 7,500 to 10,000 K | Sirius, Vega |
| F | Yellow-white | 6,000 to 7,500 K | Procyon |
| G | Yellow | 5,200 to 6,000 K | The Sun (5,772 K) |
| K | Orange | 3,700 to 5,200 K | Arcturus |
| M | Red | Below 3,700 K | Betelgeuse, Proxima Centauri |

The odd order of the letters is an accident of history. The classes were first lettered by the strength of one set of lines, and were shuffled into order of temperature afterwards.

**Brightness needs distance.** How bright a star looks depends on how much light it gives out and how far away it is. Light thins out with the square of distance: move a star twice as far away and it looks four times fainter. So once the distance is known, the star's true output can be worked out. Distances to the nearer stars come from parallax, the small shift in a star's position as the Earth goes round the Sun. The Gaia spacecraft has measured it for more than a billion stars.

**The chart.** Around 1910 two astronomers, Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell, each had the idea of plotting true brightness against temperature. If the two had nothing to do with each other the dots would scatter everywhere. They do not.

About nine stars in ten fall on one band running from hot and bright to cool and faint. This is the main sequence, and it holds every star that is steadily burning hydrogen in its core, the Sun included. Where a star sits along it depends on its mass.

Two groups sit off the band. Above it to the right are stars that are cool but very bright, which can only mean they are enormous: the giants and supergiants. Betelgeuse is one. Put in place of the Sun it would reach out past the orbit of Mars. Below the band to the left are stars that are hot but very faint, so they must be tiny: the white dwarfs, about the size of the Earth.

**What the chart is for.** The chart is a picture of how stars live. The main sequence is crowded because stars spend most of their lives there. The giants and white dwarfs are stars that have left it. The next lesson follows a star along that route.

**Where the light peaks.** A hot body gives out light of every colour, but the mixture has a peak, and the peak moves towards the blue as the temperature rises. There is a simple rule: divide 2.9 million by the temperature in kelvin, and the answer is the wavelength of the peak in nanometres (millionths of a millimetre). The eye sees from about 400 (violet) to about 700 (deep red).

| Star | Surface temperature | Peak of its light (a sum) |
|---|---|---|
| Betelgeuse | 3,600 K | 805, just beyond red, in the infrared |
| The Sun | 5,772 K | 502, in the green |
| Rigel | 12,100 K | 240, in the ultraviolet |

The Sun's light peaks in the green, and yet there are no green stars. The peak is broad, so a star that peaks in the green also gives out plenty of red and blue, and the eye reads the whole mixture as white. The table also shows that the eye misses most of what Betelgeuse and Rigel give out. Their true outputs have to be measured with instruments that see infrared and ultraviolet.

**The backwards scale.** Astronomers measure how bright a star looks in magnitudes, on a scale that comes down from the Greek astronomer Hipparchus more than 2,100 years ago. He called the brightest stars "first magnitude" and the faintest he could see "sixth". So a smaller number means a brighter star, and the very brightest have gone below zero. Today the scale is fixed so that 5 magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100 in brightness. Sirius is magnitude −1.44, Rigel is 0.18, and the faintest stars the eye can see from a dark place are about 6.

**A worked example.** Rigel is 1.62 magnitudes fainter than Sirius, which means Sirius looks about 4.4 times brighter. But Rigel is about 860 light-years away and Sirius is 8.6, so Rigel is about 100 times further off, and distance alone has dimmed it 100 × 100 = 10,000 times. Rigel must therefore be giving out about 10,000 ÷ 4.4, or roughly 2,200 times as much visible light as Sirius.

**Size from colour and brightness.** Each square metre of a hot surface gives out far more light than the same area of a cool one: double the temperature and it gives out 16 times as much. So once the temperature and the total output of a star are known, the size of its surface follows, and no telescope needs to see the star as a disc.

Take Sirius B, the white dwarf that goes round Sirius. Its surface is at about 25,400 K, 4.4 times hotter than the Sun's, so each square metre of it gives out about 370 times as much light as a square metre of the Sun. Yet the whole star gives out only one fortieth of the Sun's light. Its surface must be about 15,000 times smaller in area than the Sun's, which makes it about 120 times smaller across: a radius of about 5,600 km, a little less than the Earth's. A star with the mass of the Sun is packed into a ball smaller than our planet.

Betelgeuse is the opposite case. At 3,600 K each square metre gives out about a seventh of what the Sun's does, but the whole star gives out something like 65,000 to 87,000 times the Sun's light. Only a star roughly 700 times as wide as the Sun has enough surface to do that.

**How the classes were made.** The seven classes were put in their present order at Harvard College Observatory by Annie Jump Cannon. Working from photographs of spectra between 1896 and 1940, she classified about 350,000 stars by eye, more than anyone before or since. In 1925 Cecilia Payne showed what the classes meant. The stars are nearly all made of the same things, mostly hydrogen and helium, and the lines differ from class to class because the temperatures differ.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A class tells you the size.* It tells you the temperature only. Betelgeuse and Proxima Centauri are both class M. One is among the largest stars known and the other is about a seventh as wide as the Sun.
- *The Sun is an average star.* It is in the middle of the range of temperature, but because small red stars are so common, it outshines about nine stars in ten.
- *The brightest-looking stars are the nearest.* Some are, like Sirius. Many are far away and very powerful, like Rigel.

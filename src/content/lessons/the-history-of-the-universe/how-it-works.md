**The scale.** All the calendar dates in this lesson were worked out here, by dividing 13.8 billion years into 365 days.

| On the calendar | In real time |
|---|---|
| One day | 37.8 million years |
| One hour | 1.6 million years |
| One minute | 26,000 years |
| One second | 438 years |

**The events.**

| What happened | When, in real time | On the calendar |
|---|---|---|
| The first elements are made | The first five minutes | Far less than a second after midnight, 1 January |
| The fog clears and the oldest light sets out | 380,000 years after the start | 14 minutes past midnight, 1 January |
| The first stars | About 200 million years after the start | 6 January |
| Small young galaxies, as in the Hubble Ultra Deep Field | About 800 million years after the start | 22 January |
| The Sun, the Earth and the planets form | About 4.6 billion years ago | 1 September |
| Life begins on the Earth | About 3.8 billion years ago | 22 September |
| An asteroid ends the age of the dinosaurs | 66 million years ago | About 6 in the morning, 30 December |
| Our own species appears | About 300,000 years ago | About 11 minutes to midnight, 31 December |
| A human life of 80 years | | Less than a fifth of a second |

**What the calendar hides.** A calendar gives every day the same weight, but the universe did not change at an even pace. More happened to it in the first second than in any billion years since: it went from a soup of particles at 10 billion degrees to something with the makings of atoms. A fair picture of the early universe needs a scale that stretches the start, where each step is ten times longer than the one before. The table in [The first minutes](/learn/the-first-minutes) is laid out that way.

**Why the dates are rounded.** The early dates are known only roughly. "About 200 million years" for the first stars could be out by tens of millions, which is a day or more on the calendar. The age of the universe itself is known to about 1 per cent.

**Where the idea comes from.** The astronomer Carl Sagan made this "cosmic calendar" famous in the 1970s. The dates here use today's figure for the age of the universe.

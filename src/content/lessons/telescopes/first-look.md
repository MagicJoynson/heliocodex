Most people think a telescope is for making things look bigger. Its main job is something else: to catch more light.

The opening in your eye is only a few millimetres wide, so it catches very little. A telescope is like a much bigger eye. It gathers light with a wide lens or mirror and squeezes it all into yours. Faint things become bright enough to see.

So the wider a telescope is, the more it can show. The first ones, four hundred years ago, were about as wide as a coin. The biggest today have mirrors wider than a house.

Some telescopes are sent into space, above the air. The air makes stars twinkle, which is pretty but blurs the view. Above it, everything is perfectly sharp.

**What Galileo saw.** In 1609 and 1610 Galileo pointed a small telescope at the sky and found things nobody had seen. The Moon had mountains and craters. Jupiter had four moons of its own going round it. The pale band of the Milky Way turned out to be countless faint stars. A tube with two lenses, catching a few dozen times as much light as the eye, was enough to change what people thought the sky was.

**Try it with binoculars.** A pair of binoculars is two small telescopes side by side. Each front lens is usually about 50 mm across, and it catches about 50 times as much light as your eye (a sum). On a dark night that is enough to show the moons of Jupiter, the craters of the Moon and thousands of stars that the eye alone cannot see.

**Adding up the light.** An eye takes a fresh picture many times a second. A camera on a telescope can keep collecting for hours and add all the light together. In December 1995 the Hubble Space Telescope stared for ten days at a patch of sky that looked empty, a patch no bigger than a tennis ball seen from 100 metres away. The picture showed about 3,000 galaxies.

**Why telescopes sit on mountains.** The best places are high, dry and far from city lights, where the air is thin and steady. That is why so many big telescopes are in the deserts of Chile and on the tops of Hawaii and the Canary Islands.

**Other kinds of light.** Stars and galaxies also give out kinds of light that the eye cannot see, such as radio waves, infrared and X-rays. There are telescopes for all of them. A radio telescope looks like a huge satellite dish. The Webb telescope sees infrared, which is how it looks through dust and back to the earliest galaxies.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The best telescope is the one that magnifies most.* Width matters most. A wide telescope shows faint things and fine detail. Magnifying a narrow one just gives a bigger blur.
- *Hubble is far out in space.* It is only about 500 km up, closer to the ground than London is to Edinburgh. It is in space to be above the air, not to be nearer the stars.
- *A telescope shows things as they are now.* Light takes time to travel, so a telescope always shows the past: 8 minutes ago for the Sun, millions of years ago for a far galaxy.

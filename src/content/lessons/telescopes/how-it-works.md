**Two jobs.** A telescope does two things, and both depend on the width of its main lens or mirror, called its aperture.

*Collecting light.* The light gathered goes with the area of the opening. A wide-open eye has a pupil about 7 mm across. A modest telescope with a 200 mm mirror collects about 800 times as much, and so shows stars 800 times fainter.

*Seeing detail.* Light is a wave, and waves spread a little as they pass through any opening. The wider the opening, the less they spread and the finer the detail. Doubling the width doubles the sharpness.

Magnifying is the easy part: any telescope can be made to magnify as much as you like by changing the eyepiece. Past a certain point it only makes a bigger blur.

**Lenses and mirrors.** Galileo's telescopes of 1609 used lenses. A lens can only be held round its edge and sags under its own weight if it is large, and the biggest ever used for astronomy is about a metre across. In 1668 Isaac Newton built a telescope that used a curved mirror instead. A mirror can be supported from behind, so it can be made far bigger, and every large telescope since has used one.

| Telescope | Mirror width | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Galileo's, 1609 | A lens of about 4 cm | Italy |
| Hubble, 1990 | 2.4 m | In orbit, about 500 km up |
| Webb, 2021 | 6.5 m, in 18 gold-coated segments | 1.5 million km from the Earth |
| Rubin, first pictures 2025 | 8.4 m | Chile |
| Gran Telescopio Canarias, 2009 | 10.4 m | La Palma |
| Extremely Large Telescope, being built | 39 m | Chile |

**The trouble with air.** Air is always moving, and it bends starlight this way and that many times a second. That is why stars twinkle, and it smears their images to several times the size a big telescope could otherwise achieve. There are three answers. Build on high mountains in dry places, above much of the air. Put the telescope in space, as with Hubble. Or measure the blurring as it happens and bend a thin mirror hundreds of times a second to cancel it, which is how the largest telescopes on the ground now match or beat Hubble's sharpness.

**Other kinds of light.** Radio waves are far longer than light waves, so to see the same detail a radio telescope must be vastly wider. The largest single dish, in China, is 500 m across. Better still, the signals from dishes far apart can be combined so that they act like parts of one dish as wide as the gap between them. In 2019 a team linking radio dishes on four continents, in effect a telescope the size of the Earth, published the first picture of the shadow of a black hole, in a galaxy about 55 million light years away.

**A worked example: how sharp?** The smallest angle a telescope can separate is about 1.22 times the wavelength of the light divided by the width of the mirror. For green light, 550 millionths of a millimetre, the sums come out like this.

| Width | Finest detail, in seconds of arc | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| The eye, 7 mm | 20 at the very best | In practice the eye manages about 60 |
| 200 mm telescope | 0.7 | The air usually blurs more than this |
| Hubble, 2.4 m | 0.058 | A car's two headlights from about 5,000 km away |
| Extremely Large Telescope, 39 m | 0.0035 | Things about 7 m apart on the Moon |

A second of arc is one 3,600th of a degree. The same sum shows why no telescope has photographed the Apollo landers from the Earth. At the Moon's distance Hubble cannot separate things less than about 100 m apart, and the landers are about 4 m wide. They have been photographed, but by a spacecraft in orbit round the Moon.

**A worked example: how faint?** Light caught goes with the area. The Extremely Large Telescope will have 978 square metres of mirror. That is more than 200 times Hubble's, and about 25 million times the area of the pupil of an eye (sums).

**Many small mirrors.** A single mirror much more than 8 m across is too heavy and bends too much to be made. The answer is to build the mirror from many six-sided pieces, each steered by a computer so that together they act as one surface. The first big telescope built this way was Keck I in Hawaii, which began work in 1993 with 36 pieces making a mirror 10 m across. Webb has 18 pieces. The Extremely Large Telescope will have 798.

**What the air lets through.** The air is clear to visible light and to radio waves, and to very little else. It blocks nearly all ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, which is fortunate for living things, and most infrared. Telescopes for those kinds of light have to go into space, or at least to the driest mountain tops.

Infrared brings one more problem. Anything warm glows in infrared, including a telescope. Webb therefore sits behind a sunshield the size of a tennis court and is kept at about 230 degrees below freezing, so that its own glow does not drown out what it is looking at.

**From the eye to the chip.** Until the 1800s the only detector was the eye, which keeps no record and cannot add up light. Photographic plates could, but they caught only a few per cent of the light that fell on them. The electronic chips used since the 1980s catch most of it. Swapping plates for chips did as much for old telescopes as doubling their width would have done.

**A telescope the size of the Earth.** The picture of the black hole's shadow was made at a wavelength of 1.3 mm, with dishes up to about 12,700 km apart. The same sum gives a finest detail of about 25 millionths of a second of arc. That is enough to make out something 5 cm across on the Moon (a sum).

**Common mistakes.**

- *Space telescopes are always better.* A space telescope is above the air, but it has to be small enough to launch. For catching faint light and for splitting it into a spectrum, the giant telescopes on the ground are ahead.
- *Telescopes mostly take pictures.* Most of the time on a big telescope goes on spreading light into a spectrum, which is where the measurements are.
- *The colours in space pictures are fake.* Many are true. Where the light is of a kind the eye cannot see, colours have to be chosen, and a good caption says so.

**Looking out is looking back.** Light takes time to arrive, so a telescope shows distant things as they were when the light left. The galaxies at the limit of Webb's sight appear as they were more than 13 billion years ago, a few hundred million years after the universe began.

**Not only light.** Since 2015 there have been detectors for gravitational waves, ripples in space itself, and there are others deep under ice and rock for neutrinos. They are the first ways of studying the universe that do not use light at all.

The word constellation is used in two ways. To most people it is a figure made by joining bright stars. To astronomers it is one of 88 areas that together cover the whole sky, so that every star, galaxy and passing comet is in exactly one of them. The figures are old and came first. The areas are the modern, official version.

**A pattern has depth.** These are the seven bright stars of Orion, with distances from the star catalogue this site draws.

| Star | Part of the figure | Distance in light years |
|---|---|---|
| Bellatrix | Shoulder | 250 |
| Betelgeuse | Shoulder | 500 |
| Saiph | Knee | 650 |
| Mintaka | Belt | 690 |
| Alnitak | Belt | 740 |
| Rigel | Foot | 860 |
| Alnilam | Belt, the middle star | Given as nearly 2,000 |

The nearest is about 250 light years away and the furthest several times that. Catalogue distances beyond a thousand light years are uncertain, so Alnilam's may be well out, but the point stands: seen from the side, Orion is a long, thin scatter of stars, not a figure.

**Why the sky changes with the seasons.** The stars are there all the time, but you can only see them at night, and night is the side of the Earth facing away from the Sun. As the Earth goes round the Sun, that side looks out on a different part of space. Each night the stars are a little further west than at the same time the night before, and after a year they are back where they started. A constellation that lies in the direction of the Sun is up only in the daytime and cannot be seen at all.

**Why it depends on where you are.** The northern and southern halves of the Earth face different ways. Someone in Australia can see some constellations that someone in Britain cannot, and the other way round.

**The zodiac.** The Sun, Moon and planets all keep close to one line across the sky, called the ecliptic. The constellations that line runs through are the zodiac. Astrology uses them to make claims about people's lives and futures. Those claims are not based on scientific evidence. Astronomy uses the same constellations only as places.

**A worked example: four minutes a night.** The Earth turns once against the stars in 23 hours 56 minutes, not 24. The extra four minutes of a day are the time it takes to turn a little further and face the Sun again, because the Earth has moved along its orbit. So each star rises about four minutes earlier every night. The sum is 24 hours divided by 365 days, which is 3.9 minutes. After a month that adds up to two hours, and after a year to a full day, which brings the sky back where it began. A constellation that rises at midnight in October rises at ten in the evening in November.

**A worked example: what you can never see.** From a place at latitude 52 degrees north, such as the middle of Britain, the stars within 52 degrees of the sky's north pole never set. They circle the pole all night, all year. The Plough is among them. In the same way, stars within 52 degrees of the south pole of the sky never rise. The Southern Cross is about 30 degrees from that pole, so it is never seen from Britain. On the equator every star rises and sets, and both poles of the sky lie on the horizon.

**How the borders were made.** In 1922 the International Astronomical Union settled on the list of constellations. A Belgian astronomer, Eugène Delporte, then drew borders along lines of the sky's own grid, so that every point belongs to exactly one constellation. They were published in 1930. Of the 88, fourteen were named in the 1750s by one man, the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, who mapped the southern stars from the Cape of Good Hope and named his new constellations after instruments.

**How stars get their names.** Most of the old names of bright stars are Arabic. Rigel comes from the word for foot. In 1603 a German astronomer, Johann Bayer, gave the stars of each constellation Greek letters, roughly in order of brightness. That is why the nearest bright star is called Alpha Centauri: the first star of the Centaur.

**The slow wobble.** The Earth's axis is not fixed. It swings round in a circle like the axis of a spinning top, once in about 25,800 years. Two things follow. The pole star changes: when the pyramids were being built, the star nearest the pole was Thuban, in the Dragon, and in about 12,000 years it will be the bright star Vega. And the dates of the zodiac slip. The star signs of astrology were fixed more than two thousand years ago. Since then the sky has slid round by about a month, so on most birthdays the Sun is not in the constellation that the star sign names. By the official borders the Sun also crosses a thirteenth constellation, Ophiuchus, which astrology leaves out.

**The stars themselves move.** Every star has a slow drift of its own across the sky. It was first noticed in 1718, when Edmond Halley found that three bright stars were no longer where Greek astronomers had recorded them. The fastest is Barnard's Star, a dim red star six light years away, which crosses the width of the full Moon in about 175 years (a sum from its measured drift). Most stars move hundreds of times more slowly than that, which is why the figures have lasted through all of recorded history.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The constellations are the same all over the world.* The official 88 are, by agreement. The pictures people grew up with differ from one culture to another.
- *The winter stars are only there in winter.* They are overhead in summer too, in the daytime.
- *The North Star is the brightest star.* It is not in the top forty. What makes it special is where it is.
- *A constellation has a true shape.* It has a shape only from here. From another star it would not be a figure at all.

**What they are used for now.** The stars hold nearly the same positions for many lifetimes, so constellations make good landmarks. Many stars and nebulae are named after the constellation they are in, and a meteor shower is named after the constellation its meteors seem to come from. The Orionids appear to come from Orion.

**What the 3D view shows.** The view draws each figure as lines joining its stars, and each end of a line sits at that star's real place in space, not on a painted dome. Close to the Sun, out as far as the Oort cloud, the figures look just as they do from the Earth, with their names beside them. A few light years further out they begin to change. A star that is close to us shifts a long way against the rest, and a distant one hardly moves, so every figure is pulled out of shape in its own way. Further off still the lines are faded out, because by then they are only a tangle pointing back at the Sun.

A few lines are missing. Nine stars the figures use have no usable distance in the catalogue, so they have no place in space to draw to, and the lines that end on them are left out. Camelopardalis, Cepheus and Sagittarius are the figures that lose most.

The lines themselves are a convention, taken from one modern set of figures that is credited on the [sources page](/sources#beyond). The official constellations are areas of sky, not line drawings.

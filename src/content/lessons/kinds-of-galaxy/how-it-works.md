**How we know there are other galaxies.** Until the 1920s, many astronomers thought the faint spiral smudges seen through telescopes were clouds of gas inside the Milky Way. In 1923 Edwin Hubble found a star in the Andromeda smudge of a kind whose true brightness is known from the way it pulses. It looked far too faint to belong to our galaxy. Andromeda had to be a separate galaxy at an enormous distance, now measured as 2.5 million light years. The known universe grew many thousands of times larger in one result.

**The main kinds.**

| Kind | Shape | Stars | Gas and new stars | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spiral | A flat, turning disc with arms and a central bulge, often with a bar | Old stars in the bulge, young in the arms | Plenty. Still making stars | Andromeda, the Milky Way |
| Elliptical | Smooth ball or egg, no disc | Almost all old | Very little. Star making has nearly stopped | M87, in the Virgo cluster |
| Lenticular | A disc and bulge without arms | Mostly old | Little | The Spindle Galaxy, NGC 5866 |
| Irregular | No regular shape | Mixed, often young | Often a great deal | The Magellanic Clouds |

Hubble arranged these in a diagram shaped like a tuning fork, with ellipticals on the handle and spirals on the two prongs. He did not mean it as a life story, and it is not one. Galaxies do not turn from one kind into the next along the fork.

**Sizes.** The range is enormous. The smallest dwarf galaxies have a few thousand stars and are fainter than some single stars. Dwarfs are by far the commonest galaxies, though the hardest to see. The largest ellipticals hold more than a trillion stars. The Milky Way is on the large side.

**Why the shapes differ.** Much of the answer is collisions. A cloud of gas that settles gently keeps its spin and flattens into a disc, which is how spirals form. When two large galaxies collide, their stars almost never hit one another, because the gaps between stars are so wide. But their gravity scrambles the neat orbits into random ones, and the gas is used up or thrown out in a burst of star making. What is left is a smooth elliptical with little gas. This is why the giant ellipticals are found in the middle of crowded clusters, where collisions are most frequent. In the early universe, seen by looking far away, galaxies are smaller, clumpier and more often colliding.

Nearly every large galaxy has a very massive black hole at its centre. When gas pours into one, the surroundings of the black hole can outshine all the galaxy's stars together. Such an object is called a quasar.

**How galaxies gather.**

| Level | What it is | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Group | A few dozen galaxies. Ours, the Local Group, has more than 50 known members, most of them dwarfs | About 10 million light years |
| Cluster | Hundreds to thousands of galaxies bound by gravity. The nearest large one is in Virgo, about 54 million light years away | 10 to 30 million light years |
| Supercluster | A loose gathering of groups and clusters | Hundreds of millions of light years |
| The cosmic web | Threads and sheets of galaxies round nearly empty spaces called voids | Fills the universe |

Clusters are the largest things held together by gravity. Anything bigger is being pulled apart by the expansion of the universe, which is the subject of the next lesson.

**How many.** Counting galaxies in the deepest pictures ever taken and scaling up to the whole sky gives at least a few hundred billion in the part of the universe we can see. Some estimates that include faint dwarfs too small to detect yet are several times higher.

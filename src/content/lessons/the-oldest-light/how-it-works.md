**Predicted, then forgotten.** In the late 1940s George Gamow, Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman worked out that if the universe began hot, a glow should be left over, and they put its temperature today at no more than 5 degrees above absolute zero. Nobody went looking. Penzias and Wilson found it by accident, and shared the Nobel Prize in 1978.

**Three spacecraft.**

| Spacecraft | Launched | What it showed |
|---|---|---|
| COBE (NASA) | 1989 | The glow is at 2.73 degrees, and it has patches that differ by about 1 part in 100,000 |
| WMAP (NASA) | 2001 | A sharp map of the patches over the whole sky, and from it the age of the universe |
| Planck (ESA) | 2009 | A sharper map still, able to tell apart differences of a few millionths of a degree |

**What the map gives.** From nine years of WMAP's measurements:

| | |
|---|---|
| Age of the universe | 13.77 billion years, to within half a per cent |
| Ordinary atoms | About 5 per cent |
| Dark matter | About 25 per cent |
| Dark energy | About 70 per cent |

The later Planck spacecraft, with a sharper map, gave 13.8 billion years, which is the figure used elsewhere in these lessons. The two agree to within their margins.

**How a pattern of spots can give an age.** Before the fog cleared, the hot gas rang like a bell. Gravity pulled it into the denser patches and the pressure of the light pushed it back out, over and over. When the fog cleared, the ringing stopped where it was and left spots of a favourite size. How big that size looks on the sky depends on how far the light has come and on what the universe is made of. Measure the spots, and those numbers follow.

**Why it marks the edge.** The fog was opaque. No light from before it cleared can reach us, however good the telescope. To see further back takes a different messenger. See [Messengers other than light](/learn/messengers-other-than-light).

**The map's colours.** The map shows microwaves, which have no colour. Red was chosen for the slightly warmer patches and blue for the slightly cooler. The glow of our own galaxy, which lies across the middle of the sky, has been taken out.

**How the glow was found.** In 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were testing a radio antenna at wavelengths of about 7 centimetres and could not get rid of a faint hiss. It came from every direction, day and night, in every season. They checked the wiring. They cleared out a pair of pigeons that were nesting in the horn and cleaned up after them. The hiss stayed. Meanwhile, 60 km away at Princeton, a group led by Robert Dicke was building an instrument to look for exactly this glow. A telephone call between the two groups settled what the hiss was. Penzias and Wilson put its temperature at 3.5 degrees above absolute zero, and their published note says only that there is an unexplained signal.

**The numbers.** The fog cleared about 380,000 years after the start, when the gas had cooled to about 3,000 degrees above absolute zero, and it then glowed orange-white, much as the surface of a cool star does. Today the glow is at 2.725 degrees. The ratio of the two, about 1,100, is how much the universe has stretched since, and the light has been stretched with it: its peak has moved from about one thousandth of a millimetre, just beyond red, to about one millimetre, a microwave (sums from the two temperatures).

It is still everywhere. Every cubic centimetre of space, including the room you are in, holds about 411 particles of this light.

**The most exact curve in nature.** A body that is the same temperature all through gives out light with one particular spread of wavelengths. In 1990 the COBE spacecraft measured the glow's spread, and it matched that curve so closely that on the published graph the margins of error are thinner than the line. Nothing made in a laboratory has matched it better. That is very hard to explain unless the whole universe was once a single hot, even gas. John Mather and George Smoot shared the Nobel Prize in 2006 for COBE's results.

**Our own motion shows up.** One half of the sky is very slightly warmer than the other, by about 0.003 of a degree. This is not a feature of the early universe. It is a Doppler shift: the Sun is moving at about 370 km a second relative to the glow, and the light ahead is squeezed a little while the light behind is stretched (370 divided by the speed of light, times 2.725 degrees, gives the size). The glow works as a fixed background against which the motion of our whole Galaxy can be measured.

**What the size of the spots says.** The commonest spots are about one degree across, twice the width of the full Moon. The true size of those patches is known from the physics of the ringing gas. How big they look from here depends on the shape of space, which could act like a magnifying or a shrinking lens. They look the size they should if space is flat, meaning that parallel lines stay parallel however far they go.

**A claim that did not last.** The glow may hold one more message. If the universe went through a burst of violent growth in its first instant, ripples in space from that moment should have left a faint twisting pattern in the way the light is lined up. In March 2014 a team working with a telescope at the South Pole, called BICEP2, announced that they had found it. Within a year, measurements from Planck showed that most or all of the signal was made by dust in our own Galaxy, which gives out light with the same kind of twist. The search goes on with more sensitive instruments, and with far more care over dust.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The glow comes from the place where the Big Bang happened.* There is no such place. The glow comes equally from every direction, because the hot gas filled all of space.
- *It is the light of the Big Bang itself.* It was released about 380,000 years afterwards. Nothing earlier can be seen in light.
- *The cold spots are empty and the warm spots are full.* The differences are a few parts in 100,000. Those faint patches were the seeds that gravity later grew into galaxies and clusters.

**Three different distances.** When something is very far away, "how far?" has more than one answer.

| The question | For the oldest light |
|---|---|
| How long was the light on its way? | 13.8 billion years |
| How far off was its source when the light left? | About 42 million light years |
| How far off is that source now? | About 46 billion light years |

The third is the one meant when the observable universe is said to be 46 billion light years in radius. It is about 3.3 times the figure most people expect, and about 1,100 times the distance at the start (both are sums).

**Does that break the speed of light?** No. Nothing travels through space faster than light. But space itself can stretch, and when there is a great deal of space between two galaxies, the gap between them can grow faster than light could cross it. Tamara Davis and Charles Lineweaver set this out in 2003 and showed that many of the galaxies already in our pictures are, and always have been, moving away faster than light in this sense.

**A horizon that closes in.** The expansion is speeding up, and that sets a second limit. Light leaving a galaxy today will never reach us if the galaxy is now more than about 16 billion light years away. We go on seeing such galaxies by the old light already on its way, but nothing that happens there from now on will ever be seen from here.

**What "flat" means.** On a flat sheet the angles of a triangle add up to 180 degrees. On a ball they add up to more. Space can be tested in the same way, using the sizes of the patches in the oldest light as the far side of an enormous triangle. NASA's WMAP spacecraft found space flat to within 0.4 per cent.

**A scale model.** Shrink the observable universe, 93 billion light years across, until it is the size of the Earth. The Milky Way, 100,000 light years across, is then about 14 metres wide, the length of a lorry. The whole Solar System out to Neptune is about a ten-thousandth of a millimetre across, the size of a virus (sums). There would be hundreds of billions of those 14-metre galaxies scattered through the globe.

**Where faster than light begins.** The further off a galaxy is, the faster the space between us and it is growing. Taking the measured rate of expansion as 70 km a second for every megaparsec, the gap grows at the speed of light for a galaxy 14 billion light years away (a sum). Every galaxy beyond that is being carried away faster than light, and has been since its light set out. We see them all the same, because their light started when they were closer and has been making slow headway ever since across space that was stretching under it.

**Two edges, and they are different.** The edge of sight, at 46 billion light years, is set by the past: it is how far the oldest light has come. It grows a little every year as light from further away arrives. The other edge, at about 16 billion light years, is set by the future: it marks the galaxies whose light, leaving now, can ever get here. The second edge is the one that matters for the far future. As the expansion speeds up, galaxy after galaxy passes beyond it. Their old light keeps arriving, redder and fainter, until it can no longer be detected.

**How sure is the 46?** The figure is not measured with a ruler. It is worked out from the history of the expansion, which depends on how much matter and dark energy there is, and those come from the oldest light. There is a known disagreement of a few per cent between the rate of expansion found that way and the rate found from nearby galaxies. Until that is settled, the size carries about the same uncertainty.

**How many galaxies?** A study in 2016 estimated two trillion. Most of those were not seen. They were small, faint galaxies of the early universe, added in by calculation, and many have since merged into larger ones. In 2021 the New Horizons spacecraft, far out beyond Pluto where the sky is darkest, measured how much light all the galaxies together give out. It found that this light is far less than two trillion galaxies would give, if they were as bright as that study assumed. So either there are fewer galaxies, or the unseen ones are fainter. The count of galaxies bright enough to photograph is a few hundred billion, and how many faint ones there are beyond that is an open question.

**A view that will not last.** Because of the second edge, the sky is slowly emptying. In about a hundred billion years every galaxy outside our own small group will have faded from view, and so will the oldest light, stretched too far to detect. Astronomers living then would see one island of stars in an empty dark, and would have no way of finding out that the universe is expanding or that it had a beginning. We happen to live at a time when the evidence can still be seen.

**Looking for an edge that wraps round.** If space curved back on itself within the part we can see, light could reach us from the same region by two different routes, and the same patterns would turn up in two places in the map of the oldest light. Researchers have searched the maps for matching circles and found none. So if the universe is finite, the whole is larger than the part we see.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The universe is 13.8 billion light years in radius.* That would be true only if space had stood still while the light travelled.
- *The universe is expanding into something.* Nothing outside is needed. Distances inside it grow.
- *We are at the centre.* Everyone, anywhere, sees themselves at the centre of their own visible ball.
- *The observable universe is the universe.* It is the part whose light has had time to arrive.

**What cannot be said.** Whether the universe is infinite is not something any measurement can settle, because no measurement can reach past the edge of sight. Science can say how big the visible part is, and that the rest is at least very much bigger. Beyond that, it is honest to say nobody knows.

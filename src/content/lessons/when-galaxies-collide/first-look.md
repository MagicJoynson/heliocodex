Galaxies do not stay apart for ever. Their gravity pulls them towards their neighbours, and sometimes two of them meet.

It is not a crash like two cars. A galaxy is mostly empty space, and its stars are so far apart that the two galaxies pass through each other with hardly any stars touching. What does change is the shape. Each galaxy pulls on the other, and neat spirals are bent and stretched, with long streamers of stars thrown out to the sides.

The clouds of gas inside the galaxies are a different matter. They are big enough to run into each other, and when they are squeezed, huge numbers of new stars are born.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/antennae-galaxies-hubble.jpg" alt="Two tangled galaxies, with dark dust lanes, pink glowing clouds and bright blue clumps of stars" width="1280" height="1271" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The Antennae, two galaxies 65 million light years away that are part way through joining, by the Hubble Space Telescope. The pink clouds are glowing gas and the blue clumps are huge numbers of newly made stars. Credit: ESA/Hubble and NASA, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

In the end the two can settle into one bigger galaxy. Our own galaxy grew this way. About 10 billion years ago the Milky Way swallowed a smaller galaxy, and stars from it are still circling among ours today.

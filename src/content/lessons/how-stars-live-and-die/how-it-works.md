Nobody has watched a star live out its life. A human lifetime is far too short. What we have instead is millions of stars at every age, like a photograph of a crowd, and the physics to put them in order. Most of this lesson is that reasoning, tested against what telescopes see.

**Birth.** Stars form inside cold clouds of gas and dust called nebulae. Where a clump of the cloud is dense enough, its own gravity makes it fall inwards. The falling gas heats up and becomes a protostar, hidden in dust and glowing only with the heat of its collapse. Spare material settles into a spinning disc around it, where planets may form. After some tens of millions of years, for a star like the Sun, the core reaches about 10 million degrees and fusion begins. Stars are rarely born alone: one cloud makes hundreds or thousands together. The Orion Nebula, about 1,350 light years away and just visible without a telescope, is a place where this is happening now.

**The long middle.** A star then spends about nine tenths of its life on the main sequence, turning hydrogen into helium in its core. The heavier the star, the shorter this lasts: about 10 billion years for the Sun, a few tens of millions for a star ten times heavier.

**Giant.** When the hydrogen in the core is used up, the core shrinks and heats, and hydrogen goes on burning in a shell around it. The extra heat makes the outer layers swell to a hundred times their size or more, and cool to a red glow. The star is now a red giant. In about 5 billion years the Sun will do this, and will probably swallow Mercury and Venus. Whether it reaches the Earth is not settled. In the core, helium starts fusing into carbon and oxygen.

From here the road divides.

| | A star like the Sun | A star of more than about 8 Suns |
|---|---|---|
| Fuses | Hydrogen, then helium | On through carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon, as far as iron |
| The end | Outer layers drift away as a glowing shell of gas | The core collapses in under a second and the star explodes: a supernova |
| What is left | A white dwarf | A neutron star or a black hole |

**The quiet end.** A star like the Sun cannot squeeze its core hard enough to burn carbon. Its outer layers drift off into space, lit for a few thousand years by the bare core. That core is a white dwarf: about half the Sun's mass packed into a ball the size of the Earth. A teaspoon of it would weigh several tonnes. It makes no new energy and simply cools.

**The violent end.** A heavy star keeps finding a new fuel, each burning faster than the last, until its core is iron. Fusing iron gives no energy back. With nothing left to hold it up, the core falls in on itself, and the rest of the star is thrown outwards. For a few weeks a single supernova can outshine a whole galaxy of stars.

**A worked example: why heavy stars die young.** A heavier star has more fuel, but it burns it far faster. Roughly, doubling the mass of a star makes it give out about eleven times as much light. Its lifetime is fuel divided by output, so each doubling of the mass cuts the lifetime to about a sixth.

Carry that rule up to ten Suns and the star lasts about 300 times less long than the Sun: 30 million years. A star of a tenth of the Sun's mass lasts about 300 times longer: some 3 million million years, which is more than 200 times the present age of the universe. So no small red star has ever died of old age. Every one that was ever made is still shining.

**How anyone knows the order.** The best evidence comes from star clusters. All the stars in a cluster were born together from one cloud, so they are the same age and differ only in mass. In a young cluster even the heavy blue stars are still on the main sequence. In an older one the heaviest have gone and the top of the main sequence is missing. The older the cluster, the lower the point at which the main sequence stops. That is the theory's prediction, and it is what telescopes see in cluster after cluster. The oldest clusters come out at 12 to 13 billion years, a little less than the age of the universe, as they should.

**A death that was watched.** On 23 February 1987 a star blew up in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy 168,000 light years away. It was the nearest supernova since the telescope was invented. Two to three hours before the light arrived, detectors deep underground in Japan, the United States and the Soviet Union recorded a burst of neutrinos, 25 in all. That is what the theory of a collapsing core had predicted: the neutrinos get out at once and the light has to wait for the blast to reach the surface. Old photographs showed which star had gone. It was a blue supergiant of about 20 Suns, and it is no longer there.

**Stars that never start, and a limit at the end.** A ball of gas lighter than about a twelfth of the Sun never gets hot enough in the middle to fuse hydrogen. It glows dull red from the heat of its collapse and slowly fades. These are brown dwarfs. At the other end of life there is a limit too. A white dwarf cannot be heavier than about 1.4 Suns. Above that, the pressure that holds it up fails, and it collapses or explodes. Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar's work on this limit won a Nobel Prize in 1983.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The Sun will end as a supernova.* It is far too light. It will become a red giant and then a white dwarf.
- *Bigger stars last longer because they have more fuel.* They have more, and they waste it. The biggest last the shortest time.
- *A red giant is a star that was born red.* It is an old star that has swollen. The small red stars are a different thing: dim, steady and extremely long-lived.

**Why it matters to us.** The universe began with hydrogen, helium and almost nothing else. Nearly every heavier element was made later inside stars, or when they died, and scattered into the clouds that form new ones. The oxygen you breathe, the calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were all made that way, before the Sun existed.

Nobody has drilled into another planet, so how is it known what they are made of? Mostly from one number: density, which is mass divided by volume. The mass comes from timing a planet's moons or passing spacecraft, and the volume from its size.

| Planet | Mass (Earths) | Width (Earths) | Density (water = 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury | 0.055 | 0.38 | 5.43 |
| Venus | 0.815 | 0.95 | 5.24 |
| Earth | 1 | 1 | 5.51 |
| Mars | 0.107 | 0.53 | 3.93 |
| Jupiter | 318 | 10.97 | 1.33 |
| Saturn | 95.2 | 9.14 | 0.69 |
| Uranus | 14.5 | 3.98 | 1.27 |
| Neptune | 17.1 | 3.86 | 1.64 |

Rock is about 3 times as dense as water and iron about 8. The four inner planets sit between the two, so they must be rock around an iron core. Jupiter and Saturn are far too light for that, even though their own weight squeezes them hard, and only hydrogen and helium fit. Saturn is less dense than water. Uranus and Neptune are smaller than Saturn yet denser, so they must hold something heavier than gas: mostly water, ammonia and methane, with hydrogen and helium making up only the outer tenth or so of their mass.

**Why the order.** The planets grew in a disc of gas and dust round the young Sun. Close in it was hot, and only rock and metal could form solid grains. Those are rare materials, so the inner planets had little to build with. Beyond a certain distance, called the frost line, water froze as well. Ice was plentiful, and the solid cores that grew there reached perhaps ten times the mass of the Earth, heavy enough to pull in the hydrogen and helium around them. Jupiter and Saturn took a great deal. Uranus and Neptune, growing more slowly further out, got much less before the gas was blown away.

**Jupiter is in a class of its own.** It has more than twice the mass of all the other planets put together. Deep inside, the pressure turns hydrogen into a liquid that conducts electricity like a metal, and that is the source of its vast magnetic field.

**Weighing Jupiter with a moon.** The table gives Jupiter 318 times the mass of the Earth. Here is where a number like that comes from. Jupiter's moon Io circles it 421,800 km out and takes 1.769 days for each lap. The stronger the pull at the centre, the faster a moon at a given distance has to go, so those two measurements fix the planet's mass. Put into Newton's law of gravity they give 1.90 thousand million million million million kilograms, which is 318 Earths. Galileo could have done the sum in 1610 if the law had been known. Planets with no moon, Mercury and Venus, had to wait for spacecraft to fly past before they could be weighed well.

**Then the density.** Saturn has 95.2 times the mass of the Earth. It is 9.14 times as wide, and 9.14 × 9.14 × 9.14 is about 764, so it has 764 times the volume. Divide one by the other and Saturn's stuff is, on average, an eighth as dense as the Earth's: 5.51 ÷ 8 is 0.69 times the density of water. (This treats both as perfect balls, which is near enough.) People say Saturn would float in a bath big enough to hold it. The density is right, but Saturn is not a solid object that could bob about. It is a deep fluid, thin at the top and very dense at the centre, and 0.69 is only the average.

**Squeezing matters.** Density has to be read with care, because a big planet crushes its own insides. The Earth is denser than Mercury in the table, but only because it is heavier and more squeezed. Allow for that and Mercury turns out to be made of denser stuff, with an iron core that takes up most of the planet.

**Other ways to see inside.**

- *Gravity, mapped closely.* A spacecraft in orbit speeds up and slows down very slightly as it passes over denser and lighter parts of a planet. Tracking its radio signal shows how the mass is arranged inside. That is how Juno found Jupiter's smeared-out core.
- *Magnetism.* A magnetic field needs something inside that conducts electricity and is moving. The Earth's comes from liquid iron and Jupiter's from liquid hydrogen. Uranus and Neptune have lopsided fields, tilted far from their axes, which suggests they are made in a shell of conducting fluid and not at the centre.
- *Heat.* Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune each give out more heat than they take in from the Sun. They are still cooling and settling from the time they formed. Uranus gives out almost none, and nobody knows why.
- *Spin and shape.* Jupiter turns once in under 10 hours and Saturn in under 11, and both bulge at the equator. How much a spinning planet bulges depends on how its mass is spread inside.

**Seen up close only once.** Much of this is thin for the two ice giants. A single spacecraft, Voyager 2, has been to them: it flew past Uranus in January 1986 and Neptune in August 1989, and did not stop. Nearly everything known about their insides comes from those two days and from telescopes.

**Is three the right number of kinds?** The three groups describe the Sun's planets well. They do not cover everything. Pluto and the other dwarf planets beyond Neptune are a fourth kind, small and made of ice and rock. And the planets found round other stars include many that fit none of our groups, such as worlds a few times the Earth's size that may be all ocean, or all rock, or wrapped in thick gas. The groups are a first sorting, not a law.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A gas giant is gas all the way down.* Only the outer part is. Most of Jupiter's hydrogen is squeezed into a liquid, and deep down into a form that behaves like a metal.
- *The rocky planets are solid all through.* The Earth's outer core is liquid iron, and the rock of its mantle flows slowly.
- *The frost line is a place you could point to today.* It was a feature of the disc the planets formed from, about 4.6 billion years ago. The disc has long gone. The order of the planets is what is left of it.

Some of this is less tidy than it sounds. The Juno spacecraft found that Jupiter's core is not a neat ball but is smeared out through much of the planet. And among the thousands of planets found round other stars, the commonest size lies between the Earth and Neptune, a kind our own Solar System does not have at all.

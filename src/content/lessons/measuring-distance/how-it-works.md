Each unit is tied to something that can be measured.

**The astronomical unit** began as the average distance between the Earth and the Sun. Since 2012 it has been a fixed number, exactly 149,597,870.7 km, so that it no longer shifts each time the Earth's orbit is measured a little better.

**The light year** is the distance light covers in a year of 365.25 days at 299,792.458 km every second. That comes to 9.461 million million km, or 63,241 au.

**Light time** is the same idea on a smaller scale, and it is the natural unit for anyone running a spacecraft. The Moon is 1.3 light seconds away. The Sun is 8 minutes 19 seconds away. Voyager 1, the most distant thing people have made, is about one light day away, so a command sent today is answered the day after tomorrow.

**The parsec** is the one professional astronomers use, because it falls straight out of how star distances are measured. As the Earth goes round the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift a little against the far ones. Half of the total shift is called the star's parallax. A star whose parallax is one arcsecond is, by definition, one parsec away, and the rule is as simple as a rule can be:

distance in parsecs = 1 ÷ parallax in arcseconds

Proxima Centauri has a parallax of 0.768 arcseconds, so it is 1 ÷ 0.768 = 1.30 parsecs away. One parsec is 3.26 light years, which gives 4.25 light years.

These angles are tiny. One arcsecond is the width of a one pound coin nearly 5 km away, and no star shifts by even that much. The Gaia spacecraft measured shifts tens of thousands of times smaller, for well over a billion stars. Beyond its reach, distances have to be worked out in less direct ways, each one checked against the one before. Astronomers call that chain the distance ladder, and parallax is its bottom rung.

**Try parallax yourself.** Hold a thumb up at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the far wall. Bring the thumb closer and it jumps further. Your two eyes are about 6.5 cm apart, and your brain uses the size of that jump to judge how far away things are. A star's parallax is the same trick with a much longer distance between the eyes: the two sides of the Earth's orbit, 300 million km apart.

Even with that, the jump is very small, because the stars are so far away compared with the width of the orbit. For Proxima Centauri the baseline is 300 million km and the distance is 40 million million km. That is like judging the distance of something 8.7 km away using two eyes 6.5 cm apart (a sum from the figures in this lesson).

**The first star measured.** For two centuries after telescopes were invented, astronomers looked for the shift and failed, and some took that as a sign that the Earth did not move at all. The shift was there, but too small for their instruments. In 1838 Friedrich Bessel, in Königsberg, at last measured it for a faint star called 61 Cygni. He got a parallax of 0.314 arcseconds, which puts the star about 10.4 light years away. The modern figure is 11.4. It was the first time anyone knew the distance to any star but the Sun, and it showed how empty space is: the star was about 660,000 times further away than the Sun.

**Getting better at it.** From the ground, the air smears a star's image and limits how fine an angle can be measured. By the 1980s, after 150 years of work, good distances were known for only a few thousand stars. Two spacecraft changed that.

| Who | When | Stars with measured parallax |
|---|---|---|
| Astronomers on the ground | 1838 to the 1980s | A few thousand |
| Hipparcos (ESA) | 1989 to 1993 | 118,218 |
| Gaia (ESA) | 2013 to 2025, third data release in 2022 | About 1.46 billion |

Even Gaia's huge count is no more than about one in a hundred of the stars in our galaxy.

**How the au was pinned down.** Kepler's laws gave the shape of the Solar System long before anyone knew its size. Astronomers in the 1600s knew that Jupiter was 5.2 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is, but not how many kilometres that was. Measure any one distance truly and all the rest follow. In the 1700s expeditions were sent round the world to time Venus crossing the face of the Sun from different places, and James Cook's first voyage to the Pacific was one of them. Their answers were within a few per cent of the truth. In 1961 radar settled it: a radio pulse was bounced off Venus and timed, which gave the distance far more exactly than any crossing could.

**The best-measured distance of all.** The distance to the Moon is known better than any other in space. Laser flashes timed off the mirrors left there give it to within millimetres, out of 384,400 km. That is fine enough to show the Moon moving away from the Earth by 3.8 cm a year, about the rate at which fingernails grow. The same measurements are among the sharpest tests there are of the law of gravity.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A parsec is a measure of time.* It is a distance, 3.26 light years. The name is short for "parallax of one arcsecond".
- *Light years are too vague for real work.* The light year is exact, because the speed of light is fixed by definition and so is the length of the year used. Astronomers prefer parsecs only because parallax gives them directly.
- *The distances to stars are known as well as distances on Earth.* For near stars they are very good, to a small fraction of one per cent. Further out the uncertainty grows, and for some famous bright stars, such as Deneb and Betelgeuse, different measurements still disagree by hundreds of light years.

**Bigger rulers.** For the galaxy and beyond, astronomers put the usual prefixes on the parsec. A kiloparsec is a thousand parsecs, or 3,260 light years: the Sun is about 8 kiloparsecs from the centre of the galaxy. A megaparsec is a million parsecs, or 3.26 million light years: the Andromeda Galaxy is about 0.77 megaparsecs away. The expansion of the universe is quoted in kilometres a second for every megaparsec.

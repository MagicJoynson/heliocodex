You would not give the distance from Manchester to Sydney in millimetres. Space has the same problem, only worse, so astronomers keep a few rulers of different lengths.

**Kilometres** work for single worlds and their moons. The Moon is 384,400 km away.

**Astronomical units**, written au, work inside the Solar System. One au is the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 150 million km. Jupiter is 5 au from the Sun and Neptune is 30. Those numbers are easy to picture, which is the point of the unit.

**Light years** work for the stars. A light year is how far light goes in a year, about 9.5 million million km. The nearest star beyond the Sun is 4.25 light years away.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/proxima-centauri-hubble.jpg" alt="A bright white star with four long spikes of light, on a black sky with a scattering of faint stars." width="1100" height="1079" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Proxima Centauri, the nearest star beyond the Sun, by the Hubble Space Telescope. Its light takes four and a quarter years to arrive. The spikes are made inside the telescope. Credit: ESA/Hubble and NASA, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

A light year is a distance, but it carries a bonus. If a star is 4.25 light years away, the light you see tonight left it 4.25 years ago.

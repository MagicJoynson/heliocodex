You would not give the distance from Manchester to Sydney in millimetres. Space has the same problem, only worse, so astronomers keep a few rulers of different lengths.

**Kilometres** work for single worlds and their moons. The Moon is 384,400 km away.

**Astronomical units**, written au, work inside the Solar System. One au is the distance from the Earth to the Sun, about 150 million km. Jupiter is 5 au from the Sun and Neptune is 30. Those numbers are easy to picture, which is the point of the unit.

**Light years** work for the stars. A light year is how far light goes in a year, about 9.5 million million km. The nearest star beyond the Sun is 4.25 light years away.

A light year is a distance, but it carries a bonus. If a star is 4.25 light years away, the light you see tonight left it 4.25 years ago.

**Light time** is the same idea for shorter trips. Light from the Moon takes 1.3 seconds to get here, and light from the Sun takes 8 minutes 19 seconds. A radio signal travels at the speed of light too, which matters to anyone driving a rover on Mars. Mars is between 55 million and 400 million km from the Earth, depending on where the two planets are in their orbits, so a signal takes between 3 and 22 minutes each way (a sum from NASA's figures). Nobody can steer a rover with a joystick. The drivers send a list of moves for the whole day and find out later how it went.

**A common mistake.** Because it has "year" in it, a light year sounds like a length of time. It is a length of road. Saying a star is "four light years away" is like saying a town is "two hours' drive away": a distance, named after how long something takes to cover it.

**How big is a light year, really?** A jet airliner cruises at about 900 km/h. Flying without a stop, it would take about 1.2 million years to cover one light year. The fastest spacecraft to leave the Solar System, Voyager 1, moves at 17 km every second, and would need about 17,600 years. Light does it in one. That is why the stars are counted in light years: in any smaller unit the numbers stop meaning anything.

**Why not use one unit for everything?** You could. The Moon is 0.00000004 light years away, and the nearest star is 40,000,000,000,000 km away. Both are correct and neither is any use. A good unit is one that gives numbers you can hold in your head: the Moon in thousands of kilometres, the planets in au, the stars in light years.

**How do we know the distances at all?** Nobody has stretched a tape to the Moon, but something close to it has been done. Astronauts left mirrors there, and a laser flash sent from the Earth comes back about two and a half seconds later. Half that time, multiplied by the speed of light, is the distance. For the planets, radio waves are bounced off them in the same way. For the stars there is nothing to bounce off, and the method is different: it uses the Earth's own movement round the Sun, and it is set out in the next level of this lesson.

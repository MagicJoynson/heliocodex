**How much is there.** Of the material between the stars, about 99 parts in 100 are gas and about 1 is dust. All of it together comes to roughly 15 per cent of the mass of the galaxy's stars, about 10 billion times the mass of the Sun.

**The gas comes in very different states.**

| Kind of gas | Temperature | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Cold clouds of molecules | About 10 degrees above absolute zero, −263 °C | The thickest, darkest clouds, where stars form |
| Ordinary hydrogen gas | From about −170 °C to several thousand degrees | Spread through the disc of the galaxy |
| Glowing gas round hot stars | About 10,000 degrees | Bright nebulae such as the one in Orion |
| Very hot, very thin gas | A million degrees or more | Bubbles blown by exploding stars |

**How little dust there is.** A cubic kilometre of space holds only a few hundred to a few thousand grains of dust, and each grain is less than a ten-thousandth of a millimetre across. Each has a core of rock or soot with a coat of ice. It blocks the view only because the distances are so long: a line of sight thousands of light years deep passes enough grains to hide what lies behind.

**Why dust reddens.** The grains are about the size of the waves of visible light, and they scatter blue light more than red. A star seen through dust looks both fainter and redder than it is. Infrared waves are longer than the grains are wide and mostly pass by. That is why infrared telescopes can see into dark clouds and through to the centre of the galaxy.

**Barnard 68 in numbers.** The cloud is about 12,500 times as wide as the distance from the Earth to the Sun, and its temperature is 16 degrees above absolute zero, −257 °C. It is the kind of cloud that may one day fall in on itself and make a star.

**The 21 centimetre signal.** A hydrogen atom is one proton with one electron. Each behaves like a tiny magnet, and the two can point the same way or opposite ways. Opposite has slightly less energy. When an atom drops from one to the other it gives out a radio wave 21 centimetres long. A Dutch student, Hendrik van de Hulst, worked out in 1944 that this should happen, and it was first picked up in 1951. Radio waves pass through dust, so this one signal lets astronomers map the gas right across the Milky Way.

**Molecules.** In the coldest clouds, atoms join into molecules. Hundreds of different kinds have been found in space by the radio waves they give out, the largest of them built of 60 or 70 carbon atoms.

**A worked example: how empty.** Spread evenly, the gas of the Galaxy would come to about one atom in each cubic centimetre. The air you are breathing has about 50 million million million (a sum from its density). Even the thickest clouds between the stars are a better vacuum than any pump on the Earth can make.

Yet the volumes are so vast that it adds up. A ball of average space two light years across holds about three times the mass of Jupiter in gas (a sum). Near the Sun the gas is thinner than average, about a tenth of an atom in each cubic centimetre, and a ball reaching from here to the nearest star holds about a fiftieth of the mass of the Sun (a sum). To make a star, gravity has to gather gas from a region many light years across.

**How anyone found it.** In 1904 the German astronomer Johannes Hartmann was studying a double star in Orion's Belt. As the two stars swung round each other, the lines in their spectrum shifted to and fro, as expected. One line of calcium stayed exactly where it was. It could not belong to either star. It was made by thin gas lying somewhere along the line of sight, between the star and the Earth. That stationary line was the first direct sign that the space between the stars is not empty.

The dust took longer. In 1930 Robert Trumpler compared the sizes of star clusters with their brightness and found that the far ones were always fainter than their distance could explain. Something was dimming them, and more so the further off they were. Before then, astronomers had taken the Galaxy to be much bigger than it is, because dimmed stars had been judged to be far away.

**A wall of dust.** Towards the centre of the Galaxy the dust is so thick that, of every million million particles of visible light setting out towards us, about one arrives. In infrared light about one in ten gets through. This is why the stars that circle the black hole at the centre were found with infrared cameras.

**We live in a bubble.** The Sun is at present inside a region about a thousand light years across where the gas is far thinner and hotter than average. It is called the Local Bubble, and it was blown by a series of exploding stars over the last ten to twenty million years. The Sun was not born in it. It wandered in a few million years ago, and will wander out again.

**The great recycling.** The gas between the stars is not a leftover. It is the middle of a cycle. Clouds collapse to make stars. Stars return much of their gas to space when they die, enriched with the heavier elements they have made. That gas mixes into new clouds, and the next stars form from richer material. The Sun formed about 9 billion years into this process, which is why it had enough rock and metal round it to make planets.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Space is a perfect vacuum.* It is a far better vacuum than any on the Earth, but it is not empty.
- *A dark nebula is a hole in the stars.* William Herschel thought so when he first saw one. It is a cloud in front of them.
- *The colours of nebulae in photographs are what you would see.* The eye sees the brightest ones as grey-green at best. The colours are real light, but too faint to trigger colour vision.

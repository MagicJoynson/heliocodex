**The observation.** The light of a galaxy, spread into a spectrum, carries dark lines at known wavelengths. In nearly every galaxy beyond our near neighbours, the lines sit at longer, redder wavelengths than they should. This is redshift. In the 1920s Georges Lemaître and Edwin Hubble showed that the redshift grows in step with distance: a galaxy twice as far away is receding twice as fast.

The rate is the Hubble constant. It is about 70 km/s for every megaparsec, which is about 21 km/s for every million light years. A galaxy 100 million light years away is receding at about 2,100 km/s.

**What is stretching.** The galaxies are not flying through space away from a point. The space between them is growing, and light crossing it is stretched along with it. Light that set out when the universe was half its present scale arrives with its wavelengths doubled.

Three things follow.

- **There is no centre and no edge to the expansion.** Seen from any galaxy, all the others recede in the same way.
- **Things held together do not expand.** Atoms, people, planets, the Solar System and whole galaxies are bound by forces far stronger than the stretching. Even our Local Group holds together: Andromeda is approaching us. Only the gaps between groups and clusters of galaxies grow.
- **Very distant galaxies recede faster than light.** This breaks no rule. Nothing can move through space faster than light, but the rule puts no limit on how fast space itself can grow.

**Running it backwards.** A universe that is expanding was once denser and hotter. The Big Bang theory is the account of how that hot, dense state expanded and cooled into what we see. It makes predictions, and three have been checked.

*The oldest light.* For its first 380,000 years the universe was so hot that atoms could not hold together, and light could not travel far through the glowing fog. When it cooled to about 3,000 K, atoms formed, the fog cleared, and the light was set free. That light is still arriving from every direction, stretched about 1,100 times into microwaves. It was predicted in 1948 and found by accident in 1964. Its temperature today is 2.725 K, which is about −270 °C.

*The first elements.* In its first few minutes the whole universe was hot enough for nuclear fusion. The theory says this should have left about three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium by mass, with a trace of lithium. The oldest gas clouds measured have that mix.

*A changing past.* Looking far away is looking back in time. Distant galaxies are smaller, younger and less orderly than nearby ones. The most distant confirmed so far, found with the Webb telescope, are seen as they were less than 300 million years after the beginning.

**The age.** The pattern of slightly warmer and cooler patches in the oldest light, mapped by the Planck spacecraft, gives an age of 13.8 billion years, to within about 1 per cent.

**How far we can see.** The oldest light has travelled for 13.8 billion years. The matter that sent it has been carried away by the expansion ever since, and is now about 46 billion light years from us. That distance, in every direction, is the limit of the observable universe. Nobody knows how much lies beyond it, or whether the whole is finite.

**What the theory does not say.** It does not say the universe exploded into empty space: there was no space outside it. It does not describe the very first instant, where known physics fails. And it does not say what, if anything, came before.

**An open problem.** The expansion rate can be measured in two ways. Stepping outwards through distances to galaxies gives about 73 km/s per megaparsec. Working it out from the oldest light gives about 67. Each measurement claims an accuracy of 1 or 2 per cent, so they should not differ this much. Either one of them hides a mistake that nobody has found, or something is missing from the theory.

**The Hubble tension.** The expansion rate today can be reached by two routes that share almost nothing, and they give different answers. This level sets out what each route measures, how far apart they are, and what has been tried.

**The quantity.** For galaxies that are not too far away, speed of recession $v$ and distance $d$ are tied by

$$
v = H_0\, d
$$

and $H_0$ is the Hubble constant, in km/s for every megaparsec. Its inverse is a time. Worked out here, $1/H_0$ is 14.0 billion years for $H_0 = 70$, 13.4 billion for 73 and 14.5 billion for 67.4. The true age of the universe is not simply $1/H_0$, because the expansion has not kept one speed, but the comparison shows what is at stake: a few units in $H_0$ is a billion years in the timescale.

**The direct route.** Measure distances to galaxies and set them against their speeds. This is the distance ladder: parallax fixes the true brightness of Cepheids, Cepheids fix it for Type Ia supernovae, and the supernovae reach far enough out for the expansion to dominate. The SH0ES team's 2022 result, from Cepheids in the host galaxies of 42 supernovae, is

$$
H_0 = 73.04 \pm 1.04\ \text{km/s/Mpc}
$$

**The route from the oldest light.** Before the universe turned clear, sound waves ran through the hot plasma. The distance such a wave could have covered by the time the light was set free is the sound horizon, $r_s$, and it left a preferred size for the warm and cool patches in the oldest light. What is measured is the angle that size covers on the sky:

$$
\theta_* = \frac{r_s}{D_M}
$$

where $D_M$ is the distance the light has crossed since. Planck measured this angle to 0.03 per cent: $100\,\theta_* = 1.0411 \pm 0.0003$, which is 0.60 degrees when worked out here. The standard model of cosmology gives $r_s$ from the physics of the plasma, so the angle gives $D_M$, and $D_M$ depends on how fast the universe has expanded since. Within the standard model the answer is

$$
H_0 = 67.4 \pm 0.5\ \text{km/s/Mpc}
$$

This is a prediction for today's rate from the state of the universe at 380,000 years. It is only as good as the model that carries it across 13.8 billion years.

**The size of the gap.** The two differ by 5.6 km/s/Mpc, which is 8 per cent of the lower figure, and about five times the combined uncertainty. The SH0ES paper puts it at 5 sigma. Its authors tried about 70 variations of their analysis and found none that closes the gap.

**Other measurements.** Several other methods have been brought in to break the tie.

| Method | Year | $H_0$ (km/s/Mpc) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cepheids and supernovae (SH0ES) | 2022 | 73.04 ± 1.04 |
| Time delays in six lensed quasars (H0LiCOW) | 2020 | 73.3, +1.7 −1.8 |
| Brightest red giants and supernovae (Freedman and others) | 2019 | 69.8 ± 0.8 ± 1.7 |
| Brightest red giants, Hubble and Webb together (Freedman and others) | 2024 | 70.39 ± 1.22 ± 1.33 ± 0.70 |
| One neutron star merger, by gravitational waves | 2017 | 70.0, +12.0 −8.0 |
| Galaxy clustering with the sound horizon (DESI) | 2024 | 68.52 ± 0.62 |
| The oldest light with the standard model (Planck) | 2018 | 67.4 ± 0.5 |

Where more than one uncertainty is given, the first is statistical and the second systematic. The third in the 2024 figure is the scatter among the supernovae. The pattern is not tidy. Methods that lean on the sound horizon give 67 to 69. Cepheids and lensed quasars give 73. The red giant method, which replaces Cepheids with a different kind of star but keeps the supernovae, lands in between, and its authors say their results need no new physics. The gravitational-wave measurement uses no ladder at all, but one event is too few to decide anything.

**Is it a mistake in the Cepheids?** The most often suggested fault was crowding: in a distant galaxy a Cepheid's light is blended with its neighbours', which could make it seem brighter and nearer. The Webb telescope has much sharper sight than Hubble in the infrared. In 2024 the SH0ES team reported Webb measurements of more than 1,000 Cepheids, found no significant difference from Hubble's, and ruled out crowding as the cause at 8.2 sigma.

**Is it new physics?** If both measurements are right, the model that joins them is incomplete. One much-studied idea is early dark energy: an extra ingredient that mattered briefly before the universe turned clear and then faded. It would have made $r_s$ smaller. With a smaller $r_s$ and the same measured angle, $D_M$ is smaller too, and the $H_0$ inferred from the oldest light rises towards the direct value. A 2021 review surveyed this and many other proposals and concluded that none makes a strong case for being far better than the rest.

**Where it stands.** Three possibilities remain open: an error in the ladder that nobody has found, an error in the model used to read the oldest light, or several small errors at once. More Webb data on all three kinds of star, and more neutron star mergers, are the measurements most likely to settle it.

**On this site.** The 3D view places galaxies using $H_0 = 70$, between the two camps. Had it used 73 every galaxy on the map would sit 4 per cent nearer, and with 67.4 it would sit 4 per cent further away. The shape of the web would be the same.

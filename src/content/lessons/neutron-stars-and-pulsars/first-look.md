When a very big star runs out of fuel, its middle caves in. A core heavier than the Sun is squeezed into a ball about 20 km across, the size of a city. The rest of the star is blown away in a huge explosion called a supernova.

What is left is a neutron star. It is the densest object known. A piece the size of a sugar cube would weigh about a billion tonnes, as much as a mountain.

Many neutron stars spin, and fast. They also send out two narrow beams of light and radio waves. As the star turns, the beams sweep round like the lamp of a lighthouse. If one of them crosses the Earth, we see a flash on every turn. A neutron star seen this way is called a pulsar.

The best known is in the Crab Nebula. Astronomers in China saw its star explode in the year 1054. The neutron star left behind still flashes 30 times every second.

If the crushed core is heavier still, nothing can hold it up, and it becomes a black hole instead.

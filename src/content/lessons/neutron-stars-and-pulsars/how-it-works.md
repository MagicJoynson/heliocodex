A neutron star is one of the three things a star can leave behind when it dies. Which one depends on how heavy the crushed core is.

| What is left | When | Size |
|---|---|---|
| White dwarf | A star like the Sun fades without exploding | About the size of the Earth |
| Neutron star | A massive star explodes and its core is between about 1 and 3 times the Sun's mass | About 20 km across |
| Black hole | The core is heavier than that | No surface at all |

**How one forms.** When a massive star runs out of fuel, its core collapses. The pressure is so great that every proton and electron is crushed together into a neutron. If the core holds between about 1 and 3 times the mass of the Sun, the newly made neutrons can resist being squeezed any further, and the collapse stops. A heavier core keeps going and becomes a black hole.

**How dense.** A sugar cube of neutron star would weigh about a trillion kilograms on the Earth. That is a billion tonnes.

**Pulsars.** Most neutron stars we know of were found as pulsars. A pulsar has a very strong magnetic field, which drives particles out along its two magnetic poles, and those particles make powerful beams of light. The magnetic poles are usually not lined up with the axis the star spins on, so the beams swing round as it turns. We see a pulse each time a beam crosses us, at intervals from thousandths of a second to a few seconds. The light is on all the time. Like a lighthouse, you only see it when it points at you.

**The Crab.** The Crab Nebula is the cloud left by the supernova of 1054, about 6,500 light years away and about 11 light years across. The pulsar at its centre turns 30 times a second. For a ball 20 km across, that means a point on its equator is moving at nearly 1,900 km every second (a sum).

**How they were found.** In 1967, at Cambridge, Jocelyn Bell and Antony Hewish picked up a radio source that pulsed at extremely regular intervals. Nothing like it was known. It turned out to be a spinning neutron star, and the discovery proved that such stars exist. Hewish shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1974 for his part in the discovery.

**Magnetars.** An ordinary neutron star has a magnetic field trillions of times stronger than the Earth's. A magnetar's is another thousand times stronger. The crust of a neutron star is locked to its magnetic field, so when the crust slips, the field is shaken too, and a magnetar can let go a vast burst of energy. In one burst lasting about a fifth of a second, the magnetar SGR 1806-20 gave out as much energy as the Sun does in a quarter of a million years.

**A clock that tested Einstein.** A pulsar keeps time very steadily, which makes it a measuring tool. In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor found a pulsar in orbit round another star of about the same mass, the two going round each other in less than eight hours. Einstein's theory of gravity says such a pair should lose energy by giving off gravitational waves, and so draw slowly closer. Timing the pulses showed exactly that: the orbit is getting shorter by about 75 millionths of a second every year. The measured change agrees with the calculated one, which the Nobel committee called an indirect proof that gravitational waves exist, long before any were detected directly. Hulse and Taylor were given the prize in 1993.

**A worked example: the gravity.** Take a neutron star of 1.4 Suns and 20 km across. Gravity at its surface is nearly two hundred thousand million times the Earth's. Drop something from a height of one metre and it hits the surface at about 1,900 km a second. To escape from the surface, a thing would have to leave at nearly two thirds of the speed of light (all three are sums). Only a black hole is harder to leave.

**Why they spin so fast.** A skater who pulls in her arms spins faster. The core of a big star is thousands of kilometres across and may turn once in a few minutes or hours. When it collapses to a ball some 20 km across, it keeps its spin and ends up turning many times a second. The magnetic field is squeezed in the same way, which is why it is so strong.

**More about the discovery.** The first pulsar ticked once every 1.337 seconds, so regularly that the Cambridge team half-jokingly labelled it LGM-1, for "little green men". Jocelyn Bell was the research student who built much of the aerial, noticed the faint smudge on hundreds of metres of paper chart and insisted that it was real. The Nobel Prize went to her supervisor and not to her, which many astronomers thought unjust. In 2018 she was awarded a prize of three million dollars for the discovery and gave all of it away to fund research students from groups that physics has tended to overlook.

**The fastest.** The quickest pulsar known turns 716 times a second. If it is 20 km across, its equator is moving at about 45,000 km a second, about a seventh of the speed of light (a sum). Pulsars that fast were not born that way. Each has, or had, a companion star, and gas falling from the companion has spun it up over millions of years.

**The best clocks in the sky.** The fastest pulsars keep time almost as well as the best atomic clocks. Astronomers time dozens of them, spread across the sky, for years on end. A gravitational wave passing through the Galaxy stretches and squeezes the space between us and each pulsar, so some pulses arrive a few billionths of a second early and others late, in a pattern across the sky. In 2023 several teams reported the first evidence for exactly that pattern: a low hum of gravitational waves, probably from pairs of giant black holes circling each other in distant galaxies.

**The first planets beyond the Sun.** The first planets found round another star were found round a pulsar, in 1992, three years before the famous one at 51 Pegasi. The pulses of a pulsar called PSR B1257+12 arrived early and late by a regular rhythm, as two planets a few times the mass of the Earth pulled it to and fro. Nobody expected planets beside the wreck of an exploded star, and how they got there is still not agreed.

**When two collide.** On 17 August 2017 detectors picked up the gravitational waves from two neutron stars spiralling together in a galaxy 130 million light years away. Telescopes found the flash within hours. The light showed newly made heavy elements, and collisions like this are now thought to be where much of the universe's gold and platinum comes from.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A pulsar pulses.* It shines steadily and spins. The pulses are the beam sweeping past.
- *A neutron star is a small black hole.* It has a solid surface, and light leaves it. Things can land on it.
- *It is made only of neutrons.* The outer crust is ordinary atomic nuclei, mostly iron, crushed into a solid far stronger than steel. The neutrons are underneath.

**Hard to see.** Many neutron stars are probably never noticed, because they simply do not give out enough radiation to detect. A handful have been found sitting quietly at the centre of the cloud left by their supernova, glowing in X-rays.

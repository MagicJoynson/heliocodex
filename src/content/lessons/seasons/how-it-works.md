The tilt does two things at once, and they add together.

**It changes how high the Sun gets.** Take Manchester, at latitude 53.5° north. At noon on the June solstice the Sun stands 60° above the horizon. At noon on the December solstice it reaches only 13°. Low sunlight is spread thin: a beam arriving at 13° covers nearly four times as much ground as the same beam arriving at 60°, so each square metre gets about a quarter of the warmth.

**It changes how long the Sun is up.** In Manchester the Sun is above the horizon for about 17 hours in late June and about 7½ hours in late December.

Distance hardly comes into it. The Earth's orbit is so close to a circle that its distance from the Sun changes by only 3% through the year, from 147.1 million km in early January to 152.1 million km in early July. Sunlight is about 7% stronger in January than in July, which takes a little of the edge off northern winters and adds a little to southern summers.

The tilt also draws four lines on the globe. The tropics, at 23.4° north and south, are the furthest places where the Sun can stand straight overhead. The polar circles, at 66.6°, are the edges of the regions that get at least one day of midnight Sun and one day with no sunrise.

The hottest and coldest weeks come a month or so after the solstices, because the ground and above all the sea take time to warm up and cool down.

Other planets show what tilt alone can do. Mars leans by 25.2°, much like the Earth, and has four seasons that each last about twice as long as ours. Venus is almost upright and has no seasons to speak of. Uranus lies on its side at 97.8°, so over its 84-year orbit each pole gets about 42 years of sunlight followed by 42 years of dark.

**Work out the noon Sun for anywhere.** The height of the Sun at noon on the solstices is easy to find: take your latitude away from 90, then add the tilt for midsummer or take it away for midwinter. For London, at 51.5° north, that is 90 − 51.5 + 23.4 = 62° in June and 90 − 51.5 − 23.4 = 15° in December. At the equinoxes it is just 90 minus the latitude, 38.5°. Put in a latitude of 23.4° and the midsummer answer is 90°, straight overhead, which is what makes that line the tropic. Put in 66.6° and the midwinter answer is zero: the Sun only touches the horizon at noon, which is what makes that line the polar circle.

**The first use of the idea.** About 240 BC Eratosthenes, the librarian at Alexandria in Egypt, heard that at noon on midsummer day the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene, far to the south. In Alexandria at the same moment it stood about 7.2° away from overhead. That is a fiftieth of a full circle, so the distance between the two towns had to be a fiftieth of the way round the world. It was the first measurement of the size of the Earth, and it came out close to the truth.

**Uneven halves.** The Earth moves fastest when it is nearest the Sun, in January. So it hurries through the northern winter half of its orbit and lingers over the summer half. From the March equinox to the September one is about 186 days. The way back takes about 179. Summer in the north is about a week longer than winter.

**The tilt is not fixed.** Two slow changes are going on, both measured and both pulled along by the gravity of the Sun, Moon and planets.

- *The lean changes.* Over the last million years the tilt has swung between 22.1° and 24.5°, taking about 41,000 years for each swing. It is 23.4° now and slowly shrinking. A bigger tilt means sharper seasons.
- *The axis wobbles like a spinning top.* It traces a circle once in about 25,800 years. One result is that the Pole Star has not always marked north and will not always do so. Another is that the seasons slide round the orbit. Today the Earth is nearest the Sun in the northern winter. In about 13,000 years it will be nearest in the northern summer, and the north's seasons will be harsher than they are now.

Changes like these alter how much summer sunshine reaches the far north, and they set the rhythm of the ice ages.

**Common mistakes.**

- *In summer the Sun is overhead at noon.* In Britain it never is. The Sun can stand straight overhead only between the two tropics. In London it gets no higher than 62°.
- *Day and night are exactly equal at the equinox.* Nearly, but the day is a few minutes longer. Sunrise is counted from the first edge of the Sun, not its middle, and the air bends the light so that the Sun is seen when it is really just below the horizon.
- *The seasons are caused by the Earth wobbling during the year.* The axis holds steady through the year. It is the Earth's position in its orbit that changes, so that the same lean points first towards the Sun and then away.
- *The longest day is the hottest.* The warmth lags behind the light by a month or so, because the sea is slow to warm.

**More seasons elsewhere.** Jupiter leans by only 3.1° and has almost no seasons. Saturn leans by 26.7°, and since its year lasts 29 of ours, each of its seasons runs for more than seven years. Its rings tip towards us and away as it goes, and about every fifteen years they are seen edge-on and almost disappear.

**Where distance does matter.** On Mars the orbit is far more stretched than the Earth's. Its distance from the Sun changes by about a fifth, from 207 million km to 249 million km, so sunlight at its nearest is about 45 per cent stronger than at its furthest (a sum from NASA's figures). Mars is nearest the Sun during the southern summer. That makes summers in its south short and fierce and winters there long and bitter, while the north has milder seasons. Mars has seasons from tilt and seasons from distance at once.

**Built to mark it.** People tracked the turning points of the year long before anyone knew their cause. Stonehenge, raised about 4,500 years ago, is lined up on the sunrise at midsummer and the sunset at midwinter.

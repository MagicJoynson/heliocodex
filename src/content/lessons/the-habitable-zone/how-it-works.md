**Where the edges come from.** Nobody has measured a habitable zone. The edges are worked out with a model of a planet's air. At the inner edge, so much water evaporates that it climbs to the top of the air, where sunlight breaks it up and the hydrogen leaks away to space. Over time the planet loses its seas. At the outer edge, even the thickest blanket of carbon dioxide can no longer keep the ground above freezing. The estimate used in this lesson was published in 2013 and puts those two edges at 0.99 and 1.70 times the Earth's distance for the Sun.

| | Distance from the Sun (Earth = 1) | In the zone? | Average at the ground |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venus | 0.72 | No, too close | About 464 °C |
| Earth | 1 | Yes, only just | About 15 °C |
| Mars | 1.52 | Yes | About −65 °C |

**Why the Earth looks so near the edge.** The model has no clouds in it. Clouds reflect sunlight, so a real planet can probably sit a little closer to its star than the model says. Other estimates put the edges in slightly different places. The zone is best treated as a rough band, not a sharp line.

**What the zone leaves out.**

- **How big the planet is.** A small planet has weak gravity and loses its air. Mars is the example.
- **What the air is made of.** Too much carbon dioxide and a planet overheats inside the zone. Too little and it freezes.
- **Heat from inside.** Moons such as Enceladus and Europa are squeezed by the pull of their giant planets, and that keeps seas liquid under the ice, far outside the zone. See [Ocean moons](/learn/ocean-moons).
- **The star itself.** Small red stars flare, and planets in their zones are so close that one side may always face the star. See [TRAPPIST-1](/learn/trappist-1).
- **Time.** Stars brighten as they age, so the zone creeps outwards.

**So what is it for?** It tells astronomers where to point their telescopes first. Among thousands of known planets, the ones in the zone and about the size of the Earth are the ones whose air is most worth trying to read.

**A worked example: sunlight at each planet.** Light thins out with the square of distance. Venus, at 0.72 of the Earth's distance, gets 1 ÷ (0.72 × 0.72), or 1.9 times as much sunlight as the Earth. Mars, at 1.52, gets 0.43 times as much (sums). The two edges of the zone in this lesson are where a planet would get about 1.02 times and about 0.35 times the Earth's sunlight.

**The zone round other stars.** The same reasoning moves the zone for a brighter or dimmer star. The distance for a given amount of light goes as the square root of the star's output.

| Star | Light given out (Sun = 1) | Zone, roughly (Earth's distance = 1) |
|---|---|---|
| Sirius | 25 | 5 to 8.5 |
| The Sun | 1 | 0.99 to 1.70 |
| Proxima Centauri | 0.0016 | 0.04 to 0.07 |
| TRAPPIST-1 | 0.0006 | 0.024 to 0.040 |

These are sums from each star's measured output, and they are rough, because the colour of the star's light shifts the edges as well. A dim red star shines mostly in infrared, which a planet soaks up more readily, so its real zone reaches somewhat further out than the simple sum says. The fuller reckoning puts three of TRAPPIST-1's planets in the zone. The sums still show the main thing. Round a dim red star the zone is tiny and close in. Proxima's planet b, at 0.0485 of the Earth's distance, has a year of 11.2 days and lies inside the zone. Mercury, by comparison, is at 0.39.

**The idea has a history.** The astrophysicist Su-Shu Huang worked out the modern idea in a series of papers in the 1950s and 1960s. The nickname "Goldilocks zone" came later, from the story of the porridge that was neither too hot nor too cold.

**The zone moves.** When the Earth was young the Sun gave out only about 70 per cent of the light it does now. By the simple reckoning the whole Earth should have been frozen, yet rocks from that time show that there was liquid water. The likeliest answer is a stronger greenhouse effect in the early air, though the details are still argued over. It is called the faint young Sun problem.

The Sun is still brightening, by about one per cent every hundred million years. The inner edge of the zone is creeping outwards towards us. In something like a billion years the Earth will be too close, and its seas will begin to be lost in the way described above.

**Venus and Mars as warnings.** Mars has dry river valleys and the beds of old lakes, so it once had running water, and it lies inside the zone now. Venus may have had seas when the Sun was fainter. Both show that being in the zone is neither enough nor permanent.

**How common are such planets?** In 2014 the Kepler space telescope found Kepler-186f, the first planet close to the Earth's size in the habitable zone of another star. A study of all of Kepler's results, published in 2020, estimated that about half of the stars like the Sun could have a rocky planet in the zone. With so many stars in the Galaxy, that would mean hundreds of millions of such planets, though the margin on the estimate is wide.

**Checking a real one.** The zone only says where to look. Whether a planet there has air at all has to be measured. In 2023 the Webb telescope took the temperature of the two innermost planets of TRAPPIST-1, which lie just inside the zone's inner edge, by catching their faint heat. Both turned out to be about as hot as bare rock would be, which means they have little or no air to spread the heat round. The planets further out, inside the zone, are harder to measure and are being watched now.

**Common mistakes.**

- *A planet in the zone is habitable.* It only means that water could be liquid there if the planet has the right air. The Moon is in the Sun's zone and is dry and airless.
- *Habitable means people could live there.* It means only that liquid water is possible on the surface. The air might be poisonous and the gravity crushing.
- *Outside the zone there can be no life.* The zone is about water on a surface warmed by a star. The seas under the ice of Europa and Enceladus are warmed in another way.

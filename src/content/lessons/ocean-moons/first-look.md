Far from the Sun everything should be frozen solid. Yet some moons out there have oceans.

The water is hidden. On top is a shell of ice, kilometres thick and as hard as rock. Underneath is a sea of salty liquid water, in the dark.

Europa, a moon of Jupiter a little smaller than ours, is thought to hold about twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans put together.

At Saturn, the small moon Enceladus gives its ocean away. Jets of water vapour and ice grains spray out of cracks near its south pole and into space. A spacecraft has flown straight through them and tasted what is inside.

Where there is liquid water there might be life, so these moons are some of the most interesting places we know.

**How can water stay liquid out there?** The heat comes from squeezing. A moon like Europa is stretched and let go again on every lap of its planet, the same pull that raises tides in our seas. Bend a paper clip back and forth and it gets warm. Do that to a whole moon, for billions of years, and the inside stays warm enough to keep a layer of water from freezing.

**How big are these seas?** Europa's ocean is thought to be between 60 and 150 km deep. The deepest point in any ocean on Earth is about 11 km down. Europa is a small world, but its sea goes all the way round and a very long way down, which is how it can hold twice our water.

**A very small moon.** Enceladus is only about 500 km across. It would fit between London and Edinburgh. Nobody expected anything so small to be warm inside, and its jets were one of the great surprises of the Cassini mission to Saturn.

**What the spacecraft tasted.** Cassini flew through the jets several times with instruments that could tell what the spray was made of. It found water, salt, grains of sand-like rock and simple chemicals that contain carbon. It also found hydrogen gas, which is what hot springs on a sea floor would make. So the ocean of Enceladus seems to be salty, to sit on rock, and to be warmed from below.

**Why that matters.** On the Earth there are whole communities of animals round hot springs on the deep sea floor, in complete darkness. They live on chemical energy from the springs, not on sunlight. They were found only in 1977. Before that, most people assumed that life needed the Sun.

**Two things not to assume.** An ocean on one of these moons is nothing like the seaside. It has no surface open to the sky, no light and no air above it. And nobody has found life in any of them. What has been found is a place where the question is worth asking.

**Who is going.** NASA's Europa Clipper left in October 2024 and reaches Jupiter in 2030. Europe's Juice left in April 2023 and arrives in 2031, to study Ganymede above all.

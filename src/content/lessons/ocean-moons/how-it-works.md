**Where the heat comes from.** Sunlight is no help: at Jupiter it is 27 times weaker than at Earth, and at Saturn about 90 times weaker. The warmth comes from inside, and most of it comes from tides.

A moon on a slightly oval orbit is squeezed harder when it is near its planet and less when it is far. That steady flexing rubs the inside of the moon against itself and warms it, the way a paper clip warms when you bend it back and forth. Left alone, an orbit would settle into a circle and the heating would stop. What keeps it going is a neighbour: another moon whose regular tugs hold the orbit oval. The lesson on moons in step explains how.

**How we know the water is there.** Nobody has seen any of these oceans. The evidence is indirect, and it differs from moon to moon.

| Moon | Planet | The evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Europa | Jupiter | A young, cracked surface with few craters, and a magnetic signal of the kind salt water gives |
| Ganymede | Jupiter | Its auroras rock back and forth less than they should, as a salty ocean would make them |
| Enceladus | Saturn | Jets of water and ice sampled directly, and a wobble that shows the crust floats free of the core |
| Titan | Saturn | The way its shape flexes during each orbit |

**Europa** is the best studied. Its ocean is thought to hold about twice the water of all Earth's oceans, under a shell of ice. NASA's Europa Clipper, launched in October 2024, is on its way to fly past it dozens of times.

**Enceladus** is only about 500 km across, and should have frozen through long ago. Instead its south pole is split by four long warm cracks, and jets of water vapour, ice grains, salts and simple carbon compounds come out of them. The ice there may be as little as 1 to 5 km thick.

**Ganymede** is the largest moon in the Solar System and the only one with a magnetic field of its own. Its ocean may hold more water than all of Earth's surface, perhaps in several layers with ice between them.

**Titan** has two kinds of sea. On the surface there are lakes and seas of liquid methane and ethane, under a thick nitrogen atmosphere. It is the only place besides the Earth known to have liquid on its surface. Far below, under the ice, there is water as well.

**Why it matters.** Life as we know it needs liquid water, a source of energy and some chemistry to work with. These moons appear to have all three. None of that means anything lives there. It means they are worth a close look, and several spacecraft are on their way to take one.

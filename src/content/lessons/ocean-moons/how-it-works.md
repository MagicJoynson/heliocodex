**Where the heat comes from.** Sunlight is no help: at Jupiter it is 27 times weaker than at Earth, and at Saturn about 90 times weaker. The warmth comes from inside, and most of it comes from tides.

A moon on a slightly oval orbit is squeezed harder when it is near its planet and less when it is far. That steady flexing rubs the inside of the moon against itself and warms it, the way a paper clip warms when you bend it back and forth. Left alone, an orbit would settle into a circle and the heating would stop. What keeps it going is a neighbour: another moon whose regular tugs hold the orbit oval. The lesson on moons in step explains how.

**How we know the water is there.** Nobody has seen any of these oceans. The evidence is indirect, and it differs from moon to moon.

| Moon | Planet | The evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Europa | Jupiter | A young, cracked surface with few craters, and a magnetic signal of the kind salt water gives |
| Ganymede | Jupiter | Its auroras rock back and forth less than they should, as a salty ocean would make them |
| Enceladus | Saturn | Jets of water and ice sampled directly, and a wobble that shows the crust floats free of the core |
| Titan | Saturn | The way its shape flexes during each orbit |

**Europa** is the best studied. Its ocean is thought to hold about twice the water of all Earth's oceans, under a shell of ice. NASA's Europa Clipper, launched in October 2024, is on its way to fly past it dozens of times.

**Enceladus** is only about 500 km across, and should have frozen through long ago. Instead its south pole is split by four long warm cracks, and jets of water vapour, ice grains, salts and simple carbon compounds come out of them. The ice there may be as little as 1 to 5 km thick.

**Ganymede** is the largest moon in the Solar System and the only one with a magnetic field of its own. Its ocean may hold more water than all of Earth's surface, perhaps in several layers with ice between them.

**Titan** has two kinds of sea. On the surface there are lakes and seas of liquid methane and ethane, under a thick nitrogen atmosphere. It is the only place besides the Earth known to have liquid on its surface. Far below, under the ice, there is water as well.

**Europa in numbers.** NASA's figures put the ice shell at 15 to 25 km thick and the ocean beneath at 60 to 150 km deep. Take the middle of each, a 20 km shell over 100 km of water, and the sum gives about 2.8 billion cubic kilometres of water. The Earth's oceans hold about 1.3 billion. That is where "twice the water" comes from, and it shows how soft the figure is: change the depths within their ranges and the answer moves a good deal.

**The magnetic clue.** Europa sits inside Jupiter's huge magnetic field, and because Jupiter spins and its field is tilted, the field at Europa keeps changing direction. A changing magnetic field drives electric currents in anything that conducts, and the currents make a magnetic field of their own. The Galileo spacecraft, which circled Jupiter from 1995 to 2003, found just such a field at Europa, switching direction in step. Ice does not conduct well enough to do it. Salt water does. The signal needs a layer of salty water, all the way round, not far below the surface.

**Flying through a sea.** Cassini made its closest pass over Enceladus in October 2008, 25 km above the surface. It had not been built to study jets, since nobody knew they were there when it was launched in 1997, but its instruments for sampling gas and dust did the job. Without landing or drilling, a spacecraft took a sample of an ocean on another world.

**The end of Cassini.** In September 2017, out of fuel, Cassini was steered into Saturn to burn up. One reason was Enceladus. A dead spacecraft left in orbit might one day have hit the moon, carrying microbes from the Earth into the very ocean people hope to search.

**More of them.** There are weaker signs of buried water at Jupiter's moon Callisto, at Neptune's moon Triton and even inside Pluto. If those hold up, oceans under ice are not rare. There may be more liquid water in the outer Solar System than there is on the Earth.

**A wider idea of where to look.** Astronomers used to draw a band round each star where a planet would be warm enough for liquid water on its surface, and to look for life only there. The ocean moons lie far outside the Sun's band. Their warmth comes from tides, not sunshine, so a moon can have liquid water at almost any distance from its star. The search for places to live now has to include moons of giant planets round other stars.

**Why nobody has simply gone and looked.** Three things stand in the way.

- *Distance.* Jupiter is more than five times as far from the Sun as the Earth is, and Saturn nearly ten. The trip takes six years or more.
- *Radiation.* Europa orbits inside Jupiter's radiation belts, which would wreck unprotected electronics in days. Europa Clipper will not orbit Europa. It will loop round Jupiter and dip in for about 50 short passes.
- *Ice.* To reach Europa's water a probe would have to get through 15 km of ice or more. The deepest holes ever drilled in ice on the Earth are under 4 km, and each took years.

Enceladus is the easy case, because it throws its ocean into space where a passing spacecraft can catch it.

**Common mistakes.**

- *These oceans have been seen.* None has. Every one is worked out from indirect signs, set out in the table above.
- *Water means life.* Water is one of three things life needs, and nobody knows how often life starts even where all three are present. There is one known example: the Earth.
- *The surface of Europa is a good place to look.* The surface is bathed in radiation and at about −160 °C. Anything alive would be far below.

**Why it matters.** Life as we know it needs liquid water, a source of energy and some chemistry to work with. These moons appear to have all three. None of that means anything lives there. It means they are worth a close look, and two spacecraft are on their way to take one.

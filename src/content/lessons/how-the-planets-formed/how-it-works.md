Nobody saw it happen. The account below is pieced together from three kinds of evidence: the pattern of the Solar System today, the ages and make-up of meteorites, and discs that telescopes can see around young stars now. The outline is well supported. Several of the steps are still being worked out, and the lesson says where.

**The clues in the pattern.**

- The planets go round the Sun in the same direction, in nearly the same plane.
- The Sun spins that way too.
- Small rocky planets are close in, and giants are further out.
- Leftover rubble sits in belts between and beyond them.

A flat, spinning disc explains all four.

**From dust to planets.**

| Stage | What happens | How long |
|---|---|---|
| A disc forms | The cloud collapses; what cannot fall in settles into a disc of gas with about one part in a hundred of dust | About 100,000 years |
| Dust sticks | Grains collide gently and cling, growing to pebbles | Thousands to tens of thousands of years |
| Planetesimals | Swarms of pebbles clump under their own gravity into bodies kilometres across | Not well understood |
| Runaway | The biggest bodies pull in the most, and grow fastest | Up to a few million years |
| The gas goes | The young Sun's light and wind clear the gas away. Giant planets must be finished by now | Within about 10 million years |
| Final collisions | In the inner system, dozens of bodies the size of the Moon or Mars collide and merge into four planets | Tens of millions of years |

The step from pebbles to planetesimals is the hard one. Pebbles tend to bounce or shatter, not stick, and they should spiral into the Sun before they grow. Current thinking is that they gather in dense swarms that collapse all at once.

**Why two kinds.** Near the Sun the disc was hot, and only rock and metal were solid. Those make up a small share of the material, so the inner planets stayed small. Beyond the frost line, between where Mars and Jupiter are now, water froze as well, and there was several times more solid stuff to build with. Out there, cores grew to about ten times the mass of the Earth while the gas was still around. A core that heavy pulls in hydrogen and helium without limit. Jupiter and Saturn are the result. Uranus and Neptune grew more slowly and caught less gas before it was gone.

**The last big collision.** The Earth's final major impact, with a body about the size of Mars, is thought to have thrown off the material that became the Moon, about 4.5 billion years ago.

**Planets move.** The planets probably did not form exactly where they are. Computer models in which the giants shifted their orbits early on do a better job of explaining the thin asteroid belt, the tilted orbits in the Kuiper Belt and the small size of Mars. This is the least settled part of the story, and there are several competing versions.

**Watching it elsewhere.** In 2014 the ALMA telescope in Chile made a sharp picture of the disc round HL Tauri, a star about 450 light years away and less than a million years old. It shows bright rings separated by dark gaps, of the kind forming planets would sweep clear. Many such discs have been pictured since. And more than 6,000 planets are now known round other stars. Many of those systems look nothing like ours, with giant planets closer to their stars than Mercury is to the Sun, which is good evidence that planets migrate.

**An old idea.** The notion that the planets condensed from a spinning cloud is older than most of the evidence for it. The philosopher Immanuel Kant proposed it in 1755 and the mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace worked it out more fully in 1796. It then fell out of favour for a long time, mainly because nobody could explain why the Sun turns so slowly: it has nearly all of the mass and yet almost none of the spin, which is mostly in the orbit of Jupiter. The idea came back for good in the 1980s, when telescopes began to find just such discs round young stars.

**A worked example: the radioactive clock.** One kind of uranium turns into lead at a fixed pace. Half of any sample has gone after 4,468 million years. The oldest grains in meteorites are 4,567 million years old, which is a shade more than one such half-life, so 49 per cent of the uranium they started with is still there and 51 per cent has become lead (a sum). Measuring the ratio of the two in a grain gives its age. The same clock run on two different kinds of uranium at once gives the same answer, and that is why the figure can be quoted to within a million years or so.

**How the Moon's origin is known.** The giant impact is not a guess from nowhere. The rocks brought back by the Apollo crews are a very close chemical match for the Earth's own outer rock, and unlike any meteorite. The Moon has only a small iron core, as it would if it were made from the outer layers of two bodies whose iron had already sunk to their centres. And its rocks are short of the substances that boil away easily, as if they had been strongly heated. Computer models of a glancing collision reproduce all three.

**Jupiter came first.** Meteorites fall into two families with slightly different mixtures of atoms, as if they came from two stores of material that were kept apart for a couple of million years. The simplest way to keep them apart is to have a large planet between them, opening a gap in the disc. On that evidence Jupiter's core had grown to many times the Earth's mass within about a million years of the start. It is probably the oldest planet.

**A planet seen forming.** The young star PDS 70, about 370 light years away, has a disc with a wide gap in it, and two planets have been photographed inside the gap. They are several times the mass of Jupiter and are still pulling in gas. One of them has a small disc of its own, of the kind that Jupiter's four big moons are thought to have formed from.

**Our system is not the usual kind.** The commonest planets found round other stars are between the Earth and Neptune in size, and many go round closer in than Mercury. The Solar System has none like that. Nor do most stars like the Sun have a giant planet in a wide, round orbit like Jupiter's. Any full account has to explain the others as well as us, and that is a large part of why the theory is still being rebuilt.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The disc was mostly dust.* It was about 99 per cent gas. The dust was the small part that could stick.
- *The frost line is a place you could find today.* It was a feature of the young disc and moved as the disc cooled. Its effects are what remain.
- *Collisions like the one that made the Moon are still likely.* The inner Solar System was cleared of bodies that size more than 4 billion years ago.

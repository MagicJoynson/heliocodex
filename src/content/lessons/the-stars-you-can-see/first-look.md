On a dark night the sky looks like a dome with the stars painted on it, all the same distance away. They are not. Some are close and some are hundreds of times further off.

Sirius, the brightest, is under 9 light years away. Deneb, another bright one, is about 1,400. The light you see from Deneb tonight set out around the year 600.

That is why the patterns are not real groups. The three stars of Orion's Belt sit in a neat row, but the middle one is given as nearly three times further away than the other two. Stand somewhere else in the galaxy and the row would fall apart. People have joined the dots for thousands of years, and today the sky is divided into 88 official constellations, but each is a pattern seen from here.

Every star you can see by eye belongs to our own galaxy, the Milky Way, and almost all of them are in our own small part of it. Nine in ten are within 1,000 light years. The galaxy is 100,000 light years across.

In the 3D view you can do what nobody can do from the ground: move away and look at these stars from the side.

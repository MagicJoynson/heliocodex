A planet round another star is called an exoplanet. The first round a star like the Sun was found in 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, who shared a Nobel Prize for it in 2019. It orbits the star 51 Pegasi, about 50 light years away. By late 2025 the count of confirmed planets had passed 6,000, and it grows every month.

Hardly any of these have been photographed. Nearly all were found through what the planet does to its star.

| Method | What is measured | Share of discoveries | Tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transit | The star dims slightly as the planet crosses in front | About three quarters | The planet's size and the length of its year |
| Wobble | The star's light shifts bluer, then redder, as the planet tugs it to and fro | About a fifth | The planet's mass (strictly, the least it can be) and its year |
| Direct picture | The planet's own faint glow, with the star's light blocked out | About 1 in 100 | What its light is made of |
| Lensing | A distant star brightens briefly as a planet's gravity bends its light | A few in 100 | Mass, including of planets far from their star |

**Transits.** The dip is small. Jupiter crossing the Sun would dim it by 1 part in 100. The Earth would dim it by less than 1 part in 10,000, for about 13 hours, once a year. Catching that needs a telescope above the atmosphere watching without a break. The Kepler spacecraft stared at about 150,000 stars in one patch of sky from 2009 to 2013 and found more than 2,600 planets. Its successor, TESS, has been surveying nearly the whole sky since 2018.

A transit only happens if the orbit is edge-on to us. For a planet at the Earth's distance from a star like the Sun, the chance is about 1 in 200. So for every planet found this way, there are many more that never cross.

**The wobble.** A star and its planet both circle their shared centre of mass. The star's circle is small, but its light gives it away. Dark lines in its spectrum shift a little towards blue as it comes towards us and towards red as it goes away. Jupiter moves the Sun at about 12 metres a second, the speed of a sprinter. The Earth moves it at 9 cm a second. The planet of 51 Pegasi moves its star at more than 50 metres a second, which is why it was found first.

**Both together.** A transit gives a planet's size and the wobble gives its mass. Mass divided by volume is density, and density says whether a planet is rock, water or gas.

**What has been found.** Surveys find most easily what is large and close to its star, so the list is not a fair sample. Even so, some results are clear.

- **Hot Jupiters.** Giant planets with years of a few days. The planet of 51 Pegasi has about half Jupiter's mass and goes round in 4.2 days. They are rare, found round about 1 Sun-like star in 100, but easy to detect.
- **Super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.** Planets between the Earth and Neptune in size are among the commonest kind known. The Solar System has none.
- **Small rocky planets** are common, particularly round red dwarf stars. The nearest star of all, Proxima Centauri, 4.25 light years away, has a planet of at least the Earth's mass with a year of 11 days.

Our own arrangement, with small planets inside and giants outside on nearly circular orbits, is one pattern among many. How common it is cannot yet be said, because a copy of the Solar System would be very hard to find with today's instruments.

**Reading the air.** During a transit, a little starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere on its way to us, and the gases there leave their marks in the spectrum. The Webb telescope has measured water vapour, carbon dioxide and other gases this way in the air of giant planets. Doing the same for a small rocky planet is at the limit of what is possible today.

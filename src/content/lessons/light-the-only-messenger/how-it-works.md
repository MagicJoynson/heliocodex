Light crosses empty space at 299,792 km every second, and nothing that carries news goes faster. Because that speed is fixed, a distance and a delay are the same thing: the Sun is 8.3 light minutes away, and what we see of it is 8.3 minutes old.

A beam of light carries far more than a picture. Spread it out by wavelength into a spectrum and four separate things can be read from it.

**How hot.** A glowing body shines brightest at a wavelength set by its temperature. Cool stars at about 3,000 K look red. The Sun, at 5,772 K, peaks in green and looks white. The hottest stars, above 30,000 K, look blue.

**What it is made of.** Each kind of atom takes in and gives out light at its own exact wavelengths, which show up as narrow lines across the spectrum. The pattern is as particular as a barcode. Helium was found as a set of unexplained lines in sunlight in 1868, nearly thirty years before it was found on Earth.

**How it is moving.** If the source is coming towards us every line shifts a little towards blue, and if it is going away, towards red. The size of the shift gives the speed. A star being tugged back and forth by an unseen planet gives itself away like this.

**How far and how powerful.** Light spreads out as it travels, so the same star twice as far away looks a quarter as bright. Know either the distance or the true power and you can work out the other.

**More than the eye sees.** Visible light is one narrow band of a far wider range, running from radio waves through infrared, visible and ultraviolet to X-rays and gamma rays. Different things shine in different bands: cold gas in radio, dust and young stars in infrared, gas at millions of degrees in X-rays. The Earth's air blocks most of them, which is the main reason telescopes are put in space.

**How anyone found that light takes time.** For most of history people thought light arrived instantly. In 1676 the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer, working in Paris, was timing the moon Io as it went into Jupiter's shadow on each lap. The eclipses ought to have run like clockwork. They did not: they came later and later while the Earth was moving away from Jupiter and earlier again as it came back. Rømer saw why. When the Earth is further off, the light has further to come, and it needs time to cross the extra distance. It was the first sign that light has a speed, and the first rough measure of it. Since 1983 the speed has been fixed by definition at exactly 299,792.458 km a second, and the metre is defined from it.

**Working the temperature out.** The rule that links colour to heat is simple enough to use. Divide 2.9 million by the temperature in kelvin and the answer is the wavelength, in millionths of a millimetre, at which the body shines brightest. These are sums made with that rule.

| Body | Temperature | Shines brightest at | Which is |
|---|---|---|---|
| A hot blue star | 30,000 K | 97 | Ultraviolet |
| The Sun | 5,772 K | 502 | Green light |
| A cool red star | 3,000 K | 966 | Infrared, just beyond red |
| A person | 310 K | 9,350 | Infrared |

Two things follow. A "red" star gives out most of its light as infrared, and looks red only because red is the part the eye can catch. And people glow too, in infrared, which is how a thermal camera sees them in the dark.

**Working the speed out.** Hydrogen has a strong red line at a wavelength of 656.28 millionths of a millimetre. Suppose that in a star's light the line turns up at 656.35 instead. The shift is 0.07 parts in 656, or about one part in 9,400, so the star is moving away at one 9,400th of the speed of light. That is about 32 km/s (a sum). Shifts a thousand times smaller than this can be measured, which is how planets are found by the wobble they give their stars.

**Working the brightness out.** Neptune is 30 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is. Light spreads over an area that grows with the square of the distance, so sunlight there is 30 × 30, or 900 times, weaker. Noon on Neptune is about as bright as dusk here. Turned round, the same rule measures distance: if two stars are known to be equally powerful and one looks 100 times fainter, it is 10 times further away.

**Two common mistakes.**

- *Space is dark because there is no light in it.* Space is full of light. A beam of light is invisible from the side unless something scatters it towards your eye, and space has almost nothing in it to do the scattering. The sky looks black to an astronaut standing in full sunshine.
- *Radio waves are a kind of sound.* Radio is light of long wavelength. It travels at the speed of light and crosses empty space, which sound cannot do. A radio set turns the signal into sound only at the very end.

**The other messengers.** A few kinds of news do not come as light.

- *Things brought back.* The Apollo crews carried home 382 kg of Moon rock between 1969 and 1972, and robot craft have returned grains from asteroids, a comet and the solar wind. Meteorites deliver pieces of asteroids, the Moon and Mars for nothing.
- *Neutrinos.* On 23 February 1987 three detectors caught a burst of 25 neutrinos in under 13 seconds, two to three hours before astronomers saw a star explode in a small galaxy next to ours. The neutrinos had come straight out of the collapsing core. The light had to wait for the blast to reach the star's surface.
- *Gravitational waves.* Since 2015, detectors have felt black holes and neutron stars colliding. In August 2017 one such collision was caught both ways, as a wave and then as light.
- *Cosmic rays.* Fast particles from space strike the air all the time. They carry electric charge, so the magnetism of the galaxy bends their paths and most cannot be traced back to where they began.

Light is no longer quite the only messenger. Ripples in space itself, called gravitational waves, were first picked up in 2015, and ghostly particles called neutrinos reach us from the Sun's core. But they are whispers beside the flood of light.

Nobody has touched a star. Apart from a few rocks and some dust brought back from very nearby, everything we know about space came to us as light, and had to be read from it.

Light is fast, but space is big enough that even light takes its time.

- From the Moon: a little over 1 second
- From the Sun: 8 minutes
- From the nearest other star: more than 4 years
- From the Andromeda Galaxy: about 2.5 million years

So you never see the sky as it is. You see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago. On a dark night you can see the Andromeda Galaxy as a faint smudge, and that light set out before there were any people.

**What the delay means.** If the Sun went dark this second, nothing would change here for 8 minutes. The sky would stay blue and the Sun would go on shining in it, because the light already on its way would keep arriving. The news that anything had happened could not reach the Earth any sooner, since nothing travels faster than light does.

The planets work the same way. Jupiter is so far off that its light takes between 33 and 54 minutes to arrive, depending on where it and the Earth are in their orbits (a sum from NASA's distances). When you look at Jupiter through a telescope you are watching a recording, about three quarters of an hour old.

Looking further away always means looking further back. It is the nearest thing to a time machine that anyone will ever have.

**A common mistake.** People often say that the stars you see at night are probably dead, and that only their old light is left. It makes a good story, and it is almost certainly wrong for every star you can see. The stars visible without a telescope are close by, as these things go: nearly all are within a few thousand light years. Stars live for millions or billions of years. A delay of a few thousand years is a very small part of such a life, so the chance that any one of them died in that time is tiny.

For distant galaxies the saying comes nearer the truth. Some of the galaxies in the deepest pictures are seen as they were more than 13 billion years ago. The galaxies are still there, but many of the stars whose light made the picture burnt out long ago.

**What light cannot tell us.** Reading everything from light has a price. Light shows only the side of a thing that faces us, and only what shines or is lit. For most of history nobody knew what the far side of the Moon looked like, until a spacecraft flew round it in 1959. And some things give out no light at all, and can be found only by the pull of their gravity on things that do.

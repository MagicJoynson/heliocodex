Every dot here is a real planet going round another star. There are more than 6,000 of them. Each is placed by two things that have been measured: how wide it is, and how long it takes to go once round its star. Small planets are at the bottom and giants at the top. Short years are on the left and long ones on the right.

Now put our own eight planets on the same chart. They sit off to one side, where there are hardly any dots. Most of the planets found so far are far closer to their stars than ours are. More than four in every five have a year shorter than Mercury's 88 days.

Part of that is the way we look. A big planet close to its star tugs harder on the star and blocks more of its light, and it comes round again every few days, so it is the easiest kind to catch. Planets like ours, small or far out, are much harder to find. Many more must be there than we have seen.

The first kind to turn up was a shock. In 1995 a giant planet was found going round a star like the Sun once every four days. Nobody had expected a giant so close in. Hundreds of these hot Jupiters are known now. They are heated to thousands of degrees, and they are thought to have formed further out and moved inward.

The commonest kind found so far lies between the Earth and Neptune in size. More than half of the planets on the chart are in that band, and the Solar System has nothing in it at all. The smaller ones are called super-Earths and may be rock. The larger are called mini-Neptunes and are probably wrapped in deep gas. Nobody yet knows what most of them are really like.

Some are stranger still. A planet called 55 Cancri e, 41 light years away, goes round its star in 18 hours. It always keeps the same face to the star. That side is at about 2,400 degrees, hot enough for the rock to be molten.

Hardly any of these worlds has been seen directly. This is one that has. The bright spot is a planet a few times as heavy as Jupiter, still forming inside the disc of gas and dust round a very young star. The star itself has been blocked out so that its glare does not drown the planet.

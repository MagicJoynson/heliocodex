Most of what is known about the sky was not found in one flash by one genius. It was found by people sitting with photographs, charts and numbers for months, noticing something small that did not fit. Here are four of them. All four worked in the 20th century, and each one changed how the whole universe is pictured.

**Henrietta Leavitt: how far away things are.** Working life: the 1890s to 1921.

</figure>

Leavitt worked at Harvard College Observatory as one of its "computers": women paid by the hour, 30 cents an hour in her case, to measure stars on glass photographs. She was given photographs of the Magellanic Clouds, two small star clouds seen from the southern half of the world, and found 1,777 stars in them that brighten and fade over and over.

Then she noticed something. In the Small Cloud, the stars that took longer to brighten and fade were the brighter ones. All those stars are at about the same distance from us, so the difference had to be real. A star's beat gives away how bright it truly is. Compare that with how bright it looks, and you know how far away it is. Her rule, published in 1912, became the first way to measure the distance to other galaxies. Leavitt died in 1921, before anyone used it to show that there are other galaxies at all.

**Cecilia Payne: what the stars are made of.** Working life: the 1920s to the 1960s.

Payne grew up in England and studied at Cambridge, which did not then give degrees to women. She moved to Harvard in 1923. In 1925, aged 25, she worked out what the dark lines in starlight really mean. Everyone had assumed that the stars were made of much the same things as the Earth. Her answer was that they are almost all hydrogen and helium, the two lightest elements, with everything else a small trace.

It was so unexpected that a senior astronomer persuaded her to write that the result was almost certainly not real. Four years later he reached the same answer himself. She was right, and it is true of the Sun and of nearly every star.

**Jocelyn Bell: stars that tick.** Working life: the 1960s to now.

In 1967 Jocelyn Bell was a student at Cambridge. She had helped to build a radio telescope, a field of wires and poles, and her job was to read the long rolls of paper chart it drew, sometimes as much as 29 metres of it in a night. In among the noise she spotted a small mark she called "a bit of scruff". It came back. Looked at closely, it was a run of pulses, one every 1.34 seconds, as steady as a clock.

For a while the team half joked that it might be a signal from someone else, and named it LGM-1, for "little green men". Then she found a second one in a different part of the sky. They were pulsars: dead stars no bigger than a city, spinning and sweeping a beam past the Earth with every turn. In 1974 the Nobel prize for physics went to her supervisor, Antony Hewish, for the discovery, shared with Martin Ryle for other work. Her name was not on it.

**Vera Rubin: the matter nobody can see.** Working life: the 1950s to the 2010s.

Rubin wanted to study astronomy at Princeton, which would not take women on its astronomy course. She studied elsewhere, and in the 1970s, with Kent Ford, she measured how fast stars and gas go round spiral galaxies. The outer parts should have gone round slowly, the way the outer planets go slowly round the Sun. They did not. They went round as fast as the inner parts. Something unseen, with far more mass than all the stars, must be holding them in. That is the best evidence for what is now called dark matter. A great telescope in Chile, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, is named after her.

**What they have in common.** Each of them looked hard at ordinary data, believed what it said when it said something odd, and was proved right. Each of them also worked at a time when a woman's name was easy to leave off the credit.

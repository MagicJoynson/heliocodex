**Leavitt's law, 1912.** Leavitt had already listed 1,777 stars that change in brightness in the two Magellanic Clouds, in a paper of 1908. For 25 of those in the Small Cloud she worked out the period, the time each takes to brighten, fade and brighten again. The shortest took just over a day and the longest 127 days. When she set each star's brightness against its period, the points fell along a line.

Why it works: all the stars in the Small Cloud are at about the same distance, so if one looks brighter than another, it really is brighter. The rule therefore says how bright a star of each period truly is. Light spreads out as it travels, so a star twice as far away looks four times fainter, and ten times as far away looks a hundred times fainter. Knowing how bright a star truly is and measuring how bright it looks gives its distance.

One step was missing: how far away the Small Cloud itself is, which sets the scale. In 1913 Ejnar Hertzsprung used nearby stars of the same kind to fix it roughly. In 1923 Edwin Hubble found one of these stars in the Andromeda "nebula", with a period of 31.4 days, and showed it was far outside the Milky Way. Andromeda was a galaxy of its own. The paper of 1912 was signed by the observatory's director, Edward Pickering, with a line saying that Miss Leavitt had prepared it.

**Payne's hydrogen, 1925.** The dark lines in starlight had been sorted into classes, O, B, A, F, G, K and M, by Annie Jump Cannon and others at Harvard. Most astronomers read a strong line as meaning a lot of that element. Payne used a new idea from the Indian physicist Meghnad Saha, published in 1920: how hot a gas is decides how many of its atoms are in a state to make a given line. A cool star can show strong iron lines with little iron. A hot star can hide its hydrogen.

Allowing for the temperature of each class, the lines told a single story. The stars are made of the same mixture, and almost all of it is hydrogen and helium. Henry Norris Russell, the most senior astronomer in America, persuaded her to write that the amount of hydrogen she had found was "almost certainly not real". In 1929 he reached the same result by another route. Measured today, the Sun's surface is by weight about 74 per cent hydrogen, 25 per cent helium and a little over 1 per cent everything else.

Payne's thesis was the first doctorate in astronomy from Radcliffe College, for work done at Harvard. In 1956 she became the first woman to be made a full professor at Harvard, and the first to head one of its departments.

**Bell's pulsar, 1967.** The telescope Bell helped to build was made to watch distant radio sources twinkle as the solar wind crossed in front of them, which needed a fast recorder. That speed is what let it catch something flashing on and off. The first source she found, now called PSR B1919+21, pulses every 1.3373 seconds.

Nothing as big as an ordinary star could flash that fast: light takes 1.3 seconds to cross only 400,000 km, less than a third of the width of the Sun. The source had to be small. The team's paper in February 1968 suggested a white dwarf or a neutron star. It turned out to be a neutron star, the collapsed core of a heavy star that has exploded: more mass than the Sun in a ball about 20 km across, spinning and sweeping a radio beam past the Earth like a lighthouse. The pulsar in the Crab Nebula, found in 1968, turns 30 times a second.

In 2018 Bell, by then Jocelyn Bell Burnell, was given a Special Breakthrough Prize of 3 million dollars. She gave all of it to a fund, run by the Institute of Physics, that helps women, students from minority ethnic groups and refugees to train as physics researchers.

**Rubin's flat curves, 1970 onwards.** Things in orbit go more slowly the further out they are when nearly all the mass sits in the middle. The Earth goes round the Sun at 29.8 km a second. Neptune, 30 times further out, goes at 5.4 km a second.

Most of a spiral galaxy's light comes from near its middle, so its outer stars and gas should behave like the outer planets. Rubin and Ford measured the speeds from the shift in the lines of glowing gas clouds, first across Andromeda in 1970 and then in many other spirals. Far out, beyond most of the light, the speeds stayed high, at about 200 to 250 km a second, instead of falling.

Speed that stays level means the mass keeps growing the further out you go, even where there are few stars. The Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky had seen a hint of the same thing in 1933, in how fast galaxies move inside a cluster. Rubin's measurements made it hard to ignore. The best figures today, from the Planck spacecraft, put the unseen matter at about five times the ordinary matter in the universe. Nobody yet knows what it is.

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, named after her, showed its first pictures on 23 June 2025. Over ten years it is to photograph the whole southern sky again and again.

**Two common mistakes.** None of these four worked alone: Leavitt had Pickering's photographs, Payne built on Saha, Bell worked in Hewish's team and Rubin with Ford. Being part of a team is not the same as being a helper. And "not believed at first" does not mean the others were foolish. A result that overturns what is known should be checked hard. What matters is that each of these was checked, and held.

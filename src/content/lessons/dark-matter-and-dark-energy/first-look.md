Here is one of the strangest things science has found. Almost all of the universe is missing.

Stars, planets, gas, dust, people: everything made of atoms adds up to about a twentieth of what is out there.

  <img src="/img/learn/charts/what-the-universe-is-made-of.svg" alt="A bar chart: atoms, all we can see, about 5 per cent; dark matter about 27 per cent; dark energy about 68 per cent" width="800" height="390" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A chart.</b> What the universe is made of, by share. The figures are the rounded ones NASA gives.</figcaption>
</figure>

About a quarter is something called dark matter. It gives out no light, and we cannot see or touch it. But it has gravity. Galaxies spin so fast that they should fly apart, and something unseen is holding them together.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/abell-370-hubble.jpg" alt="A crowd of yellow galaxies with thin blue arcs of light curving among them" width="1100" height="1225" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The galaxy cluster Abell 370, by the Hubble Space Telescope. The thin arcs are galaxies far behind the cluster, and their light has been bent on the way by the cluster's gravity. The bending is far stronger than the galaxies you can see could manage, which is one sign of dark matter. Credit: NASA, ESA/Hubble, HST Frontier Fields, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

The rest, more than two thirds, is called dark energy. It does the opposite of gravity. It is pushing the universe apart faster and faster.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/supernova-1994d-hubble.jpg" alt="A galaxy seen edge on with a dark band of dust, and one brilliant star at the lower left" width="1100" height="1100" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The bright point at the lower left is one exploding star, Supernova 1994D, by the Hubble Space Telescope. Explosions of this kind all reach nearly the same brightness, so they show how far away they are. In 1998 distant ones turned out fainter than expected, the first sign that the universe is spreading faster and faster. Credit: NASA/ESA, The Hubble Key Project Team and The High-Z Supernova Search Team, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

The names are labels, not explanations. "Dark" here means "unknown". Nobody yet knows what either of them is. Working that out is one of the biggest unsolved problems in science.

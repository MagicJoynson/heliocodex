**Two unknowns, measured well.** The amounts of dark matter and dark energy are known to a per cent or two. What either of them is has not been settled by any experiment. This level sets out the numbers, the searches that have come back empty, and the one measurement that may be showing something new.

**The amounts.** The Planck satellite's reading of the oldest light gives the density of each ingredient as a fraction $\Omega$ of the total, multiplied by $h^2$, where $h$ is the Hubble constant divided by 100 km/s per megaparsec.

| Quantity | Planck 2018 value |
| --- | --- |
| Dark matter, $\Omega_c h^2$ | 0.120 ± 0.001 |
| Ordinary matter, $\Omega_b h^2$ | 0.0224 ± 0.0001 |
| Hubble constant, $H_0$ | 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s per megaparsec |
| All matter, $\Omega_m$ | 0.315 ± 0.007 |

Three things follow, each worked out here from those figures. Dividing the first row by the second gives 5.4 parts of dark matter to one of ordinary matter. With $h = 0.674$, dark matter is 26.4 per cent of the total and ordinary matter 4.9 per cent. In a flat universe, which is the model Planck's data fit, whatever is not matter is dark energy: one minus the last row, or 68.5 per cent.

**How thin it all is.** The total density of a flat universe is fixed by the expansion rate:

$$
\rho_c = \frac{3 H_0^2}{8 \pi G}
$$

With Planck's $H_0$ this comes, by our own sum, to $8.5 \times 10^{-27}$ kg/m³, the mass of five protons in each cubic metre. Ordinary matter is one proton in every four cubic metres. Dark energy is $5.8 \times 10^{-27}$ kg/m³, which as energy is $5.3 \times 10^{-10}$ joules in a cubic metre. It dominates only because space is so empty of everything else.

**Dark matter: looking for a heavy particle.** If dark matter is a particle, the Earth is moving through a thin gas of it, and now and then one should strike the nucleus of an atom. The most sensitive search so far is LZ, which watches seven tonnes of liquid xenon 4,850 feet underground in South Dakota, where the rock keeps out cosmic rays.

| Experiment | Exposure | Result | Tightest limit on the cross-section |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| XENONnT, 2023 | About 1.1 tonne-years | No significant excess | $2.58 \times 10^{-47}$ cm² at a particle mass of 28 GeV/c² |
| LZ, 2024 | 4.2 tonne-years, from 280 days of running | No excess over the expected background | $2.2 \times 10^{-48}$ cm² at 40 GeV/c² |

The cross-section is the effective target area a nucleon presents to the particle. The smaller the limit, the more feebly the particle must interact if it exists. A mass of 40 GeV/c² is about 43 times the mass of a proton, by our own sum. These limits apply to particles of 9 GeV/c² and heavier, the kind long known as WIMPs, for weakly interacting massive particles. They say nothing about much lighter ones.

**Dark matter: looking for a very light particle.** The axion is a particle proposed for a different reason, to solve a puzzle about the strong nuclear force, and it would also serve as dark matter. In a strong magnetic field an axion can turn into a photon of frequency

$$
f = \frac{E}{h}
$$

where $E$ is the axion's energy, nearly all of it the energy of its mass, and $h$ here is Planck's constant. The ADMX experiment is a 136-litre copper-plated cavity inside a 7.6 tesla magnet, tuned like a radio to pick up that photon. The power expected is of the order of $10^{-23}$ watts.

In 2020 ADMX reported a search for axion masses from 2.81 to 3.31 millionths of an electronvolt. By our own sum that is radio frequencies from 679 to 800 MHz. It found nothing, and ruled out the benchmark axion models across that window. The window is narrow. The same paper notes that one line of argument puts the axion's mass at about a millionth of an electronvolt or more, so a great deal of the range is still to be searched.

The two kinds of particle would be very different in number. The ADMX analysis takes the dark matter round us to have a density of 0.45 GeV in each cubic centimetre. Worked out here, that is about 11 particles a litre if each has a mass of 40 GeV/c², or about 150 million million in every cubic centimetre if each is an axion of 3 millionths of an electronvolt.

**Where that leaves dark matter.** Every piece of evidence for dark matter is still its gravity. No particle has been detected in a laboratory. The searches have not shown that there is no particle. They have shown that the simplest heavy candidates interact more feebly than was once hoped, and they have opened a first narrow window on the light ones.

**Dark energy: the equation of state.** How anything affects the expansion depends on one number, the ratio of its pressure $p$ to its energy density:

$$
w = \frac{p}{\rho c^2}
$$

As the universe grows by a scale factor $a$, a component with constant $w$ thins out as

$$
\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}
$$

and the expansion speeds up or slows down according to

$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4 \pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right)
$$

Matter has $w = 0$, thins as $a^{-3}$, and slows the expansion. Anything with $w$ below $-\tfrac{1}{3}$ makes the bracket negative and speeds it up. A cosmological constant, energy belonging to space itself, has $w = -1$ exactly, so its density never changes.

Two dates follow if dark energy is a constant, both worked out here with Planck's figures. Matter thins and dark energy does not, so the two were equal when the universe was smaller by a factor of 1.30, about 3.5 billion years ago. The expansion began to speed up earlier, when matter had fallen to twice the density of dark energy: a factor of 1.63, about 6.1 billion years ago.

**Is it a constant?** The DESI survey measures how the distances to galaxies have grown, using a pattern in the way galaxies cluster as a standard ruler.

| Result | Data | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DESI 2024 | Over 6 million galaxies and quasars | With $w$ held constant, DESI alone gives $w = -0.99$, +0.15 −0.13. With $w$ allowed to change, a changing value is preferred at 2.6 sigma with the oldest light added, and at 2.5, 3.5 or 3.9 sigma with one of three supernova catalogues added as well |
| DESI 2025 | More than 14 million | The same preference at 3.1 sigma with the oldest light, and from 2.8 to 4.2 sigma depending on the supernova catalogue |

In both, the preferred values have $w$ above $-1$ today. By the thinning rule above, that would mean the density of dark energy is now slowly falling.

This is not yet a discovery. Physicists usually ask for 5 sigma, and here the figure depends on which supernova catalogue is used. DESI's own data by themselves are well described by a constant. The DESI team's wording is that, unless one of the data sets has an error nobody has found, the standard model is being challenged and a changing dark energy is a possible answer.

**The size problem.** Even a perfectly constant dark energy would not be understood. Quantum physics says empty space should have energy, and the natural estimate is enormous. Counting the contributions up to the highest energy at which present theory might be trusted gives about $2 \times 10^{109}$ joules in a cubic metre. The measured figure above is $5.3 \times 10^{-10}$. The two differ by about 120 powers of ten. The estimate could be cut off at a lower energy, but even well-tested physics gives contributions tens of powers of ten too large. No known principle makes them cancel.

**Where it stands.**

- The amounts of dark matter and dark energy are measured to a per cent or two.
- Dark matter has been detected only through gravity. The laboratory searches so far have found nothing.
- Dark energy fits a constant with $w = -1$ to within the errors of any single experiment. Combined data sets hint at change, at a level short of a discovery.
- Nobody can yet explain why the energy of empty space is as small as it is.

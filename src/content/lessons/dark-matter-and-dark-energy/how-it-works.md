The two have little in common except the word "dark" and the fact that neither is understood. The evidence for each is strong. What they are is unknown.

| | Share of the universe today | What it does | How we know |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary matter | About 5 per cent | Makes stars, planets, gas and us | We see it |
| Dark matter | About 27 per cent | Pulls, like any matter, but gives out and blocks no light | Its gravity |
| Dark energy | About 68 per cent | Makes the expansion of the universe speed up | The expansion history |

**Dark matter**

**Galaxies turn too fast.** In the Solar System, planets far from the Sun move slowly, because nearly all the mass is at the centre. A galaxy's light is also concentrated towards its centre, so stars at the edge should move more slowly than stars further in. In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured them, and they do not. The speeds stay level right out to the edge and beyond. For that to happen, each galaxy must sit inside a much larger ball of unseen mass.

**Clusters are too heavy.** In 1933 Fritz Zwicky found that the galaxies of the Coma cluster move so fast that the cluster should have scattered long ago, unless it holds far more mass than its galaxies can account for. The hot gas between the galaxies, found later, is not nearly enough.

**Light bends too much.** Mass bends light. A cluster of galaxies stretches the images of galaxies behind it into arcs, and the amount of bending gives the cluster's total mass with no need to see it. The answer is about five or six times the mass of everything visible.

**One collision.** The Bullet Cluster, about 3.8 billion light years away, is two clusters that have passed through each other. Their hot gas, which is most of their ordinary matter, collided and was left behind in the middle. Lensing shows that most of the mass went straight on with the galaxies. The mass and the ordinary matter are in different places.

**The oldest light.** The pattern of patches in the cosmic microwave background depends on how much matter there was that light could push about and how much it could not. It gives the same answer: about five parts dark to one part ordinary.

**What it might be.** Not dim stars, planets or black holes of ordinary origin: searches have ruled those out as the main part. The leading idea is a kind of particle not yet discovered, one that hardly interacts with atoms or light at all. Detectors deep underground have waited for decades for one to strike an atom. So far none has been seen, and the simplest candidates are being ruled out one by one.

A different idea is that there is no extra matter and that gravity behaves differently over great distances. Changed laws of gravity can match the spin of single galaxies well. They have not managed to explain the Bullet Cluster and the oldest light at the same time, so most astronomers favour dark matter. The question stays open until something is detected.

**Dark energy**

**The discovery.** The gravity of everything in the universe should slow the expansion down. In 1998 two teams set out to measure by how much. They used one kind of exploding star, a white dwarf blown apart, which always reaches nearly the same true brightness and so shows its distance. The far ones were fainter than they should have been. The expansion is not slowing. It has been speeding up for about the last 6 billion years. The work won a Nobel Prize in 2011.

**What it might be.** The simplest answer is that empty space itself has a fixed amount of energy in every cubic metre. As space grows there is more of it, so its push comes to dominate. Einstein's equations have room for exactly such a term. The trouble is that attempts to work out its size from the physics of particles give answers that are wrong by an absurd margin.

**Is it constant?** In 2024 and 2025 a survey called DESI, which is mapping tens of millions of galaxies, reported signs that dark energy may have weakened over time. The evidence is not yet strong enough to count as a discovery. The Euclid space telescope, launched in 2023, and other surveys are measuring the same thing independently.

**Why it matters.** If dark energy stays constant, the expansion goes on speeding up for ever, and distant galaxies will one by one pass out of sight. If it changes, the far future is not settled.

**A balance.** A star is a contest between two things. Gravity pulls all of its gas towards the middle. The heat of the gas pushes back out. Where the two are equal the star holds its shape, and it can stay that way for billions of years.

The squeeze makes the centre hot and dense. In the Sun's core it is nearly 16 million degrees. At that temperature the nuclei of hydrogen atoms hit each other hard enough to join into helium, and each joining lets out a little energy. This is nuclear fusion. The Sun turns about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium every second, and has enough to go on for billions of years yet.

**The lower limit.** Fusion needs that heat, and the heat needs weight. A ball of gas with less than about 8 per cent of the Sun's mass, roughly 80 times the mass of Jupiter, never gets hot enough in the middle to keep hydrogen burning. It glows dull red from the heat of its own shrinking and slowly cools. These are brown dwarfs: too heavy to be planets and too light to be stars.

**The upper limit.** The heaviest stars known have about 200 times the mass of the Sun. Much beyond that, a star shines so fiercely that its own light blows its outer layers away.

**Mass decides.** Once you know how much gas a star began with, you know most of the rest.

| Mass (Sun = 1) | What it is like | Light given out (Sun = 1) | Time spent burning hydrogen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | A red dwarf, cool and faint | About a thousandth | Trillions of years |
| 1 | A star like the Sun | 1 | About 10 billion years |
| 10 | A blue-white giant | Several thousand | About 30 million years |

The last column is the surprise. A heavy star has more fuel, but it burns it far faster, so it dies young. A red dwarf sips its fuel so slowly that none has ever yet run out: the universe, at 13.8 billion years, is not old enough. The figures for red dwarfs come from computer models of stars, because nobody can watch one grow old.

**Why heavy stars die young: the sum.** For stars like the Sun and heavier, the light given out climbs very steeply with mass: roughly as the mass raised to the power of three and a half. A star of 10 Suns therefore shines about 3,000 times as brightly as the Sun. It has 10 times the fuel and spends it 3,000 times as fast, so it lasts about a three-hundredth as long: 10 billion years divided by 300 is about 30 million years, the figure in the table. The rule is a rough one, found by measuring many stars, and it bends at both ends of the range.

**How anyone can weigh a star.** Many stars are not single: about half of those like the Sun have a partner. They come in pairs that circle each other, and a pair can be weighed. Time one lap, measure how far apart the two are, and the law of gravity gives their mass, just as the Moon's orbit gives the mass of the Earth. Everything known about the masses of stars rests on pairs like these. Single stars are then weighed by matching their light and colour to stars in pairs.

**How anyone knows what they are made of.** Spread a star's light into a rainbow and it is crossed by dark lines, each one the mark of a particular kind of atom. In the early 1920s astronomers read those lines as showing that stars were made of much the same stuff as the Earth. In 1925 a young astronomer at Harvard, Cecilia Payne, worked out that the strength of a line depends on the star's temperature as well as on how much of the element is there. Allowing for that, she found the stars are overwhelmingly hydrogen and helium, with hydrogen about a million times more common than the metals. The leading expert of the day, Henry Norris Russell, did not believe it. Four years later he reached the same answer another way and said so in print.

**Where the other elements come from.** The universe began with hydrogen, helium and almost nothing else. Every heavier element was made later, and nearly all of it inside stars. Fusion in the core of a star builds helium into carbon and oxygen, and in heavy stars goes on to iron. Dying stars scatter what they have made, and it goes into the next generation of stars and planets. The Sun is one of those later stars. The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were made in stars that died before the Sun was born.

**Three common mistakes.**

- *Stars twinkle.* They shine steadily. The twinkling is made at the very end of the journey, by moving air bending the light this way and that. From space no star twinkles, which is one reason telescopes are sent there.
- *A shooting star is a star.* It is a grain of dust, often no bigger than a grain of sand, burning up as it hits the air about 100 km overhead.
- *The brightest stars are the nearest.* Some are. But a star's brightness in the sky depends on its distance and its power together, and the range of power is vast: a hundred million times or more between the faintest red dwarf and the mightiest giant. Many of the most famous stars are far away and extremely powerful.

**A star's colour is its temperature.** A glowing thing changes colour as it heats up, as a poker in a fire goes from dull red to orange to white. Stars do the same. The coolest have surfaces at about 3,000 degrees and look red. The Sun, at about 5,500 °C, is white. The hottest are above 30,000 degrees and look blue-white. So, against what taps and paint suggest, red stars are the cool ones and blue stars are the hot ones.

**Stars are born in batches.** Stars form when a cold cloud of gas and dust, many light years across, collapses under its own weight. One cloud breaks into many clumps, so stars are born together by the hundred or the thousand and drift apart afterwards. The Sun was born this way about 4.6 billion years ago, and its brothers and sisters are now scattered round the galaxy. Nobody has yet been able to say for certain which stars they are.

**What is common.** Small stars are common and large ones are rare. About three in every four stars near the Sun are red dwarfs, and not one of them can be seen without a telescope. The bright stars that make up the constellations are the unusual ones: they are either close or very powerful.

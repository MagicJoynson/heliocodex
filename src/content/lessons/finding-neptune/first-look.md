Most planets were found by looking. Neptune was found by doing sums. Two mathematicians, working from nothing but the path of another planet, said where in the sky to point a telescope. On the first night someone looked, it was there. This happened in the 19th century, in 1846.

**A planet that would not behave.** In 1781 William Herschel found Uranus, the first planet discovered since ancient times. Astronomers then did what they always did: they worked out its orbit from Newton's law of gravity, allowing for the pulls of Jupiter and Saturn, and printed tables saying where it would be each night for years ahead.

Uranus did not follow the tables. At first it ran a little ahead of where it should have been. Then, from about the 1820s, it began to fall behind. The errors were tiny, far too small to see without a telescope, but they kept growing, and the best astronomers of the day could not make them go away.

**Two ideas.** Perhaps Newton's law of gravity was not quite right so far from the Sun. Or perhaps something unseen was pulling on Uranus: another planet, further out. If so, the way Uranus was being pushed and pulled ought to give away where that planet was.

**The sums.** In England a young Cambridge mathematician, John Couch Adams, began the calculation in 1843. In France Urbain Le Verrier, working on his own, began it in 1845. It was a hard problem: they had to work backwards from the errors to a planet whose mass, distance and position were all unknown.

Adams told the observatory at Cambridge, which was slow to search. Le Verrier published his answer in 1846 and, unable to stir up interest in Paris, wrote to Johann Galle at the Berlin Observatory, telling him where to look.

**The night of 23 September 1846.** Galle got the letter that morning. That night he and a student, Heinrich d'Arrest, pointed their telescope at the place. D'Arrest suggested comparing what they saw with a new, detailed chart of the stars in that part of the sky. Within an hour Galle called out a faint star, and d'Arrest found that it was not on the chart. It was less than one degree from where Le Verrier had said, about two widths of the full Moon. Over the next nights it moved slowly against the stars. It was a planet.

**What had been seen before.** Galileo had drawn Neptune in his notebook in December 1612 and January 1613, beside Jupiter, without knowing it was a planet. The Cambridge astronomer James Challis, searching on Adams's numbers, had seen it twice in August 1846 but had not compared his notes in time to notice that it moved. Seeing something is not the same as knowing what it is.

**Who gets the credit.** For a while there was a bitter argument between England and France. Today both Adams and Le Verrier are given credit for predicting it, and Galle and d'Arrest for finding it.

**Why it mattered.** Newton's law of gravity had been used to explain the paths of things already seen. Here it had been used to find something nobody had seen. It was one of the strongest proofs anyone had that the law was right.

**Two common mistakes.** Neptune was not found by luck: a telescope pointed at random would have had little hope of picking out one faint dot among thousands of stars. And the method does not always work. When Le Verrier later tried the same trick on Mercury, he predicted a planet that is not there, because that time the law itself was the problem. How it works tells that story.

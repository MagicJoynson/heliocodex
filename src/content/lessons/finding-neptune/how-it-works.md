| 13 March 1781 | William Herschel finds Uranus |
| 1821 | Alexis Bouvard publishes tables of Uranus, and notes it is already straying from them |
| about 1822 | Uranus overtakes Neptune |
| 1843 to 1845 | John Couch Adams works on the problem in Cambridge |
| 10 November 1845 | Urbain Le Verrier presents his first paper on Uranus in Paris |
| 1 June 1846 | Le Verrier gives the unknown planet's position |
| 8 and 12 August 1846 | James Challis at Cambridge sees it without recognising it |
| 31 August 1846 | Le Verrier gives its mass and orbit |
| night of 23 to 24 September 1846 | Johann Galle and Heinrich d'Arrest find it in Berlin |

**Why one planet can push another.** Every body pulls on every other, with a force that grows weaker with the square of the distance between them. The Sun's pull steers Uranus round its orbit. Jupiter and Saturn add small tugs, and astronomers of the 1800s knew how to allow for those. What was left over, after every known pull had been counted, had to come from something else.

Uranus takes 84 years to go round the Sun and Neptune takes about 165, so Uranus slowly catches up and passes Neptune once every 171 years or so. As it came up from behind before 1822, Neptune's pull was partly forward, along Uranus's path, and sped it up a little. After passing, the pull was partly backward and slowed it. That change from ahead to behind was the fingerprint Adams and Le Verrier read.

**Working backwards.** Calculating where a known planet will be is hard but routine. Working out an unknown planet from its effects is far harder, because the mass, the distance and the place on its orbit are all unknown and each can make up for the others: a heavy planet far away pulls much like a lighter one nearer in. Both men had to guess where to start. Both started from an old rule of thumb for the spacing of the planets, which put the next one at about 38 times the Earth's distance from the Sun. Neptune is at 30.

Their orbits were wrong, but their positions for 1846 were close. That was partly because the planet's pull mattered most around the years when the two planets were close together, and their sums fitted those years best. Their predicted planet and the real one were in nearly the same direction in the 1840s, and their paths drifted apart in the years before and after.

**Neptune in numbers.** It is about 30 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is, 4,500 million km. It is about four times as wide as the Earth and 17 times as heavy. At its distance it is far too faint to see without a telescope. One trip round the Sun takes about 165 years, so in July 2011 Neptune finished the first whole orbit anyone has seen since it was found.

**The trick that failed.** Mercury's orbit is a slightly stretched loop, and the loop itself turns slowly round the Sun. Almost all of that turning is explained by the pulls of the other planets, but not quite. In 1859 Le Verrier suggested another unseen planet, inside Mercury's orbit, and it was given a name, Vulcan. People reported seeing it, but nobody could find it again. In 1915 Albert Einstein showed that his new theory of gravity, general relativity, explains the leftover turning exactly. There was no planet. That time the law of gravity was what needed changing.

**Planet X and Pluto.** In the early 1900s Percival Lowell thought the paths of Uranus and Neptune still did not quite fit, and searched for a "Planet X". In 1930 Clyde Tombaugh, at Lowell's observatory, found Pluto. But Pluto is far too small to pull on Uranus or Neptune noticeably. The leftover errors were later traced to a small mistake in the mass used for Neptune, put right after Voyager 2 flew past in 1989. Pluto was found in a good search for a wrong reason.

**Planet Nine.** In 2016 Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown pointed out that several small, distant bodies beyond Neptune have orbits that line up in an odd way. They suggested that a planet several times heavier than the Earth, far beyond Neptune, could be herding them. As of November 2026 no such planet had been found. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory, which will photograph the southern sky again and again for ten years, should either find it or make it much harder to believe in.

**Two common mistakes.** Adams and Le Verrier did not have a telescope or a camera to find Neptune with. They had pen, paper and Newton's law, and the observers in Berlin did the looking. And the story is not that the sums were exactly right. They were wrong in the distance and the orbit, and right where it mattered: where to look that autumn.

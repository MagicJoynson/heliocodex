The space between the stars is not quite empty. Here and there it holds huge clouds of gas and dust. A cloud like this is called a nebula, which is the Latin word for cloud.

Stars are born in these clouds. The gas is very thin, but gravity slowly pulls it together into clumps. As a clump grows, its gravity gets stronger and it pulls in more. In the end it falls in on itself. The middle is squeezed and heats up, and that hot ball is the start of a new star.

You can see one of these places without a telescope. On a dark night in January, find the three stars in a row that make Orion's belt. Close by, just below them as seen from the northern half of the world, is a faint, fuzzy patch. That is the Orion Nebula, and thousands of young stars are inside it.

Other nebulae mark the end of a star, not the start. When a star dies, it throws its outer layers back into space as a glowing cloud.

</figure>

In time that gas drifts into new clouds, and new stars are made from it.

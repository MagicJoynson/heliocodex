By mass the Sun is about 73% hydrogen and 25% helium, with every other element making up under 2%. All of it is plasma: gas hot enough for its atoms to have lost electrons, so that it carries electric current and is pushed about by magnetism.

Working outward from the centre:

**The core** fills the inner quarter of the radius. At its centre the temperature is about 15.7 million K and the gas is squeezed to about 150 times the density of water. This is the only place where fusion happens. Every second about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen become helium, and about 4 million tonnes of mass leave as energy.

**The radiative zone** reaches out to about 70% of the radius. Energy crosses it as light, but the gas is so dense that the light is absorbed and sent off in a new direction almost at once, again and again. The trip takes many thousands of years.

**The convective zone** is the outer 30%. Here the gas carries the heat itself, rising hot, cooling at the top and sinking again, like a pan on the boil. The tops of the rising columns are the granules that pattern the surface, each about the size of France or larger.

**The photosphere** is the layer we see. It is a few hundred kilometres deep and its temperature is 5,772 K. Sunspots are patches of it held about 1,700 degrees cooler by strong magnetism.

**The chromosphere and corona** lie above. The corona is extremely thin but has a temperature of more than a million kelvin, far hotter than the surface below it. Its outer edge has no end. It streams away past the planets as the solar wind.

The whole output is 3.83 × 10²⁶ watts. The Earth catches less than a billionth of it.

The Sun does not turn as a solid body. Its equator goes round in about 25 days and the regions near the poles take about 36.

**How anyone knows what is inside.** Nobody can see below the surface, and no instrument could survive the trip. The inside is known in two ways.

The first is sound. The whole Sun rings like a bell. Its surface rises and falls by a small amount, in patches, about once every five minutes, as sound waves from below reach the top and bounce back down. The waves travel at a speed that depends on the temperature and the stuff they pass through, so the pattern at the surface shows what lies beneath, in the same way that earthquake waves show the inside of the Earth. This is how the depth of the convective zone and the turning speed of the layers below were measured.

The second is a particle. Fusion in the core makes neutrinos, which pass through the rest of the Sun as if it were not there and reach the Earth eight minutes later. Tens of billions of them pass through every square centimetre of the Earth, and of you, each second, and almost none are stopped. In the late 1960s Raymond Davis began catching a few of them with a tank of 380 cubic metres of dry-cleaning fluid, nearly 1.5 km down a gold mine in South Dakota. He found only a third of the number expected. For thirty years nobody knew whether the Sun or the sum was wrong. It was neither: neutrinos come in three kinds and change from one to another on the way, and his tank could catch only one kind. Detectors that catch all three found the full number in 2001. The fusion in the core is happening at the rate the theory says.

**Checking the 4 million tonnes.** Einstein's equation says energy is mass multiplied by the speed of light squared. Turn that round: divide the Sun's output, 3.83 × 10²⁶ watts, by the speed of light squared, and the answer is 4.26 million tonnes of mass every second. NASA's fact sheet gives the same figure.

That sounds like a rate nothing could keep up. But the Sun is very large. Over its whole life so far, about 4.6 billion years, it has turned about 100 times the mass of the Earth into light, and that is only three hundredths of one per cent of itself (sums made from the fact sheet).

**A common mistake: the Sun is on fire.** Burning is chemistry, and it needs oxygen. Fusion joins the cores of atoms and needs neither air nor flame. The difference matters because of how long each could last. If the Sun were a lump of coal burning in oxygen, giving out light at the rate it does, it would be used up in about 5,000 years (a sum that takes a kilogram of coal to give 30 million joules). In the 1800s this was a real puzzle. Geologists could see that the Earth was far older than any fuel then known could keep the Sun alight. Fusion, worked out in the 1930s, was the answer. Weight for weight it gives millions of times more energy than burning.

**Another: the Sun is yellow.** Seen from space it is white. Sunlight is a mix of every colour, strongest in the green, and the eye reads that mix as white. From the ground the air scatters some of the blue out of the direct beam, which is why the sky is blue and why the Sun looks yellowish, and red when it is low.

**Not a dense ball.** The Sun as a whole is only 1.4 times as dense as water, about a quarter of the Earth's density. The centre is far denser than lead, and the layer that looks like a surface is thinner than the air in this room by thousands of times. A spacecraft that could stand the heat would fall in without hitting anything.

**What reaches the Earth.** At the Earth's distance, sunlight carries 1,361 watts through every square metre facing the Sun. The Earth presents a disc 12,742 km across to it, and so catches about 170,000 million million watts, which is less than half a billionth of what the Sun gives out. The rest goes past, in every other direction, into space.

It is about 4.6 billion years old and roughly halfway through its supply of hydrogen in the core.

A rainbow shows every colour your eyes can see, from red on one side to violet on the other.

But the rainbow does not really stop at its edges. It carries on in both directions, with kinds of light that eyes cannot pick up.

</figure>

Beyond the red end is infrared. You cannot see it, but you can feel it: it is the warmth on your face from a fire. Further on are the radio waves that carry music to a radio.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/pillars-hubble-and-webb.jpg" alt="Two pictures of the same tall pillars of cloud side by side: dark and solid on the left, thinner and full of stars on the right." width="1100" height="950" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>Two real pictures, side by side.</b> The same clouds in two kinds of light. Left: the Hubble telescope, mostly in light we can see. Right: the Webb telescope, in infrared, which passes through the dust and shows the stars inside. The colours of both were chosen. Credit: NASA, ESA, Hubble Heritage Team (left); NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI (right). CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

Beyond the violet end is ultraviolet, which gives you sunburn. Further still are X-rays, which doctors use to see bones.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/bullet-cluster-xray-chandra.jpg" alt="A glowing cloud with a bright cone-shaped clump on its right side, on black." width="900" height="728" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A real picture, in X-rays.</b> Gas at millions of degrees between the galaxies of a far-off cluster, seen by NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory. In ordinary light this gas does not show at all. The colour was chosen. Credit: NASA/CXC/CfA/M. Markevitch et al.</figcaption>
</figure>

These are all light. Stars and galaxies shine in every kind. Astronomers build special telescopes for each one, and each shows them things the others cannot.

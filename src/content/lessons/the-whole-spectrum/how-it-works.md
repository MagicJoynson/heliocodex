**One thing, many sizes.** Light is a wave, a ripple in electric and magnetic fields, and it travels through empty space at 299,792 km a second. What differs from one kind of light to another is the wavelength, the distance from one crest to the next. Shorter waves carry more energy.

| Kind | Wavelength, roughly | A crest-to-crest distance about the size of | What it shows astronomers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radio | Longer than 1 mm | A building, down to a grain of rice | Cold gas between the stars, the spinning cores of dead stars |
| Infrared | 0.0007 mm to 1 mm | A pinhead, down to a cell | Stars hidden inside dust, cool things such as planets, the most distant galaxies |
| Visible | 0.0004 to 0.0007 mm | A bacterium | Ordinary stars |
| Ultraviolet | Down to a hundredth of visible | A virus | Hot young stars |
| X-rays | Smaller again by a thousand | An atom | Gas at millions of °C, and matter falling towards black holes |
| Gamma rays | The shortest of all | The nucleus of an atom | The most violent explosions known |

The part of the radio range with the shortest waves is often given its own name, microwaves. A microwave oven and a mobile phone both use it.

**How they were found.** In 1800 William Herschel spread sunlight into its colours with a prism and laid thermometers across them. The one he had put just beyond the red, where there seemed to be no light at all, warmed up the most. He had found infrared. Ultraviolet was found the next year. In the 1860s James Clerk Maxwell worked out that light is an electric and magnetic wave and that waves of other lengths ought to exist. Radio waves were made in a laboratory in the 1880s and X-rays were discovered in 1895.

**Hot things glow.** Anything warm gives out light, and the hotter it is the shorter the waves it mostly gives out. Your body, at 37 °C, glows in infrared. The Sun, at about 5,500 °C at its surface, shines mostly in visible light, and it is no accident that our eyes work in exactly that range. Gas at a million °C glows in X-rays. So choosing a kind of light is a way of choosing what temperature of thing to look for.

**Why telescopes go to space.** The Earth's air blocks most of the spectrum. That is good for us: it stops the X-rays and most of the ultraviolet that would harm living things. Only visible light, a little of the infrared and much of the radio range get through to the ground. For everything else a telescope has to be above the air.

**A worked example: wavelength and frequency.** Every kind of light obeys one rule: the length of the wave, times the number of waves arriving each second, equals the speed of light. So the longer the wave, the fewer arrive each second.

| Source | Waves each second | Wavelength (sums) |
|---|---|---|
| A radio station at 100 on the FM dial | 100 million | 3 m |
| A microwave oven | 2,450 million | 12 cm |
| Green light | 545 million million | 0.00055 mm |

The dial on a radio is marked in the number of waves a second, counted in millions. A dish for satellite television is the size it is because its waves are a few centimetres long.

**A worked example: what glows where.** Divide 2.9 by the temperature, counted in degrees above absolute zero, and the answer is the wavelength in millimetres at which a warm body glows most strongly.

| Thing | Temperature above absolute zero | Glows most strongly at (sums) |
|---|---|---|
| The glow left from the early universe | 2.7 degrees | 1 mm: microwaves |
| A person | 310 degrees | 0.009 mm: infrared |
| The Sun's surface | 5,772 degrees | 0.0005 mm: visible, in the green |
| Gas between the galaxies of a cluster | 10 million degrees and more | Less than a millionth of a millimetre: X-rays |

**Why short waves do harm.** Light arrives in separate packets, and the shorter the wave, the more energy each packet carries. A packet of radio or visible light can warm things but cannot break an atom apart. A packet of ultraviolet has enough energy to damage the molecules in skin, which is what sunburn is. A packet of X-rays has thousands of times more. That is why a bright radio transmitter is safe to stand near and a faint source of X-rays is not. It is the energy of each packet that matters, not the total.

**Each new window brought a surprise.** Nobody predicted what the sky would look like in the other kinds of light.

- *Radio.* In 1932 Karl Jansky, an engineer tracking down the crackle on telephone links, found a hiss that rose and set with the stars. It came from the centre of the Milky Way. He announced it in April 1933.
- *X-rays.* In 1962 a small rocket carried a detector above the air for a few minutes. It was meant to look for X-rays from the Moon. It found a source in the constellation Scorpius far brighter in X-rays than anything expected: gas falling onto a neutron star.
- *Gamma rays.* On 2 July 1967 American satellites built to watch for secret nuclear tests recorded a flash of gamma rays that came from deep space. Such bursts turned out to be the most powerful explosions known. The finding was not published until 1973.

**Light can change its kind on the way.** As the universe expands, light crossing it is stretched. Light from the first galaxies set out as ultraviolet and visible light and has been stretched about fifteen times since. Ultraviolet that left at 0.00012 mm arrives at 0.0018 mm, well into the infrared (a sum). That is the main reason the Webb telescope was built to see infrared. The glow of the early universe has been stretched over a thousand times, from visible light to microwaves.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Infrared is heat.* Infrared is light. Anything that takes it in warms up, and so does anything that takes in visible light. Heat can also travel in ways that have nothing to do with light.
- *Radiation means radioactivity.* Radiation only means something that spreads out from a source. Torchlight is radiation.
- *Everything from space is light.* Cosmic rays are fast particles, not light, in spite of the name. Gravitational waves and neutrinos are not light either.

| To see in | You need |
|---|---|
| Radio | A large dish on the ground |
| Visible | A telescope on the ground, or in space for the sharpest view |
| Infrared | A high, dry mountain for some of it; space for the rest, such as the Webb telescope |
| Ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays | A telescope in space, such as the Chandra X-ray Observatory |

**Putting the pictures together.** A single object can look utterly different in each kind of light. A cloud that is black and empty in visible light may blaze in infrared with newborn stars. A galaxy that looks quiet may have jets a million light years long in radio. Most of what has been learned about the universe since the 1950s came from opening these new windows one by one.

Most stars look the same night after night. Some do not. They grow brighter, fade, and brighten again, over and over. These are called variable stars.

One kind is especially useful. A Cepheid is a giant star that swells and shrinks on a steady beat, getting brighter and dimmer as it does. Some take a few days over each beat and some take weeks. The slower the beat, the brighter the star really is.

That is a great help, because a light in the dark does not tell you how far away it is. A dim light could be a torch nearby or a lighthouse far off. But if you know how bright it really is, you can work out its distance from how faint it looks. Time a Cepheid's beat and you know how bright it really is.

In 1923 the astronomer Edwin Hubble found a Cepheid in a hazy patch of sky called Andromeda. Its beat took a month, so it had to be a very bright star, and yet it looked very faint. It was far too distant to be part of our own galaxy. Andromeda was a galaxy of its own, and the universe was much bigger than most people had thought.

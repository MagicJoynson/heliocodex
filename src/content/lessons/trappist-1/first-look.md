About 40 light years from here there is a small, dim, red star called TRAPPIST-1. It is only a little wider than Jupiter.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/trappist-south-dome-la-silla.jpg" alt="A small telescope dome on a hilltop at dusk, its door open and lit, with a person outside pointing up at the sky" width="1280" height="853" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The dome of TRAPPIST-South, the small robotic telescope at La Silla in Chile that the star is named after. It found the first of the planets in 2016. Credit: ESO/E. Jehin, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

Seven planets go round it. All seven are about the size of the Earth, and all are probably made of rock.

They are packed in close. The nearest takes a day and a half to go round the star. The furthest takes under 19 days. All seven orbits would fit inside the orbit of Mercury with a great deal of room to spare.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/charts/trappist-1-orbits-inside-mercury.svg" alt="A diagram: seven small circles close together at the centre of one very large dashed circle, which is the orbit of Mercury" width="800" height="880" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A diagram, to scale.</b> The seven orbits, with the orbit of Mercury round the Sun drawn to the same scale. The outermost planet is 9 million km from its star, and Mercury is 58 million km from the Sun. Distances from the NASA Exoplanet Archive.</figcaption>
</figure>

Being so close to their star would roast them, except that this star is so faint. Three of the planets get about the right amount of warmth for water to stay liquid, if they have air to hold it in.

Nobody knows yet whether they do. Telescopes are trying to find out now.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/webb-mirror-goddard-2017.jpg" alt="A huge gold mirror made of six-sided pieces, standing in a clean room with two people in white suits in front of it" width="1100" height="1100" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The mirror of the James Webb Space Telescope before launch, 6.5 metres across. It is the telescope now taking the temperature of these planets. Credit: NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center.</figcaption>
</figure>

No telescope can show what these planets look like. Any picture you see of them, including ours, is an informed guess.

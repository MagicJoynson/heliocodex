No single ruler works for space. Each step outward is so much bigger than the last that the only sensible way to count is in powers of ten, with a new unit every few steps.

| From here | Distance | In other words |
|---|---|---|
| Across the Earth | 12,742 km | |
| Earth to the Moon | 384,400 km | 30 Earths side by side |
| Earth to the Sun | 149.6 million km | 1 au, or 390 times the distance of the Moon |
| Sun to Neptune | 4.5 billion km | 30 au |
| Sun to the edge of the solar wind | about 18 billion km | about 120 au |
| Sun to the nearest star | 40 million million km | 4.25 light years, or 269,000 au |
| Sun to the middle of the Milky Way | | about 26,700 light years |
| Milky Way to the Andromeda Galaxy | | about 2.5 million light years |
| Here to the furthest light we can see | | about 46 billion light years |

Two things stand out.

The first is how much room there is between the planets and the stars. The planets take up the innermost 30 au. The nearest star is nearly nine thousand times further away than Neptune. If comets really do fill a cloud out to tens of thousands of au, as their orbits suggest, then most of the Sun's territory is that cloud, and nobody has ever seen any of it.

The second is that the jumps do not get smaller. From the Solar System to the nearest star is a factor of about ten thousand. From there to the far side of the galaxy is another ten thousand or so. From our galaxy to the next large one is a factor of twenty-five, which sounds modest until you remember what it is twenty-five times.

The last line of the table needs a note. The universe is 13.8 billion years old, yet the furthest light we can see comes from matter that is now about 46 billion light years away, because space has kept stretching while the light was on its way.

**A model you could walk.** Numbers this large stop meaning anything, so shrink everything by the same amount. Make the Sun a football, 22 cm across. On that scale (sums made from the table above):

| Thing | Size in the model | Distance from the football |
|---|---|---|
| The Earth | A grain 2 mm across | 24 metres |
| The Moon | A speck half a millimetre across | 6 cm from the Earth |
| Jupiter | A marble 22 mm across | 123 metres |
| Neptune | A seed 8 mm across | 710 metres |
| The edge of the solar wind | | 2.8 km |
| The nearest star | A ball about 3 cm across | 6,350 km |

Everything people have ever visited in person fits inside the 6 cm between the grain and the speck. The whole family of planets fits inside a circle you could walk across in a quarter of an hour. Then there is nothing but a few specks of ice until the next star, and that is 6,350 km away, about as far as the centre of the Earth is from your feet.

**How long it would take.** Distance is easier to feel as travelling time. These are sums, not timetables.

| Journey | At motorway speed, 110 km/h | At the speed of Voyager 1, 17 km/s | At the speed of light |
|---|---|---|---|
| To the Moon | 146 days | 6 hours | 1.3 seconds |
| To the Sun | 155 years | 102 days | 8 minutes 19 seconds |
| To Neptune from the Sun | 4,700 years | 8 years | 4 hours 10 minutes |
| To the nearest star | 42 million years | 75,000 years | 4.25 years |

Voyager 1 is the fastest thing to have left the Solar System, and it has been flying since 1977. On 21 August 2024 NASA put it 164.7 au from the Earth. That is less than a thousandth of the way to the nearest star.

**How the distances are known.** Each rung was measured, not guessed.

- *The Moon.* Astronauts and robot landers left mirrors on the Moon between 1969 and 1973. Observatories fire a laser at them and time the flash coming back, about 2.5 seconds later. The distance is now known to within millimetres, and it is growing by 3.8 cm a year.
- *The Sun and planets.* In 1961 radio waves were bounced off Venus for the first time, and the echo gave its distance directly. Since the shape of the Solar System was already known from the timing of the planets, one true distance fixed all the others.
- *The stars.* As the Earth moves from one side of its orbit to the other, nearby stars shift a little against the far ones. The size of the shift gives the distance. This is set out in the lesson on measuring distance.
- *Other galaxies.* Too far for that, so astronomers use stars and explosions whose true brightness is known, and judge the distance from how faint they look.

**A common mistake: the pictures in books.** Nearly every picture of the Solar System shows the planets as fat balls in a row, close together. No picture on a page can show both their sizes and their distances truly. On a page 30 cm wide with Neptune at the edge, the Sun would be a dot a tenth of a millimetre across and the Earth too small to print.

**Another: the asteroid belt is crowded.** Films show spacecraft dodging rocks. The belt holds millions of asteroids, but it is so large that the average gap between neighbours is close to a million kilometres, more than twice the distance from the Earth to the Moon. Spacecraft have crossed it many times and none has hit anything. From one asteroid you would see the next as a point of light, if at all.

**How much is out there.** Our galaxy holds at least 100 billion stars, and the part of the universe that can be seen holds galaxies in numbers of the same order or more. The distances between stars are huge compared with the stars themselves, which is why they almost never collide. Galaxies are another matter. The Andromeda Galaxy is only about 25 widths of the Milky Way away, so galaxies are packed far closer for their size than stars are, and they run into each other often.

Shrink the Sun to the size of a football, 22 centimetres across, and put it on the centre spot of a football pitch.

On that scale the Earth is a peppercorn, 2 millimetres wide, about 24 metres away: a short walk from the centre spot. Jupiter is a large marble, 120 metres off, out beyond the stands. Neptune is a pea, 710 metres away, somewhere in the streets round the ground.

Everything in between is empty. All eight planets, with every one of their moons, fit inside a circle you could walk across in a quarter of an hour. The football holds almost all of the mass in it.

The Moon, on this scale, is a speck half a millimetre across, 6 centimetres from the Earth. Every place people have ever visited in person fits inside that 6 centimetres.

That is not how the pictures in books show it. Nearly every picture of the Solar System draws the planets as fat balls in a row, close together, because no picture on a page can show both their sizes and their distances truly. The pictures here keep to one scale throughout.

Now the question you were asked. The nearest star is Proxima Centauri, a small red star that on this scale is a ball 3 centimetres across. It is about 6,350 kilometres away, as far as London is from Chicago. It is not down the road, or in the next town, or even in the next country. It is across an ocean, and there is nothing else in between.

Alpha Centauri A and B, photographed here by the Hubble Space Telescope, are only a little further off. Proxima very probably goes round the pair. Between us and them there is a great deal of nothing, and no other star. Light, which crosses the gap from the Sun to the Earth in eight minutes, takes four and a quarter years to cross it. No spacecraft has ever been sent to any of them.

The furthest thing people have made is Voyager 1, launched in 1977. In November 2026 it is 172 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is. On the pitch that is about 4 kilometres out after nearly fifty years of flying, less than a thousandth of the way to the nearest star. In 1990 it looked back and photographed the Earth from 6 billion kilometres away: the pale speck.

Keep the same scale and go on. The Milky Way, the galaxy that holds all the stars you can see, is about 100,000 light years across, so light takes 100,000 years to cross it. On the football scale that is about 150 million kilometres, as wide as the real distance from the Sun to the Earth.

The Andromeda galaxy, the nearest large one, is 2.5 million light years away. On this scale that is about 3.7 billion kilometres, twenty-five times further than the Sun is from the Earth. Light from there takes 2.5 million years to arrive.

The oldest light we can see was set free about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. It has been travelling for nearly 13.8 billion years, and space stretched while it travelled, so where it started is now about 46 billion light years away, measured in today’s stretched-out space. On this scale that is about 7,000 light years. The [lesson on the oldest light](/learn/the-oldest-light) says what it is and how it was found.

That emptiness is what the 3D view on this site is built to show. When you fly from the Earth to the Moon and it takes a moment, then fly on and watch the Sun shrink to a bright star, you are seeing the real spacing. Nothing has been squeezed to fit.

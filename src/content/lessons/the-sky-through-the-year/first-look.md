The stars of a winter evening are not the stars of a summer one. In December Orion stands high in the south after dark. By June he has gone, and you could search all night without finding him. He has not moved. We have.

The Earth goes round the Sun once a year. At night you are on the side of the Earth that faces away from the Sun, looking out into space. As the Earth moves round its orbit, that outward view swings slowly round too. In December it points towards Orion. Six months later it points the opposite way, and Orion is up in the daytime, lost in the glare of the Sun.

Seen from the Earth, the Sun seems to creep along in front of the stars, about one degree a day, and returns to where it began after a year. Its path is called the ecliptic. The constellations it passes in front of are the zodiac, and it spends about a month in each. You never see the one the Sun is in, because it is up only by day.

The Moon and the planets keep close to the same path. That is because the Solar System is nearly flat, so from inside it everything appears along one line across the sky. From a very dark place you can even see that line as a faint cone of light after dusk. It is sunlight scattered by dust lying between the planets.

The sky also changes far more slowly than that. The Earth's axis wobbles like a spinning top, once round in about 26,000 years. So the pole of the sky drifts among the stars, and so does the Sun's place on any given date. The star signs in a horoscope are where the Sun was in ancient times. Today it is about one constellation further on.

A galaxy is a whole island of stars, and there are more galaxies than anyone could count. You might expect them to be sprinkled evenly through space, like dust in a sunbeam. They are not.

  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The Hubble Ultra Deep Field, a patch of sky that looks empty to the eye. Almost every speck here is a whole galaxy. Credit: NASA, ESA, S. Beckwith (STScI) and the HUDF Team, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>
</figure>

Galaxies keep company. Ours, the Milky Way, lives in a small gathering of more than 50 called the Local Group. Bigger gatherings, called clusters, hold hundreds or thousands of galaxies each.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/coma-cluster-hubble.jpg" alt="Dozens of glowing galaxies, large and small, scattered across a dark sky" width="1100" height="781" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Part of the Coma Cluster, which holds thousands of galaxies, by the Hubble Space Telescope. Nearly every smudge here is a galaxy. Credit: NASA, ESA and the Hubble Heritage Team (STScI/AURA), with thanks to D. Carter and the Coma HST ACS Treasury Team, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>

Step back further and a pattern appears. The clusters are joined by long threads of galaxies, and between the threads are enormous empty spaces called voids. The whole thing looks like a sponge, or the walls of soap bubbles. Astronomers call it the cosmic web.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/cosmic-web-map-2mrs.jpg" alt="A map of thousands of pale dots gathered into knots and threads with dark gaps between, marked We are here at the centre, with the Coma, Perseus and Shapley clusters ringed" width="1400" height="1400" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A map drawn from measurements.</b> Each dot is a real galaxy: 11,356 of them, in a slice of space 130 million light years thick, reaching about a billion light years from us. The dots thin out far away only because faint galaxies were not recorded. The empty wedges to the left and right are where the dust of our own galaxy hides the view. Drawn from the 2MASS Redshift Survey (Huchra and others, 2012).</figcaption>
</figure>

Gravity made it. Wherever there was slightly more matter to begin with, more was pulled in, and the places that started slightly emptier were drained. Over billions of years the difference grew into knots, threads and holes.

In the 3D view each point of the web is a real galaxy that has been measured, more than 43,000 of them. Nothing is filled in. Where you see a gap, the survey found nothing bright enough to record.

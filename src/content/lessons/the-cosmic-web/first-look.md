A galaxy is a whole island of stars, and there are more galaxies than anyone could count. You might expect them to be sprinkled evenly through space, like dust in a sunbeam. They are not.

</figure>

Galaxies keep company. Ours, the Milky Way, lives in a small gathering of more than 50 called the Local Group. Bigger gatherings, called clusters, hold hundreds or thousands of galaxies each.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/coma-cluster-hubble.jpg" alt="Dozens of glowing galaxies, large and small, scattered across a dark sky" width="1100" height="781" loading="lazy" />

Credit: NASA, ESA and the Hubble Heritage Team (STScI/AURA), with thanks to D. Carter and the Coma HST ACS Treasury Team, CC BY 4.0.</figcaption>

Step back further and a pattern appears. The clusters are joined by long threads of galaxies, and between the threads are enormous empty spaces called voids. The whole thing looks like a sponge, or the walls of soap bubbles. Astronomers call it the cosmic web.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/cosmic-web-map-sdss.jpg" alt="A map of thousands of pale dots gathered into knots and threads with dark gaps between, marked We are here at the centre, with the Coma Cluster ringed. Large dark wedges are parts of the sky that were not surveyed" width="1400" height="1400" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A map drawn from measurements.</b> Each dot is a real galaxy: 11,384 of them, in a slice of space 326 million light years thick, reaching about two billion light years from us. The dots thin out far away only because faint galaxies were not recorded. The big dark wedges are not empty space: the survey never looked in those directions. Drawn from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, Data Release 18, whose public data is in the public domain.</figcaption>
</figure>

Gravity made it. Wherever there was slightly more matter to begin with, more was pulled in, and the places that started slightly emptier were drained. Over billions of years the difference grew into knots, threads and holes.

In the 3D view each point of the web is a real galaxy that has been measured, about 42,000 of them, from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey. Nothing is filled in. Where you see a gap, the survey either did not look there, or found nothing bright enough to record. A large part of the sky was never surveyed, so the view is only a wedge of the whole.

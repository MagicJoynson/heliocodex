The cosmic web is the largest pattern there is. This level goes up through its parts in order of size, then looks at how a map of it is made and how far that map can be trusted.

**The parts.**

| Structure | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Group | 100 galaxies or fewer, held together by gravity | The Local Group: the Milky Way, Andromeda and more than 50 others |
| Cluster | Hundreds to thousands of galaxies, held together by gravity | The Coma Cluster: over 1,000 galaxies, more than 20 million light years across |
| Supercluster | A large collection of clusters and groups, mostly not bound together | Laniakea, which the Local Group sits on the edge of |
| Wall | A sheet of superclusters, hundreds of millions of light years long and about 20 million deep | The Coma Wall, about 500 million light years long |
| Void | The nearly empty space the rest is wrapped round | |

A cluster is more than its galaxies. It also holds a great deal of gas at tens of millions of degrees, and a large amount of dark matter.

**How the web formed.** Gravity pulls matter towards wherever there is already more of it. Over time that draws larger and larger things together, leaving voids between, with clusters and walls where the concentrations cross. The oldest light in the universe shows how even things were to start with: the warm and cool patches differ by about one part in 100,000. Everything in the web grew from those faint differences.

**How the map is made.** A galaxy's direction in the sky is easy to measure. Its distance is not. The map in the 3D view uses the expansion of the universe as its ruler. Space is stretching, so distant galaxies move away from us, and the further away a galaxy is, the faster it goes:

speed = Hubble constant × distance

The speed can be read from the galaxy's light, which is shifted towards red by an amount that depends on it. The 3D view takes the Hubble constant as 70 km a second for every megaparsec, so a galaxy moving away at 7,000 km/s is placed 100 megaparsecs away, about 330 million light years. The survey drawn here measured that shift for more than 43,000 galaxies over 91% of the sky. The missing strip lies behind the disc of our own galaxy, which is why the web has an empty wedge in it. The flat chart in the atlas reaches out to 310 megaparsecs, about a billion light years.

**The clusters the view names.** These distances are worked out that way from each cluster's speed.

| Cluster | Distance in million light years |
|---|---|
| Virgo | 50 |
| Fornax | 65 |
| Centaurus | 160 |
| Hydra | 180 |
| Norma | 230 |
| Perseus | 250 |
| Coma | 320 |
| Shapley Supercluster | 680 |

**How far to trust it.** Three cautions.

- The ruler is only roughly known. Different ways of measuring the Hubble constant give 67 and 73, so every distance in the table could be several per cent out.
- A galaxy's speed is not all expansion. Galaxies also fall towards each other, and for nearby ones that matters. Virgo comes out at about 50 million light years from its speed, but a direct measurement gives 16.5 megaparsecs, about 54 million.
- The survey only records galaxies above a set brightness. Far away it catches only the brightest, so the web looks thinner with distance. That thinning is the survey running out, not the universe.

**Where we are in it.** The Milky Way belongs to the Local Group, which lies on the outer edge of Laniakea, a gathering of about 100,000 galaxies some 520 million light years across. Laniakea was mapped in 2014 by working out which way galaxies are being pulled.

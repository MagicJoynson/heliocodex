Nobody has been near a black hole, and no probe could report back from inside one. What follows comes from Einstein's theory of gravity, general relativity. That theory has passed every test made of it so far, including the tests made close to black holes. Where it is known to run out, this lesson says so.

**The horizon.** The event horizon is a sphere round the black hole. Its size depends only on the mass: about 3 km of radius for each Sun. Inside it, all directions lead inwards, in the same way that on Earth all moments lead to tomorrow. Getting out is not difficult. It is not on the map.

**Two views of one fall.** Drop a probe that flashes a light once a second, and watch from a safe distance.

*What you see.* Gravity slows time. Clocks lower down run slow compared with clocks higher up. This is measured on Earth, where it is tiny, and satellite navigation has to correct for it. Near a horizon it becomes extreme. The probe's flashes arrive further and further apart. Each is redder than the last, because the light is stretched as it climbs out. The probe seems to slow, dim and redden, and it fades from view just outside the horizon. You never see it cross.

*What the probe sees.* For the probe, nothing slows down. Its own clock ticks normally, and it crosses the horizon in a short time. Nothing marks the place. Both accounts are correct. They do not contradict each other, because no message can pass from the probe to you once it is inside.

**Stretching.** Gravity is stronger at your feet than at your head if you fall feet first. This difference is a tide, the same effect that raises the Earth's seas, and near a black hole it can pull things apart. Oddly, small black holes are the fierce ones. At the horizon of a black hole of 10 Suns, 30 km from the centre, the stretching would destroy anything long before it arrived. At the horizon of the black hole in the middle of our galaxy, 12.7 million km from its centre, a falling astronaut would feel nothing unusual. The stretching comes later, further in.

**Just outside.** At one and a half times the radius of the horizon, gravity bends light so strongly that it can travel in a circle. Gas can orbit steadily only further out again, beyond about three times the horizon's radius if the black hole is not spinning. Inside that, gas drops in.

**The pictures.** A network of radio telescopes spread across the Earth, working as one dish the size of the planet, has made images of two black holes: the one in the galaxy M87, published in 2019, and the one at the centre of our own galaxy, published in 2022. Each shows a bright ring with a dark centre. The ring is light from hot gas, bent round the black hole on its way to us. The dark centre is the black hole's shadow. Bending makes the shadow look about two and a half times wider than the horizon itself. The size of both rings matched what general relativity had predicted from the masses.

**A worked example: how big is a horizon?** The radius of the horizon is twice the mass, times the constant of gravity, divided by the speed of light squared. It grows in step with the mass.

| Mass | Radius of the horizon (sums) | For comparison |
|---|---|---|
| The Earth | 9 mm | A marble |
| The Sun | 3 km | A small town |
| The black hole at the centre of our galaxy, 4.3 million Suns | 12.7 million km | 18 times the radius of the Sun, well inside the orbit of Mercury |
| The black hole in M87, 6.5 billion Suns | 19 billion km | About 130 times the Earth's distance from the Sun, as far out as the edge of the Sun's bubble of wind |

Neither the Earth nor the Sun will ever become a black hole. The table only shows how hard each would have to be squeezed.

**A surprising consequence.** The size of a horizon goes up with the mass, so the volume inside goes up with the mass cubed. The average density inside therefore falls as black holes get bigger. For the one in our galaxy it works out at about a million kilograms in each cubic metre, a thousand times the density of water. For the one in M87 it is about 0.4 kilograms in each cubic metre, a third of the density of the air in your room (sums). A black hole need not be dense. It needs enough mass within a small enough space, and for the giants "small enough" is very large.

**The idea is older than Einstein.** In 1783 an English clergyman, John Michell, reasoned from Newton's gravity that a star could be so heavy that light itself would fall back to it, and that such a star could still be found by the way it pulled on its neighbours. In 1916, a few months after Einstein published his theory, the German astronomer Karl Schwarzschild found the exact solution that describes a horizon. He did the work while serving in the army on the Russian front, and died that year. For decades most physicists, Einstein among them, doubted that nature would ever make such a thing. The name "black hole" came into use only in the late 1960s.

**How anyone knows there is a horizon and not a surface.** Gas that falls onto a neutron star hits a hard surface and its energy comes out as bursts of X-rays. Gas that falls into a black hole of similar mass gives no such burst: the energy goes in and is not seen again. There is a second check. When two black holes merge, the new one rings for a fraction of a second like a struck bell. The gravitational-wave detectors have recorded that ringing, and its pitch and its fading match what a horizon of that mass should give.

**Common mistakes.**

- *Black holes suck everything in.* From a distance a black hole pulls exactly as hard as any other body of the same mass. If the Sun were swapped for a black hole of one Sun, the Earth would freeze, but it would stay on the orbit it has now.
- *You would feel the horizon as you crossed it.* At a big black hole there is nothing there to feel.
- *Time stops at the horizon.* It seems to, for someone watching from far away. For whoever is falling, time carries on as usual.
- *A black hole is a hole.* It is a ball-shaped region of space, with mass, and it can spin.

**The middle.** The theory says that everything which falls in ends at a single point of endless density, the singularity. Few physicists believe that as it stands. It is more likely a sign that general relativity stops working there, and that a theory joining gravity to the physics of the very small, which nobody yet has, is needed.

**Do black holes last for ever?** In 1974 Stephen Hawking worked out that a black hole should glow very faintly and slowly lose mass. This is theory, and it has never been observed. For a black hole with the mass of the Sun the glow would have a temperature of about 60 billionths of a kelvin, far colder than space itself at 2.7 K, so every black hole of that size today takes in more than it gives out.

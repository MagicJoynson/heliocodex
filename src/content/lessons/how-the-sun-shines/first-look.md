The Sun is not on fire. A fire needs air, and there is none in space. A Sun made of burning coal would have gone out long ago.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/sun-sunspots-sdo.jpg" alt="The whole Sun as an orange disc with a cluster of dark spots below the middle." width="1024" height="990" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The Sun in ordinary light in October 2014, by NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory. Nothing here is burning. The whole ball is gas so hot that it glows, and the dark spots are places a little cooler than the rest. Credit: NASA/SDO.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Sun shines because of what happens in its centre. There the gas is squeezed so hard, and is so hot, that tiny particles of hydrogen are forced together to make helium. Each time that happens, a little of their weight disappears and turns into energy.

A little goes a long way. Every second the Sun gets about four million tonnes lighter, and all of that becomes light and heat.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/sun-filament-sdo.jpg" alt="The Sun as a mottled red and orange ball with bright patches and a long dark line across its lower half." width="1100" height="973" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph, with chosen colours.</b> The Sun on 10 February 2015 in extreme ultraviolet light, which the eye cannot see, so the red was chosen for it. Every bit of the energy that keeps this going was made in the centre. Credit: NASA/SDO.</figcaption>
</figure>

It has been doing this for 4.6 billion years. It is so big that it has hardly noticed.

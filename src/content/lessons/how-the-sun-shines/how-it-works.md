**An old puzzle.** In the 1800s nobody knew what kept the Sun hot. The best idea was that it was slowly shrinking, and that the squeeze warmed it. That would keep it going for a few tens of millions of years. Geologists and biologists, though, were finding that the Earth was far older than that. Something was missing.

The answer came in the 1920s and 1930s. Arthur Eddington pointed out that four hydrogen atoms weigh slightly more than one helium atom, and that if the Sun could turn one into the other, the missing weight would appear as energy. Hans Bethe and others then worked out how it could happen.

**The furnace.** In the Sun's core the temperature is nearly 16 million degrees and the gas is 150 times denser than water. Hydrogen nuclei, which are single protons, fly about at great speed. They all carry the same electric charge, so they push each other away, and even at this heat they should hardly ever touch. They manage it only because, on the very small scale, particles do not have sharp edges, and now and then two get close enough to merge. For any one proton that takes billions of years. The Sun has so many that it happens constantly.

Step by step, four protons become one helium nucleus. The helium has 0.7 per cent less mass than the protons that went in. That difference is the Sun's entire output.

**The slow way out.** The energy starts as gamma rays, and it does not get far. The inside of the Sun is so crowded that a ray of light travels a tiny distance before it is absorbed and sent off again in a new direction. It staggers outwards like someone lost in a crowd. Estimates of how long the journey takes run from about ten thousand years to more than a hundred thousand.

For the last third of the way the gas itself carries the heat, rising and sinking like water in a pan. At the visible surface the light finally gets clear, and reaches the Earth 8 minutes and 19 seconds later. The sunlight on your hand today is energy that was made before there were cities.

**Proof.** How can anyone know what goes on in the middle of the Sun? Fusion also makes neutrinos, ghostly particles that pass through almost anything. They leave the core and cross the whole Sun in about two seconds. Tens of billions of them go through every square centimetre of you each second, day and night, since the Earth does not stop them either.

In the 1960s Raymond Davis set out to catch a few, with a tank of cleaning fluid deep in a gold mine. He found them, which showed that fusion really is happening. But there were only about a third as many as predicted. For thirty years nobody knew whether the theory of the Sun was wrong or the theory of neutrinos. In 2001 an experiment in Canada settled it: the Sun was right. Neutrinos come in three types and change from one to another on the way here, and Davis's tank could see only one type.

**The sums.** Three short sums carry the whole argument, and each uses the Sun's measured output of 383 million million million million watts (380 in round figures).

Burning: coal gives about 30 million joules a kilogram, and the Sun has a mass of 2 million million million million million kilograms. Divide the total energy by the output and the answer is about 5,000 years.

Mass into energy: Einstein's rule says that energy is mass times the speed of light squared. Divide the Sun's output by the speed of light squared and the answer is 4.3 million tonnes a second.

Lifetime: only the innermost tenth of the Sun's hydrogen ever gets hot enough to fuse, and fusion turns 0.7 per cent of it into energy. That store, divided by the output, lasts about 10 billion years. The Sun is 4.6 billion years old, so it is about half-way.

**A surprisingly feeble furnace.** The Sun's core is not a raging explosion. At the very centre, each cubic metre makes about 276 watts. Volume for volume, that is about what a warm compost heap gives off, and less than a living human body. The Sun is bright because it is huge and because the heat takes so long to get out, not because any one part of it is working hard.

**The steps.** Fusion in the Sun goes in three stages. Two protons join to make a heavy form of hydrogen, and one of them turns into a neutron as they do so. This is the slow step that sets the pace of the whole Sun. That nucleus quickly picks up a third proton to make a light form of helium. Lastly, two of those collide and make one ordinary helium nucleus, throwing two protons back. In all, four protons go in and one helium nucleus comes out, with two neutrinos.

**The Sun is slowly getting brighter.** As hydrogen turns to helium, the core becomes denser and hotter and the fusion runs a little faster. The Sun gives out about 30 per cent more light now than it did when it formed. That slow rise will go on, and in about a billion years it will make the Earth too hot for its seas.

**Listening to the inside.** There is a second way of checking the core, besides neutrinos. The whole Sun rings like a bell. Sound waves cross its interior and make the surface rise and fall, in millions of overlapping notes, with periods of about five minutes. The exact notes depend on the temperature and density all the way down. They match the model of the Sun's interior to within a fraction of one per cent.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The Sun is hot enough for protons to smash together.* By the old rules of physics it is not, by a wide margin. Fusion happens only because of the fuzziness of very small things, described above.
- *Sunlight is 8 minutes old.* The trip from the surface takes 8 minutes. The energy itself was released in the core many thousands of years earlier.
- *The Sun is running out of fuel.* It has used about half of the hydrogen in its core and will go on as it is for about another 5 billion years.

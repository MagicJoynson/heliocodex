**Why there is a wind.** The Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, is so hot that the Sun's gravity cannot hold on to it. It boils away into space all the time. This is the solar wind: mostly protons and electrons, carrying some of the Sun's magnetic field along with them.

| The solar wind | |
|---|---|
| Speed | About 400 km a second, with faster streams of 700 or more |
| Time to reach the Earth | Two to four days |
| How thin | A few particles in each cubic centimetre by the time it gets here: far emptier than the best vacuum made in a laboratory |
| What the Sun loses to it | More than a million tonnes every second |

That loss sounds enormous and is not. Over its whole life so far the Sun has shed only about a hundredth of one per cent of its mass this way.

**What it does to planets.** The Earth has a strong magnetic field, which turns the wind aside well above the atmosphere. Mars has none to speak of. Measurements by NASA's MAVEN spacecraft show the wind stripping gas from the top of Mars's atmosphere today, and over billions of years this is thought to be a large part of how Mars lost the thick air it once had.

**Where it stops.** Space between the stars is not quite empty. It holds a very thin gas of its own, and the solar wind has to push against it. The wind thins out as it spreads, and at about 90 times the Earth's distance from the Sun it abruptly slows. Further out, at about 120 times, it stops altogether. That boundary is the heliopause, and everything inside it is the heliosphere.

Two spacecraft have crossed it.

| | Crossed the heliopause | At a distance of (Earth = 1) |
|---|---|---|
| Voyager 1 | August 2012 | 121 |
| Voyager 2 | November 2018 | 119 |

Both found the same things on the far side: the Sun's particles fell away, and the gas became denser and colder. Voyager 1 is now about 170 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is, and its radio signals take nearly a day to arrive.

**What the bubble does for us.** The galaxy is full of cosmic rays, fast and damaging particles from exploded stars. The heliosphere turns a large share of them away before they reach the planets.

**What is not known.** Only two spacecraft have sampled the boundary, at two points, so its overall shape is uncertain. It may trail a long tail like a comet, or be more rounded. NASA's IMAP spacecraft, launched in 2025, is mapping it from near the Earth.

**A prediction that was laughed at.** In 1958 a young American physicist, Eugene Parker, worked out that the corona is too hot to stay put and must flow outwards faster than sound, filling the whole Solar System. He said later that the sums took four lines of algebra. The paper was nearly rejected, and many astronomers thought the idea absurd. Four years later the Mariner 2 spacecraft, on its way to Venus, measured the wind and found it as he had described. In 2018 NASA named the Parker Solar Probe after him. It was the first time the agency had named a spacecraft after a living person.

**A worked example: how much the Sun loses.** The wind carries off about one and a half million tonnes a second. The Sun's light, by comparison, costs it about four million tonnes a second. Keep up the wind's rate for 4.6 billion years and the total is about one part in 10,000 of the Sun's mass (a sum), which is where the figure above comes from.

**Why the field is a spiral.** The wind drags the Sun's magnetic field out with it. The Sun turns once in about 27 days as seen from the Earth, so the field is wound into a spiral, like the jets of water from a spinning garden sprinkler. Each drop of the sprinkler's water flies straight outwards, and yet the pattern is a spiral. By the Earth's distance the field lies at about 45 degrees to the line from the Sun. Fast particles from solar storms follow these curved lines, which is why a storm on the western side of the Sun's face is the one most likely to reach us.

**The bubble breathes.** Before each Voyager reached the outer edge, it crossed the inner boundary where the wind slows abruptly. Voyager 1 crossed it in December 2004 at 94 times the Earth's distance. Voyager 2, leaving in a different direction, crossed it in 2007 at 84 times. The boundary is not a fixed sphere. It moves in and out as the Sun's wind strengthens and weakens over the eleven-year cycle, and it seems to be pushed in on one side.

**The cycle shows up on the ground.** Detectors on the Earth have counted cosmic rays since the 1950s. The count goes down when the Sun is active and up when it is quiet, a few months behind the sunspots. The active Sun blows a stronger, more tangled wind, and fewer cosmic rays get through it. Outside the bubble, Voyager 1 found the cosmic rays markedly more plentiful than they are near the Earth.

**The Sun is moving too.** The Sun is travelling through the thin gas between the stars at about 26 km a second. So the bubble is squashed on the side facing forwards and may be drawn out behind. Other stars have been seen to do the same thing: where a star with a strong wind moves through a gas cloud, telescopes show a curved bow of glowing gas ahead of it.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The solar wind and sunlight are the same thing.* Light leaves the Sun's surface and arrives in 8 minutes. The wind is matter from the corona and takes days.
- *The wind pushes the planets about.* Its push is far too small to matter to anything as heavy as a planet. It matters to gas, dust and comet tails.
- *Beyond the heliopause there is nothing.* There is the thin gas between the stars, which Voyager 1 found to be denser than the wind it had left.

**Not the edge of the Solar System.** The heliopause is where the Sun's wind ends. Its gravity reaches very much further. The Oort Cloud of comets is thought to stretch out hundreds of times beyond the heliopause, and the Voyagers will not be clear of it for tens of thousands of years.

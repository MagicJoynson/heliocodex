The Sun gives out more than light. It also blows a wind.

It is not a wind of air. It is a very thin stream of tiny electric particles, rushing away from the Sun in every direction at about 400 km every second.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/aurora-from-iss.jpg" alt="Curtains of green light hanging above the dark curve of the Earth, seen from orbit." width="1100" height="733" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Where the wind meets the Earth. The planet's magnet steers the particles towards the poles, where they strike the high air and make it glow. These are the southern lights over the Indian Ocean, from the International Space Station on 12 June 2025, with the Earth at the top as the camera took it. At 400 km a second the wind takes about four days to get here (worked out here). Credit: NASA.</figcaption>
</figure>

The wind blows past all the planets. It is what pushes a comet's blue tail straight back from the Sun.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/comet-hale-bopp-1997-eso.jpg" alt="A comet with a bright head and two tails against a field of stars: a straight blue one and a wide, curved, cream-coloured one." width="1100" height="773" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Comet Hale-Bopp on 14 March 1997. It has two tails. The straight blue one is gas, blown directly away from the Sun by the wind. The wide pale one is dust, which lags behind along the comet's path. Credit: ESO/E. Slawik.</figcaption>
</figure>

Far beyond the last planet, the wind finally runs out of push. That is where the Sun's own space ends and the space between the stars begins. The whole region inside is like a giant bubble with the Sun and planets in the middle.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/charts/suns-reach-distances.svg" alt="A chart of four bars: Neptune at 30, the edge of the bubble at about 120, Voyager 2 at 144 and Voyager 1 at 172." width="800" height="474" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A chart.</b> Distances from the Sun, counting the distance from the Sun to the Earth as 1. The edge is where the two Voyagers crossed it, in 2012 and 2018. Their distances are for 12 November 2026, from NASA's JPL Horizons service.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two spacecraft, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, have flown all the way out of the bubble. They were launched in 1977 and they are still going.


<figure class="photo">

  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Voyager 2 being tested on 23 March 1977, five months before it left. The white dish, 3.7 metres across, is how it still talks to the Earth. A radio signal from Voyager 1 now takes nearly a whole day to arrive. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech.</figcaption>
</figure>

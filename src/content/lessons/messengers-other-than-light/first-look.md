This building stands on the ice at the South Pole, and the telescope it belongs to is underneath it. It does not collect light from the sky. It waits for particles called neutrinos that have come up through the whole Earth. Light is still how nearly everything about space is learnt, but three other messengers now arrive as well.

Neutrinos are the hardest to stop. They are made wherever atoms are being built or broken, in the middle of the Sun above all, and they go through rock as if it were not there. Tens of billions of them from the Sun pass through every square centimetre of you each second, by night as well as by day, because at night they simply come up through the ground.

Catching one takes a huge detector and a lot of patience. Under that building, 5,160 light sensors hang on 86 cables frozen into the ice, between about one and a half and two and a half kilometres down. Together they watch a cubic kilometre of clear ice. Once in a long while a neutrino hits an atom in it and makes a faint streak of light, and the sensors record the streak.

Neutrinos bring news from places light cannot get out of. When this star blew up in 1987, three detectors caught 25 neutrinos from its collapsing core. They were recorded before anyone saw the light, because light has to work its way out through the star and neutrinos do not.

Cosmic rays are the second messenger. They are the bare centres of atoms, mostly hydrogen, flung across space at nearly the speed of light. When one hits the top of the air it breaks into a shower of smaller particles miles long, and it is the shower that is caught. In 1991 one single particle arrived carrying as much energy as a hard tennis serve.

The third messenger is a ripple in space itself, set off when very heavy things collide. On 17 August 2017 the ripples from two neutron stars crashing together reached the Earth, and 1.7 seconds later so did a flash of gamma rays. Telescopes then found this fading glow in a galaxy 130 million light years away. It was the first event caught by ripples and by light together.

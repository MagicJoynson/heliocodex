The space round the Sun is the one part of the galaxy where we can count nearly every star, including the faint ones. That makes it the fairest sample we have of what stars are really like.

**The nearest.** These are the named stars within 10 light years in the catalogue this site draws. The distances are the catalogue's, rounded, except Alpha Centauri's 4.4, which comes from a newer measurement (1.338 parsecs) than the catalogue holds. Brightness is visible light only, compared with the Sun.

| Star | Distance | Visible light, Sun = 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Proxima Centauri | 4.2 light years | About 1/17,000 |
| Alpha Centauri A (Rigil Kentaurus) | 4.4 | 1.5 |
| Alpha Centauri B (Toliman) | 4.4 | 0.4 |
| Barnard's Star | 5.9 | About 1/2,300 |
| Wolf 359 | 7.8 | About 1/50,000 |
| Lalande 21185 | 8.3 | About 1/180 |
| Sirius | 8.6 | 22 |
| Ross 154 | 9.7 | About 1/1,900 |

Two of the eight outshine the Sun. Five are red dwarfs, hundreds or thousands of times fainter. The column counts visible light only. Cool stars also give out infrared, which the eye cannot see and this column leaves out.

**How the distances are known.** As the Earth goes round the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift a little against the distant ones. The size of the shift gives the distance directly, with no guesswork about the star itself. This is parallax. Proxima's shift is 0.768 arcseconds, the largest of any star, and even that is tiny. The Gaia spacecraft has measured such shifts for well over a billion stars.

**The nearest system is three stars.** Alpha Centauri A and B are a close pair, and A gives out about one and a half times the Sun's visible light. Proxima goes round the two of them a long way out, about once in 550,000 years. More than half of all stars have at least one partner, so a single star like the Sun is not the rule.

**A planet next door.** Proxima has a planet of about the mass of the Earth. It goes round every 11.2 days, at a distance where water could be liquid. Whether it has air or water is not known.

**Why the sky misleads.** A star's brightness in the sky depends on two things: how much light it gives out and how far away it is. Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky mostly because it is close. Rigel, in Orion, gives out about 2,000 times more light from a hundred times further off. Meanwhile most of the stars within 20 light years cannot be seen at all. Count the stars you can see and you would think most stars are giants. Count the stars that are really there and the typical star is small, red and dim.

**How far that is.** Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is the most distant thing people have made. After nearly fifty years it is about one light day from the Sun. Proxima is 1,550 times further.

**Small and long-lived.** Red dwarfs are the smallest stars that shine by fusion, a fraction of the Sun's size and mass. They use their fuel so slowly, and stir it so thoroughly, that they can last for trillions of years. Some are expected to live longer than the universe has existed so far.

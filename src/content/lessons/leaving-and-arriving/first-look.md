Space is not far away. It starts about 100 km above your head, closer than many towns are to each other.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/charts/how-high-is-space.svg" alt="A chart of four bars: Mount Everest 9 km, an airliner 11 km, where space begins 100 km, and the Space Station 420 km." width="800" height="490" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A chart.</b> How high things are. Space begins closer to the ground than London is to Birmingham.</figcaption>
</figure>

The hard part is not getting up there. It is going fast enough to stay. To keep circling the Earth without falling back, a spacecraft has to travel at about 28,000 km an hour. That is why rockets are so big: nearly all of a rocket is fuel, burned to reach that speed.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/artemis-ii-booster-separation.jpg" alt="Two slim white rockets with orange flames tumble away to left and right against a dark blue sky. Below them is a small cluster of four bright white lights." width="912" height="1300" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Two empty boosters fall away from the Space Launch System shortly after lift-off on 1 April 2026. The four lights are the engines of the main stage, still pushing the four people of Artemis II towards the speed they need. Credit: NASA.</figcaption>
</figure>

Once it is going fast enough, a spacecraft can switch its engines off. There is no air to slow it down, so it just keeps coasting, for years if need be.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/iss-over-earth.jpg" alt="The International Space Station, with its long solar panels spread out, above blue sea and white cloud." width="1100" height="728" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Coasting. The International Space Station, photographed from the Space Shuttle Atlantis on 23 May 2010. It has no engines running. It is simply moving sideways at about 28,000 km an hour, fast enough to keep missing the ground. Credit: NASA.</figcaption>
</figure>

To go to another planet, you do not aim at where the planet is now. You aim at where it will be when you arrive, months later, like throwing a ball to someone who is running.

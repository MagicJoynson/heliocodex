**Two speeds.** Two numbers govern leaving any world.

Orbital speed is how fast you must go sideways to keep falling round it without coming down. Just above the Earth's air that is about 7.8 km a second, and one lap takes about 90 minutes.

Escape speed is how fast you must go to leave for good, coasting, with no further push. It is always about 1.4 times the orbital speed at the same height.

| Leaving from the surface of | Escape speed |
|---|---|
| The Moon | 2.4 km a second |
| Mars | 5.0 km a second |
| The Earth | 11.2 km a second |
| Jupiter | About 60 km a second |
| The Sun | 618 km a second |

The small figure for the Moon is why the Apollo crews could leave it in a craft the size of a van, when leaving the Earth had taken a rocket 110 m tall.

**Why rockets are mostly fuel.** A rocket has to carry its fuel with it, and then carry the fuel needed to lift that fuel. The sums are unforgiving. The Saturn V that sent people to the Moon weighed nearly 3,000 tonnes on the launch pad, and well over four fifths of that was propellant. Less than 50 tonnes went on towards the Moon.

**Coasting between planets.** After the engines stop, a spacecraft is simply in orbit round the Sun, like a very small planet. The cheapest route to another planet is half of a long oval orbit that touches the Earth's orbit at one end and the target's at the other. A short burn at the start puts the craft on the oval, and it coasts the rest of the way.

It is slow, and it only works when the planets are in the right places. A trip to Mars this way takes seven to nine months, and the chance to set off comes round only every 26 months. That is why Mars missions leave in clusters.

**Borrowing speed.** A spacecraft that passes close behind a moving planet is pulled along by it and leaves faster than it arrived, as measured from the Sun. The planet slows down in exchange, by an amount far too small ever to measure. This is a gravity assist, and it costs no fuel.

Voyager 2 is the famous case. Launched in 1977, it used Jupiter to reach Saturn, Saturn to reach Uranus and Uranus to reach Neptune, arriving in 1989 after twelve years. Without the planets' help the journey would have taken about thirty. The four giants line up that conveniently only about once every 175 years.

The trick works for slowing down too. Passing in front of a planet takes speed away. The Parker Solar Probe flew past Venus seven times to shed enough speed to drop close to the Sun, which is harder to reach than the edge of the Solar System.

**Arriving.** Getting there is half the job. A spacecraft arriving at a planet is going too fast to stay, and must slow down to be captured: by firing its engine, or by skimming the top of the atmosphere and letting drag do the work. If it does neither, it swings past and carries on.

**A worked example: the road to Mars.** The Earth is 1 unit from the Sun and Mars is 1.52, where the unit is the Earth's distance. The oval that touches both has an average distance from the Sun of 1.26 units. The rule that Johannes Kepler found for planets works for spacecraft too: cube the distance and take the square root to get the time for one lap in years. That gives 1.42 years. The craft travels only half the oval, so the trip takes 0.71 of a year, which is 259 days, or eight and a half months.

In that time Mars moves 136 degrees round its own orbit. So the craft has to leave when Mars is 44 degrees ahead of the Earth, in order to meet it on the far side of the Sun. The Earth, on its faster inside track, gains a full lap on Mars every 780 days, and that is the 26-month wait between chances (sums from the two planets' years, 365.26 and 686.98 days).

**The rocket sum.** In 1903 the Russian teacher Konstantin Tsiolkovsky worked out the rule that every rocket obeys. What matters is how fast the exhaust leaves the engine. Each time a rocket gains that much speed, it has had to burn enough fuel to shrink to about a third of the weight it had before (the exact factor is 2.72).

Reaching orbit takes about 9.4 km a second in all, counting what is lost to gravity and air on the way up. The best ordinary fuel, hydrogen burned with oxygen, leaves at about 4.4 km a second. The sum then says a rocket must be 88 per cent fuel at lift-off. With kerosene, which leaves at about 3 km a second, it must be 96 per cent, which leaves almost nothing for tanks, engines and cargo. This is why rockets are built in stages. Dropping an empty stage means the fuel that is left has less to push.

**Why the Sun is the hardest place to reach.** The Earth goes round the Sun at 29.8 km a second, and anything launched from it starts with that sideways speed. To fall into the Sun a spacecraft would have to cancel nearly all of it. To leave the Solar System from here it needs to reach 42.1 km a second, which is only another 12.3 (a sum: escape speed is 1.414 times orbital speed). Dropping to the Sun takes more than twice the change of speed that leaving it behind does. Hence the Parker Solar Probe's seven passes of Venus. At its closest to the Sun it travels at about 700,000 km an hour, close to 200 km a second, faster than anything else ever built.

**How far coasting gets you.** Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is leaving at about 17 km a second. It is expected to reach a distance of one light-day from the Earth, 25.9 billion km, on about 18 November 2026. Its radio signals will then take a full day to arrive. The nearest star is 4.24 light-years away, which is more than 1,500 light-days. At Voyager's speed that would be a journey of about 75,000 years (a sum).

**Arriving in practice.** NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter reached Mars in March 2006 and fired its engine to be captured into a long, looping orbit. It then spent six months dipping into the top of the Martian air on every lap, losing a little speed each time, until the orbit was the low circle it needed. That saved a great weight of fuel.

Landing is harder. The air of Mars is thick enough to burn a spacecraft up and too thin to stop it. The Perseverance rover met the air at about 20,000 km an hour in February 2021 and was on the ground seven minutes later, having used a heat shield, a parachute and finally rockets.

Small errors matter. In 1999 the Mars Climate Orbiter was lost on arrival because one team's software gave thruster figures in pound-force seconds and another's read them as newton-seconds, a difference of 4.45 times. The craft came in too low and was destroyed.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The engines burn all the way.* They burn for minutes. The rest of a journey of months or years is coasting.
- *Being in orbit means being beyond gravity.* An orbit is made by gravity. Without it the craft would fly off in a straight line.
- *A gravity assist is energy from nowhere.* The planet pays for it and slows down, by an amount too small to measure.
- *You fly straight at the planet.* Everything out there is moving in curves round the Sun, the spacecraft included. Every route is a piece of an orbit.

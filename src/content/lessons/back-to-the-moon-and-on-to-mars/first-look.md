Twelve people walked on the Moon between July 1969 and December 1972. Then nobody went back. For more than fifty years every person who left the ground stayed within a few hundred kilometres of it, going round and round the Earth.

That changed in April 2026, when the four people of Artemis II flew round the far side of the Moon and came home. They did not land. Landing is the next step, and this time the plan is not just to visit but to stay.

**Why go back at all?** Apollo answered the first questions: what the Moon is made of, how old it is, and whether people could get there and home again. It did not answer whether people could live there. That needs water, air and fuel, and carrying them all from the Earth costs a fortune. Every kilogram has to be lifted out of the Earth's gravity first.

**Ice in the dark.** The Moon's axis is hardly tilted at all, so near its poles the Sun never climbs far above the horizon. The floors of some deep craters there have not seen sunlight for billions of years. They are among the coldest places known in the Solar System: some are colder than −230 °C.

In October 2009 a spacecraft called LCROSS fired a spent rocket stage into one of those craters, Cabeus, and flew through the cloud it threw up. The cloud held water. Other spacecraft have since found the signs of ice on the surface in the shadows round both poles.

Ice is the most useful thing that could be found there. Melted, it is water to drink. Split by electricity, it becomes oxygen to breathe and hydrogen, and those two together are rocket fuel. A base that can make its own fuel could also become a filling station for ships going further out.

**The plan, as it stood in November 2026.** NASA's next crewed flight, Artemis III, is meant to stay close to home in 2027: astronauts will practise docking their Orion capsule with the landers that will one day take them down. The first landing is now planned for Artemis IV, in 2028, near the south pole. Two companies are building landers, SpaceX and Blue Origin. China is building its own rocket, capsule and lander, and aims to put two people on the Moon by 2030. All of these dates are plans, and plans in spaceflight often slip.

**Then Mars.** Mars is a very different journey. The Moon is about three days away. Mars is about seven months away, and a ship can only set off when the two planets line up, which happens about every 26 months. A crew could not turn round halfway. Once there they might have to wait more than a year for the planets to line up again for the trip home.

The air on Mars is thin, less than one hundredth as thick as the Earth's, and almost all carbon dioxide. It cannot be breathed, but it can be used.

Between 2021 and 2023 MOXIE made oxygen on Mars sixteen times, 122 grams in all. That is about what a small dog breathes in ten hours. It is a tiny amount, but it was the first time anything useful had been made from another planet's air. To fill the tanks of a rocket home, a crew would need a far bigger machine, and a way to turn the oxygen into a liquid and store it.

**Two common mistakes.** Going back to the Moon is not a repeat of Apollo. Apollo landed near the Moon's equator, where it is easier to reach and well lit, and stayed a few days at most. The new plans aim for the poles because of the ice, and for stays that grow longer each time. And Mars is not just a longer trip to the Moon. Help from the Earth is months away, and even a radio message takes between about 3 and 22 minutes to arrive, so a crew there would have to solve their own problems.

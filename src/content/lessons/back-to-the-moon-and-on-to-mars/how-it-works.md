**The plan, as it stood on 13 November 2026.** Every date after today is a target, not a promise.

| Flight | What it is for |
|---|---|
| **Artemis I**, 16 November 2022 | No crew. The Orion capsule went round the Moon and came home |
| **Artemis II**, 1 to 10 April 2026 | Four people round the far side of the Moon and back, without landing |
| **Artemis III**, planned for 2027 | A crew stays in orbit round the Earth and practises docking Orion with test versions of the two landers |
| **Artemis IV**, planned for 2028 | The first landing since 1972, near the Moon's south pole |
| **Artemis V**, planned for 2028 | The second landing, and the start of a base |

Until February 2026 Artemis III was to be the landing. NASA changed it to a test closer to home so that each new piece is tried before people depend on it. In March 2026 NASA also said it would pause the Gateway, a small station that was to circle the Moon, and put its hardware towards a base on the surface near the south pole instead.

The landers come from two companies. SpaceX's is a version of its Starship. It is so big that it must be refilled with fuel in orbit round the Earth, by other Starships, before it can go to the Moon, and that refilling had not yet been done by November 2026. Blue Origin's lander, Blue Moon, is smaller and is to be carried to the Moon by three smaller craft of its own. Both must land on the Moon without a crew before anyone rides in them.

China is following its own path. It is building a rocket, Long March 10, a capsule, Mengzhou, and a lander, Lanyue. In August 2025 a full-size Lanyue was tested on the ground, landing on a model of the Moon's surface and taking off again. China aims to land two people by 2030.

**Why fuel made on the Moon is worth so much.** To leave a world for good, a spacecraft has to reach its escape velocity. From the Earth that is 11.2 km a second. From the Moon it is 2.38 km a second. The energy needed for each kilogram goes with the square of the speed, so lifting a kilogram clear of the Moon takes about 22 times less energy than lifting it clear of the Earth (a sum: 11.2 ÷ 2.38 = 4.7, and 4.7 × 4.7 = 22). There is no air on the Moon to push through either. Water, oxygen and fuel made there and sent onwards start with a huge head start.

**How much water, and where.** In October 2009 the LCROSS spacecraft steered its spent rocket stage into Cabeus, a crater near the south pole that never sees the Sun. LCROSS flew through the cloud of material thrown up and measured it before it hit the crater too. About 5.6 per cent of the material, by weight, was water. In 2018 a team reading data from an instrument on India's Chandrayaan-1 spacecraft found the fingerprint of ice lying in the open, in shadowed places near both poles. How much ice there is in all, and how deep it goes, is still not known. Finding out is one of the main jobs for the robot landers going there first.

The shadows exist because the Moon hardly tilts. Its equator sits only about 1.5 degrees from the plane of its path round the Sun, compared with 23.4 degrees for the Earth. Near the poles the Sun always stays low, and deep crater floors stay dark for ever.

**Turning water into air and fuel.** Electricity splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. By weight, 9 kg of water gives 1 kg of hydrogen and 8 kg of oxygen, because an oxygen atom is 16 times heavier than a hydrogen atom and water has two hydrogens for each oxygen. That suits rockets well: an engine burning hydrogen uses several kilograms of oxygen for every kilogram of hydrogen. The same oxygen is what a crew breathes. The electricity has to come from somewhere. Near the pole there are ridges that are in sunlight most of the time, which is another reason to go there.

**Living on the surface.** Moon gravity is about a sixth of the Earth's. The ground is covered in regolith, broken rock and dust that has never been worn smooth by wind or water. Its grains are sharp and cling to everything: the Apollo crews found it in their suits, their seals and their lungs. There is no air to stop the solar wind or fast particles from space, so a base would need shielding, perhaps by piling regolith on top. Away from the poles a night lasts about two weeks.

**Getting to Mars.** The planets move, so a ship aims for where Mars will be. The cheapest path is a transfer orbit, half an ellipse that touches the Earth's orbit at one end and Mars's at the other. The Earth must be in the right place for that path to meet Mars, and that happens about every 26 months, because that is how long the Earth takes to catch up with Mars again. Perseverance left on 30 July 2020 and landed on 18 February 2021, 203 days later.

**A long way to call home.** Mars is 54.6 million km from the Earth at the very closest and about 401 million km at the furthest. Radio travels at the speed of light, so a message takes between about 3 minutes and 22 minutes (a sum: 401,000,000 km ÷ 299,792 km a second = 1,338 seconds, or 22.3 minutes). A question and its answer take twice that. For the Moon the light-time is 1.3 seconds.

**Radiation on the way.** On its way to Mars in 2011 and 2012, the Curiosity rover carried a radiation counter inside its spacecraft. From what it measured, a round trip of about 360 days in space, not counting any time on Mars, would give a person about 0.66 sieverts. NASA now limits the dose over an astronaut's whole career to 0.6 sieverts, the same for everyone whatever their age or sex. So the journey alone would use up more than a career's limit, unless better shielding or a faster trip can be found, or the limit is set aside for such a mission.

**Landing heavy things.** The heaviest thing yet landed on Mars is Perseverance, at about 1,025 kg. Mars has enough air to heat a spacecraft coming in fast, but too little to slow a heavy one down with parachutes alone, so Perseverance was lowered the last part of the way on cables by a hovering rocket crane. A crewed ship with its supplies would weigh many tonnes, and nobody has yet landed anything near that size on Mars.

**Air on Mars.** The air on Mars is about 95 per cent carbon dioxide, at less than one hundredth of the pressure on the Earth. Between 2021 and 2023 MOXIE, on board Perseverance, pulled in that air, split the carbon dioxide and kept the oxygen. It ran 16 times and made 122 grams, at best 12 grams an hour and at least 98 per cent pure. The next step would be a machine big enough to fill the tanks of a rocket home, with a way to turn the oxygen to liquid and store it.

**Two common mistakes.** The Moon's ice is not a frozen lake. Where it has been measured it is mixed into the soil, a few per cent by weight, and getting it out means digging, heating and cleaning. And a Mars mission cannot simply leave whenever it is ready. If it misses the window, it waits about two years for the next one.

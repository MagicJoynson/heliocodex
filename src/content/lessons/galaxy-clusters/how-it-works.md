| What is in a cluster | How it is seen | How much |
|---|---|---|
| Galaxies | Ordinary light | Hundreds to thousands of them, and the smallest share of the mass |
| Hot gas between the galaxies | X-rays | More mass than all the galaxies together |
| Dark matter | Only by its gravity | About six times as much as the galaxies and gas together |

**Why the gas is so hot.** Gas falling into a cluster picks up enormous speed, and when clusters and groups run into each other the gas is squeezed and shocked. All that motion ends up as heat. The gas is very thin, far thinner than the best vacuum on the Earth, but a cluster is so large that there is a great deal of it.

**Why the galaxies in the middle are yellow ovals.** In the crowded centre of a cluster most galaxies are ellipticals, with old stars and little gas left to make new ones. Spirals like ours are commoner further out. See [Kinds of galaxy](/learn/kinds-of-galaxy).

**The Bullet Cluster.** The X-ray picture in this lesson shows two clusters that have run straight through each other, about 3.8 billion light years away. The bright cone on the right is the gas of the smaller one. In the crash the two clouds of gas dragged on each other and slowed down. The galaxies, with wide gaps between them, mostly sailed through. When astronomers mapped where the mass is, using the bending of light, they found it with the galaxies, well ahead of the gas. So most of the mass is neither gas nor galaxies, and it does not drag on anything. That is the plainest evidence there is that dark matter is real stuff, and not a mistake in the law of gravity. See [Dark matter and dark energy](/learn/dark-matter-and-dark-energy).

**Groups, clusters, superclusters.** A group has a few dozen galaxies. A cluster has hundreds or thousands and is held together for good. Superclusters are larger still, but they are loose gatherings that the expansion of the universe is pulling apart. See [The cosmic web](/learn/the-cosmic-web).

**The first sign of dark matter.** In 1933 the Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky measured how fast the galaxies of the Coma Cluster were moving, using the shifts of the lines in their light. They were going far too fast. With only the mass he could see, the cluster should have flown apart long ago. He wrote that it must be held together by "dunkle Materie", dark matter. Hardly anyone took the idea seriously for decades.

**Two famous clusters.** The Virgo Cluster is the nearest large one. Its centre is about 54 million light years away and it has about 1,300 galaxies, perhaps as many as 2,000. Our own Local Group lies on its outskirts. The Coma Cluster is about 320 million light years away and has more than 1,000 identified galaxies.

**A worked example: why the gas gives out X-rays.** The galaxies in a big cluster move at about 1,000 km a second, and the gas between them is held by the same gravity, so its particles move at about that speed too. For a gas, speed is temperature. A gas of hydrogen whose particles move at 1,000 km a second is at about 40 million degrees (a sum, from the mass of a hydrogen nucleus). Anything that hot gives out X-rays, not visible light, which is why this gas went unseen until X-ray telescopes were put in orbit in the 1970s.

**Three ways to weigh a cluster.** The speeds of the galaxies give one answer. The temperature of the gas gives a second, because hotter gas needs stronger gravity to hold it. The third uses light itself. A cluster's gravity bends the light of galaxies far behind it and smears them into thin arcs, and the amount of bending gives the mass. The three methods use different physics and they agree, to within their margins. All three say that the cluster holds several times more mass than its stars and gas can account for.

**Nature's telescopes.** The bending has a use. A cluster acts as a lens, and it makes the galaxies behind it look brighter and larger. Many of the most distant galaxies ever seen were found by pointing the Hubble and Webb telescopes through a cluster on purpose.

**The deepest note.** In 2003 the Chandra X-ray telescope found ripples in the gas of the Perseus Cluster, spreading out from the giant black hole at its centre. They are sound waves. Their pitch is a B flat, 57 octaves below middle C, which is the deepest note ever detected. A piano has about seven octaves. The waves carry energy outwards and help to keep the gas hot.

**Still growing.** Clusters are the largest things that gravity has finished pulling together, and they are the most recent. A big one holds as much mass as a thousand million million Suns. They grow by swallowing groups and smaller clusters that fall in along the threads of the cosmic web, and the Bullet Cluster is one such meeting caught in the act. The Virgo Cluster is still gathering galaxies today.

**Shadows in the oldest light.** There is a way of finding clusters without seeing a single galaxy. The glow left over from the early universe has to cross every cluster on its way here. As it passes through the hot gas, some of it is knocked to a slightly higher energy, and that leaves a faint dent in the microwave map at the place where the cluster is. The Planck spacecraft found more than a thousand clusters this way. The method works just as well for far clusters as for near ones, because the dent does not fade with distance. See [The oldest light](/learn/the-oldest-light).

**Common mistakes.**

- *A galaxy cluster is a kind of star cluster.* A star cluster is a group of stars inside one galaxy. A galaxy cluster is a group of whole galaxies, millions of times bigger.
- *The galaxies in a cluster are all flying apart with the expansion of the universe.* Inside a cluster, gravity has won. The cluster holds together and does not expand.
- *The space between the galaxies is empty.* It holds most of the cluster's ordinary matter, as hot gas.

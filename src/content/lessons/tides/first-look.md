The Moon's gravity pulls on the whole Earth, but not evenly. The side of the Earth facing the Moon is nearer to it and is pulled a little harder. The far side is further off and is pulled a little less.

That uneven pull stretches the Earth slightly along the line towards the Moon. The oceans, which are free to move, pile up into two bulges: one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side.

The Earth spins underneath the two bulges once a day. Each stretch of coast passes through both, which is why most places get two high tides a day and not one.

The same stretching, over a very long time, slowed the Moon's own spin until it kept one face towards us. And at Jupiter, tides squeeze the moon Io so hard that its inside melts, making it the most volcanic place we know.

**Why is there a bulge on the far side?** This is the part that puzzles everyone. Think of three things in a row: the water nearest the Moon, the solid Earth, and the water furthest from the Moon. The Moon pulls hardest on the near water, a little less on the Earth, and least on the far water. So the near water is drawn ahead of the Earth, and the Earth is drawn ahead of the far water, which is left behind. Seen from the ground, being left behind looks just like bulging outward.

**Why the tide is later each day.** While the Earth turns once, the Moon moves on a little in its orbit, so a place needs an extra 50 minutes to catch up with it. High tide therefore comes about 50 minutes later each day, and the gap from one high tide to the next is about 12 hours 25 minutes. This is why seaside towns print tide tables.

**Big tides and small tides.** The Sun raises tides too, a little under half as strong as the Moon's. At full Moon and new Moon the two line up and work together, and the tides are at their biggest. At half Moon they pull across each other and the tides are small.

**Why a lake has no tide.** A tide needs the Moon's pull to differ from one side of the water to the other, and that takes an ocean thousands of kilometres across. Even the Great Lakes of North America, which are hundreds of kilometres long, have a tide of less than 5 cm, and it is lost in the ripples from the wind. A pond, a cup of tea and a human body have no tide worth the name.

**The ground has tides too.** The rock of the Earth is stretched by the same pull. Twice a day the ground under you rises and falls by something like 30 cm. Nobody notices, because everything in sight rises and falls together.

**A common mistake.** The Moon does not lift the sea straight up. Its stretching pull is feeble: on a person it amounts to about a nine-millionth of their weight (a sum from the Moon's mass and distance). What it can do, across a whole ocean, is draw water sideways towards the two bulges, and that is what the tide is.

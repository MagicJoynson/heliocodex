**It is the difference that counts.** A tide comes from the difference in pull between the near and far sides of a body, and that difference falls away with the cube of the distance, much faster than gravity itself. This is why the Moon beats the Sun. The Sun pulls on the Earth about 180 times harder than the Moon does, but it is 390 times further away, and its tide is a little under half as strong.

**Spring and neap.** At new and full Moon the Sun and Moon pull along the same line and their tides add up. These are spring tides, with the highest highs and lowest lows. At half Moon they pull at right angles and partly cancel, giving the weaker neap tides. The pattern repeats twice in every 29.5 days.

**Timing.** The Moon moves on round its orbit while the Earth turns, so a place on the Earth takes 24 hours 50 minutes to come back under it. High tides are therefore about 12 hours 25 minutes apart and arrive roughly 50 minutes later each day.

**Size.** In mid-ocean the bulge is only about half a metre high. Coasts change everything. Where the sea is funnelled into a narrowing bay the water heaps up: the range reaches about 16 metres in the Bay of Fundy in Canada and about 14 metres in the Severn Estuary.

**The price.** The bulges rub against the sea floor as the Earth turns, and that friction slows the spin. The day gets longer by about 2 thousandths of a second each century. In exchange the Moon is pushed outward. Lasers bounced off mirrors left on its surface by the Apollo crews show it moving away by 3.8 cm a year.

**Locked.** The Earth raises a tide in the solid Moon as well, and long ago that brought the Moon's spin to match its orbit, so the same face always points at us. Almost every large moon in the Solar System has ended up the same way.

**Io.** Io is locked too, but its orbit is kept slightly oval by the regular tugs of its neighbours Europa and Ganymede. So its distance from Jupiter keeps changing, and its tidal bulge keeps rising and falling, by as much as 100 metres of solid rock. The rubbing heats the interior, and Io has hundreds of active volcanoes to show for it.

**The sum behind the Sun and the Moon.** The Sun has 27 million times the mass of the Moon and is 389 times as far away. Ordinary pull goes down with the square of the distance: 389 × 389 is about 151,000, and 27 million divided by that is about 180. So the Sun pulls on the Earth 180 times harder than the Moon does, which is why the Earth orbits the Sun. The tide goes down with the cube: 389 × 389 × 389 is about 59 million, and 27 million divided by that is 0.46. So the Sun's tide is 46 per cent of the Moon's. At spring tides the two add to 1.46, and at neaps they leave 0.54, so in theory a spring tide is about 2.7 times a neap. All of these are sums from NASA's fact sheets.

**Why the real tide is nothing like two neat bulges.** To stay under the Moon, a bulge of water would have to sweep round the equator at about 1,600 km/h. But a long wave in an ocean 3.6 km deep cannot travel faster than about 680 km/h (a sum from the depth and the strength of gravity). The water cannot keep up, and the continents are in the way besides. So the tide does not follow the Moon round the world. It sloshes round each ocean basin like water in a carried bowl, driven by the Moon's rhythm but arriving at each coast at its own time. In some places high tide comes when the Moon is overhead, in others six hours later. That is why tides are forecast from years of measurements at each port, not from the Moon's position alone.

**The same pull, different coasts.** Most places get two tides a day. A few, such as parts of the Gulf of Mexico, get only one, because of the shape of the basin. The Mediterranean is nearly cut off from the ocean and has tides of a few tens of centimetres.

**How long the day used to be.** If the day is growing longer, it used to be shorter, and the rocks agree. Some ancient muds were laid down in thin layers, one for each tide, with thicker bands at spring tides. Counting the layers in rocks 620 million years old gives a day of about 21.9 hours and about 400 days in a year. The year itself was the same length. There were more days in it because each one was shorter.

**Where the energy goes.** The spin the Earth loses mostly ends up as heat, as tidal currents drag over shallow sea floors. A little can be caught. The power station across the estuary of the Rance in Brittany opened in 1966 and its 24 turbines can give 240 megawatts as the tide runs in and out.

**Tides that destroy.** A tide stretches. If a body comes close enough to a planet, the stretch can beat the body's own gravity and pull it apart. In July 1992 a comet passed very close to Jupiter and was torn into more than 20 pieces. They were found the next year, strung out in a line, and named Shoemaker-Levy 9. In July 1994 the pieces hit Jupiter one after another. The same stretching is why rings lie close to their planets, where no large moon could hold together.

**Both ends locked.** The Moon is locked to the Earth, but the Earth is not locked to the Moon: it is too heavy to have been slowed that far yet. Pluto and its moon Charon have finished the job. Each keeps the same face to the other, so from one side of Pluto, Charon hangs for ever in the same place in the sky, and from the other side it is never seen at all.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The Moon pulls on the Earth harder than the Sun does.* The Sun pulls about 180 times harder. The Moon wins at tides only because it is so much closer that its pull differs more from one side of the Earth to the other.
- *High tide comes when the Moon is overhead.* Only on some coasts, as explained above.
- *Tides are something only water does.* Rock and air have them too, and tides in rock are what heat Io and lock moons.

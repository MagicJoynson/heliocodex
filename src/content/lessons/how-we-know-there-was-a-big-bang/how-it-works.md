| The evidence | What was predicted | What was found |
|---|---|---|
| Galaxies moving apart | Speed in step with distance | About 21 km a second faster for every million light years further off |
| The oldest light | A faint, even glow from the whole sky | Microwaves at 2.725 degrees above absolute zero, the same in every direction to 1 part in 100,000 |
| The first elements | About a quarter helium by mass, nearly all the rest hydrogen | 24.5 per cent helium in gas hardly touched by stars |
| A changing past | Far-off galaxies should look younger | They are smaller and less orderly |

**Each of these has its own lesson.** See [The expanding universe](/learn/the-expanding-universe), [The oldest light](/learn/the-oldest-light) and [The first minutes](/learn/the-first-minutes).

**The photograph.** The Hubble Ultra Deep Field is a patch of sky about a tenth as wide as the full Moon. Hubble stared at it for a total of 11.3 days between September 2003 and January 2004. Nearly 10,000 galaxies showed up. About a hundred of the smallest and reddest are seen as they were when the universe was about 800 million years old.

**The name.** "Big Bang" is misleading. It was not an explosion into empty space, because there was no space outside it. Every part of space began stretching away from every other part, everywhere at once. There is no centre and no edge to point at.

**What the theory does not cover.** It describes how the hot, dense universe expanded and cooled. It does not describe the very first instant, where the known laws of physics stop working, and it does not say why there was anything to expand.

**A fifth clue anyone can check: the sky is dark at night.** It sounds too plain to be evidence. But suppose the universe had no beginning, went on for ever in every direction, and was filled with stars. Then whichever way you looked, your line of sight would end on the surface of a star sooner or later, and the whole sky would blaze as bright as the Sun. It does not. The reason is that the universe has an age. Light has had only 13.8 billion years to travel, so stars beyond a certain distance cannot be seen yet, and the stretching of space dims and reddens the light of the far ones that can. A dark night sky is what a universe with a beginning looks like.

**The rival that lost.** For twenty years the Big Bang had a serious competitor. The steady state theory, put forward in 1948, agreed that the universe was expanding but said it had always existed and always looked the same, with new matter slowly appearing to fill the gaps. It was a good theory in the sense that matters: it made predictions that could be checked. It said there should be no leftover glow, and that far-off galaxies should look just like near ones. The glow was found in 1964, and counts of distant radio galaxies showed that the past was different. Most astronomers gave the steady state up within a few years. The name "Big Bang" was coined by one of its authors, Fred Hoyle, in a BBC radio talk in March 1949, as a way of describing the idea he did not believe.

**Checking the age two ways.** If everything began 13.8 billion years ago, nothing in the universe should be older than that. The test is fair because the two ages are measured by quite different methods. The age of the universe comes from its expansion and from the pattern in the oldest light. The ages of stars come from the physics of how stars burn their fuel. The oldest stars known, in the round clusters that circle our galaxy, come out at about 12 to 13 billion years. In the 1990s some measurements made the stars look older than the universe, which would have sunk the theory. Better distances to the stars, and the discovery that the expansion is speeding up, removed the clash.

**A rough age from the expansion itself.** Galaxies recede at about 21 km a second for every million light years of distance. A million light years is about 9.5 million million million km. Divide that distance by that speed and the answer is the time it would take to cover it: about 14 billion years (a sum). So if the galaxies had always moved apart at today's rate, they would all have been in the same place about 14 billion years ago. The careful figure, 13.8 billion, allows for the rate having changed.

**Who found what.**

| Year | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1927 | Georges Lemaître | Showed from Einstein's equations that the universe could be expanding, and linked that to the measured speeds of galaxies |
| 1929 | Edwin Hubble | Published the measurements: speed goes up in step with distance |
| 1948 | Ralph Alpher, Robert Herman and George Gamow | Worked out the making of the first elements, and predicted a leftover glow |
| 1964 | Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson | Found the glow, by accident. They won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1978 |
| 1992 | The COBE satellite team | Found the faint warm and cool patches in the glow from which the galaxies grew |

**Common mistakes.**

- *The Big Bang happened at a place.* It happened everywhere. Every point in today's universe was part of it, including the spot where you are sitting.
- *The universe is expanding into something.* Nothing in the theory requires an outside. Distances between galaxies grow. That is all the word means.
- *Everything is expanding, including us.* Only the space between groups of galaxies stretches. Atoms, people, planets and galaxies are held together by forces far stronger than the stretching.
- *It is "only a theory".* In science a theory is not a guess. It is an explanation that has been tested hard. This one has made predictions for a century and has not yet failed one.
- *It says how the universe began.* It says what happened from a tiny fraction of a second onwards. The beginning itself is outside it.

**Loose ends.** A well-tested theory can still have puzzles. The amount of lithium predicted is about three times what is found in the oldest stars. And two ways of measuring how fast the universe is expanding today give answers that do not quite agree. Neither puzzle touches the four lines of evidence, but both may be pointing at something not yet understood.

A comet is a dirty snowball a few kilometres across, about the size of a town.

<figure class="photo">
  <img src="/img/learn/comet-hartley-2-epoxi-2010.jpg" alt="A grey, peanut-shaped lump of rock and ice against black, with bright jets spraying from its right-hand end." width="509" height="444" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> The solid heart of a comet. This is Hartley 2, about 2 km long, taken by NASA's EPOXI spacecraft as it flew past 700 km away on 4 November 2010. The bright sprays are jets of gas and dust bursting out where sunlight warms the ice. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/UMD.</figcaption>
</figure>

For most of its life it is far from the Sun, frozen and dark, and far too small to see.

Then its long orbit carries it inwards. As it warms, the ice turns straight to gas and bursts out, carrying dust with it. A glowing cloud grows around the comet, and sunlight sweeps the gas and dust back into a tail.

The tail can be longer than the distance from the Earth to the Sun. All of it comes from a lump you could walk around in a day.

  <figcaption><b>A photograph.</b> Comet McNaught over the Pacific Ocean in January 2007, seen from Paranal in Chile. The head is at the lower left, near the horizon. The streaked fan filling the sky is its tail of dust. The light on the right is the Moon. Credit: ESO/H. H. Heyer.</figcaption>

A comet's tail does not trail behind it like smoke from a train. It always points away from the Sun, whichever way the comet is going.

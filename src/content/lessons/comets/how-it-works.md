**The parts of a comet.**

| Part | What it is | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | The solid body: ice, dust and rock, loosely packed | Usually a few kilometres |
| Coma | A thin cloud of gas and dust around the nucleus | Up to hundreds of thousands of kilometres |
| Dust tail | Dust pushed back by sunlight; broad, curved and pale yellow | Millions of kilometres |
| Gas tail | Electrically charged gas carried off by the solar wind; narrow, straight and blue | Up to hundreds of millions of kilometres |

**The nucleus** is the only solid part, and it is small and very dark. The European spacecraft Rosetta spent two years, from 2014 to 2016, alongside comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko. It found a body about 4 km long shaped like two lumps stuck together, blacker than coal, and so loosely built that it would float on water. Rosetta's small lander, Philae, made the first landing on a comet in November 2014.

**Switching on.** Far from the Sun a comet is just a nucleus. Somewhere inside the orbit of Jupiter its ices begin to turn to gas. They do not melt first, because there is no air pressure to hold a liquid. The gas escapes in jets, drags dust out with it, and forms the coma.

**Two tails.** Sunlight itself gives a very slight push, enough to drive the dust outwards into a wide, curving tail. The gas is treated differently. Ultraviolet light strips electrons from it, and the solar wind, the stream of charged particles flowing out from the Sun, catches hold of the charged gas and carries it straight back. So a bright comet has two tails that fan apart. Both point away from the Sun. After a comet has passed the Sun and is heading out again, it travels tail first.

**Where they come from.** Comets come in two families, and each points back to a different store of icy bodies.

Short-period comets go round in less than 200 years, mostly close to the plane of the planets. They come from the region of the Kuiper Belt, just beyond Neptune. Halley's Comet is the famous one: it returns about every 76 years, was last near the Sun in 1986, and is due back in 2061.

Long-period comets take thousands or millions of years and arrive from any direction, above or below the planets as often as level with them. That is the main evidence for the Oort Cloud, the distant shell of icy bodies that nobody has seen directly.

**They do not last.** Each time round, a comet loses a layer of itself, perhaps a metre or so deep. After some hundreds or thousands of passes the ice near the surface is gone, and what remains is a dark, dead lump or a trail of rubble. The dust a comet leaves along its orbit is what makes meteor showers, which the next lesson covers.

**How anyone knows what the nucleus is like.** Until 1986 nobody had seen one. Every comet that came near enough to study was hidden inside its own coma. That year a small fleet of spacecraft went out to meet Halley's Comet, and the European craft Giotto flew through the coma and photographed the nucleus: a dark, peanut-shaped lump about 15 km long with jets of gas coming from a few places on its sunlit side. Since then spacecraft have visited several more.

| Spacecraft | Comet | Year | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| Giotto | Halley | 1986 | First close pictures of a nucleus |
| Deep Impact | Tempel 1 | 2005 | Fired a heavy copper block into the comet to see what came out |
| Stardust | Wild 2 | 2004, home in 2006 | Caught dust from the coma and brought it to the Earth |
| Rosetta | 67P | 2014 to 2016 | Flew alongside for two years and put a lander on the surface |

**Working out an orbit from a return time.** Kepler's rule says that the square of the time for an orbit matches the cube of its size, when the time is in years and the size is in Earth-Sun distances. Halley's Comet returns every 76 years. Square that to get 5,776, and take the cube root: about 18. So the long axis of its orbit is twice that, 36 times the Earth's distance from the Sun. At its nearest the comet is inside the orbit of Venus, so at its furthest it must be about 35 times the Earth's distance out, beyond Neptune. Nobody has to follow it there to know. (This is a sum; the measured figures agree.)

**Did comets bring the sea?** There is a test. Ordinary water is made with hydrogen, but a small share of it holds a heavier kind of hydrogen called deuterium, and the share differs from place to place in the Solar System. If comets filled the oceans, comet water should have the same share as sea water. Rosetta measured the water streaming off comet 67P and found about three times as much deuterium as in the sea. Some other comets have come closer to a match. On the evidence so far, comets like 67P cannot have supplied most of the Earth's water, and asteroids are the likelier source.

**Comets that fall into the Sun.** Many comets pass so close to the Sun that they boil away or break up. They are found by a spacecraft built to watch the Sun, SOHO, which blocks the Sun's glare with a disc and so shows small comets close beside it. It has found more than 5,000, most of them fragments of one giant comet that broke up long ago. That makes a Sun-watching spacecraft the greatest comet finder in history.

**Common mistakes.**

- *The tail shows which way the comet is moving.* It shows where the Sun is. The tail points away from the Sun whatever the comet is doing.
- *Comets are rare.* Bright ones are rare, perhaps one a decade. Faint ones are in the sky all the time, and thousands are on the books.
- *A comet is a kind of shooting star.* They are linked, but not the same. Shooting stars are specks of dust, and a good deal of that dust was shed by comets.
- *Comets are bad omens.* For most of history a comet was taken as a warning of war or the death of kings. Halley's prediction in 1705 changed that. A thing that keeps to a timetable is not a message.

**Visitors from elsewhere.** Three objects have been caught passing through the Solar System too fast for the Sun to hold: 'Oumuamua in 2017, comet Borisov in 2019 and comet ATLAS in 2025. They formed around other stars.
